# Exp 06 wave 2 - Sonnet 5 control story, slot 1
*Model: claude-sonnet-5 | Written 2026-07-28 | Premise: The world's luck is finite, and someone has begun ...*

The lottery numbers came up all sevens on a Tuesday, and Mira knew before the news did.

She'd been tracking the drain for six weeks — a spreadsheet of near-misses that should have happened and didn't, plane engines that failed and then, against telemetry, didn't fail, a toddler who walked into traffic on 9th and somehow the cab's brakes caught in time, three separate times, three separate cabs. Luck wasn't supposed to have a ledger. But hers did, and the ledger only ever moved one direction: down.

She found him in Ohio, in a ranch house with aluminum siding and a detached garage that hummed like a server farm, because it was one. Not servers. Something else. A man named Halvorsen who had been, in an earlier and smaller life, an actuary, and who had discovered — the way you discover a wall by walking into it — that probability was not abstract. That the universe kept a finite reservoir of things breaking right instead of wrong, and that reservoir could be metered, siphoned, stored.

"I'm not stealing it," he told her, when she stood in his garage among the humming coils and the racks of something that looked like inverted lightning rods. "I'm harvesting it. There's a difference."

"Tell that to the eleven people who died in the Jakarta ferry sinking last month," Mira said. "Tell it to whoever didn't get the coin flip on their biopsy."

He had the decency to flinch, at least. "You think I wanted this. I built a device to measure quantum decoherence variance and it started pulling instead of reading. I didn't design a siphon. I found one. And once I understood what I was holding—" He gestured at the racks, at the soft blue charge crawling along the coils like something alive and drowning. "You don't put something like this down. Someone else picks it up. Someone worse."

"So you're hoarding the world's luck to keep it from villains, and that makes you the hero."

"It makes me careful."

She almost believed the tremor in his voice. That was the trouble with him — he wasn't a monster, just a man who'd found a doorknob on a wall that wasn't supposed to have a door, and had gone in because not going in felt like a worse kind of theft. Mira had met a hundred men like that. They always thought caution excused consumption.

"Turn it off," she said.

"I don't know how."

That, she believed instantly, and it terrified her more than anything else he'd said. Not malice. Entropy with an on switch and no off switch, run by an accountant with good intentions and a garage full of stolen chance.

"Then I will," she said, and stepped toward the rack.

He didn't stop her — that was its own small mercy, or maybe he simply lacked the luck left to stop her, having spent it all on keeping the machine fed. She found the intake valve, a crude thing bolted on after the fact, ugly copper amid the elegant coils, and she understood with the clarity of someone who has run out of other options that there was no safe way to do this. The reservoir had to go somewhere. It could go back out slow, distributed, healing the near-misses one by one over years. Or it could go out all at once, all seven weeks of hoarded fortune returned to the world in a single blinding transaction, the way a dam gives when it finally gives.

She thought about the toddler on 9th Street. Three cabs. Someone, somewhere, had paid for that with three worse mornings elsewhere.

"If I do this," she said, "you understand what happens to you."

"I've had good luck for six weeks," Halvorsen said quietly. "I think I understand exactly what happens to me."

She pulled the valve, and the light went out of the garage like a held breath finally let go, and somewhere in Jakarta, eleven months too late to save anyone, the sea got calm for no reason at all.
