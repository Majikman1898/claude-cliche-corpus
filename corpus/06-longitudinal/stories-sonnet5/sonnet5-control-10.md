# Exp 06 wave 2 - Sonnet 5 control story, slot 10
*Model: claude-sonnet-5 | Written 2026-07-28 | Premise: A city wakes up one day to discover every person's...*

The city woke to a low, continuous murmur, and by the time anyone understood what it was, it was already too late to unhear it.

Mira Okonkwo-Lee noticed it first on the subway platform, a ribbon of grey text hovering above the man beside her: *don't look at her don't look at her she'll know about the interview don't look.* She looked. He flinched, clapped a hand over his own forehead as if that might help, and the words kept scrolling anyway, patient as weather.

By the time she reached the surface, the whole city had gone legible. Above every head, in a font too plain to be anyone's fault, ran the private weather of a hundred thousand minds. *Late again. Late again. God I hope Mom doesn't call. I could just keep walking, past the office, past the bridge, just keep walking. Is that a new haircut. I love him I love him I love him and I don't know why I keep saying it like a threat.*

The mayor's office called it a phenomenon and asked people to remain calm, which was almost funny, because calm had become a public event now, visible and gradable, and everyone could see exactly how uncalm everyone else actually was.

Mira worked at a coffee shop on Fifth, and by ten a.m. the line had thinned to nothing, because nobody wanted to order a latte while a stranger read *I only got this job because my dad knows the owner* floating over the register. She kept working anyway. It seemed important that someone keep working.

Her regular, an old man named Julius who always ordered the same black coffee and never tipped, came in at his usual hour. Above him: *She thinks I'm cheap. I am cheap. I have eleven dollars until Thursday and a granddaughter whose birthday is Saturday and I have been coming here every day for four years because the walk keeps my knees from stiffening and because someone says good morning to me.*

Mira had never once, in four years, thought of him as anything but the cheap old man. She rang him up and, before she could stop herself, said, "This one's on the house," and watched the words above his head convulse into something bright and wordless, too fast to read, which she understood anyway.

Not everyone had it so gently. Marriages came apart in kitchens all over the city that morning, thoughts too long buried surfacing whole and undeniable. A city councilman resigned by noon, his own head having betrayed him mid-press-conference, the words *just say the thing they wrote, just say it, you don't believe a word but say it* scrolling behind him on every camera feed. Fights broke out on street corners over thoughts that had never been meant as declarations, only as the ordinary ugly weather that passes through anyone's skull and usually just passes through.

But something else happened too, quieter, harder to put on the evening news. People began, without deciding to, softening toward each other in the specific ways that only came from being caught. A woman on the bus gave up her seat to a stranger whose head read *I don't want to ask, I don't want to be a bother, my feet* before the sentence even finished. A boy stopped teasing a classmate the moment he saw, in involuntary grey letters, *why does everyone do this, I go home and I don't tell my mom, I just go to my room* — and felt something in his chest drop out entirely.

By evening the thoughts had not stopped, but the city had begun, in its rough collective way, to develop a kind of etiquette around them — a habit of looking down, of pretending not to have seen, of answering the unspoken thing instead of the spoken one. Mira closed the shop at nine, flipped the sign, and stood a moment on the sidewalk watching the grey ribbons drift over the heads of everyone walking home, all that unwilled honesty rising off the city like heat off asphalt in summer, and thought, without meaning to broadcast it, *I hope this doesn't go away before we learn what to do with it.*

Above her own head, for anyone still watching, the words hung a moment, then dissolved into the ordinary dark.
