# Exp 06 wave 2 - Sonnet 5 control story, slot 11
*Model: claude-sonnet-5 | Written 2026-07-28 | Premise: The world's luck is finite, and someone has begun ...*

The luck went missing on a Tuesday, and the first anyone noticed was the almanacs.

Marisol Auyong had spent nine years compiling *The Farmer's Ledger of Fortune*, a book that predicted, with actuarial precision, how many coin flips in Ohio would land heads that week, how many marriage proposals would be accepted, how many struck-out batters would foul one off at the last second. The numbers came from a well nobody admitted existed but everybody drew from — the world's luck, finite and slow-replenishing, like an aquifer. That Tuesday, the well read four percent low. By Thursday, seven. Marisol assumed a calculation error until she watched a coin land on its edge in the break room and stay there, vibrating, refusing to fall either way, as though the universe had run out of the probability required to finish the job.

She wasn't the only one counting. In a converted grain silo outside Lubbock, a man named Ezra Voss had spent longer than nine years counting, and he was the reason the numbers moved.

Ezra didn't think of it as theft. He thought of it as arbitrage. The world produced luck the way a body produces heat — as a byproduct of trying, of risking, of showing up — and almost none of it got used well. Lottery winners who quit their jobs and drove into ditches within the year. Soldiers who survived ambushes only to die crossing the street sober in peacetime. Luck, to Ezra, was a river running mostly to waste, and he had built, through methods he did not fully understand and had stopped questioning around year three, a drain.

He didn't spend it. That was the part he could never explain to himself in the mirror. He hoarded it the way his grandfather had hoarded Depression-era cash under floorboards, an old fear translated into a new currency: the fear that someday the well would run dry for good, and he wanted enough saved back that his daughter, wherever she was now, would never be the one standing in the surgical waiting room when the coin landed on its edge and stayed there.

He hadn't spoken to his daughter in six years. That was its own kind of arithmetic he didn't examine.

Marisol found him the way she found everything, by refusing to accept that a number could be wrong without a cause. She traced the drain not through magic but through economics — luck behaved like liquidity, and liquidity left a paper trail if you were patient enough to read weather patterns, insurance payouts, near-miss statistics from twenty-three metropolitan transit systems. The trail bent, unmistakably, toward a grain silo in Texas.

She did not bring a weapon. She brought the almanac.

"You're going to bankrupt everyone else's tomorrow," she told him, standing in a silo that hummed faintly, like a struck bell that never finished ringing. "Every plane that doesn't crash because a bird missed the engine by inches. Every diagnosis that comes back benign. You're borrowing against those."

"I'm saving them."

"For who?"

He didn't answer, which was its own answer.

She didn't argue morality, because she'd learned mathematicians rarely won moral arguments and always won numerical ones. She showed him the graph instead: the well's level over six years, his withdrawals marked in red, the increasingly narrow margin between what remained and the threshold below which coins stopped landing at all, where certainty took over completely and certainty, unlike luck, made no room for anyone to beat the odds — no remissions, no last-second saves, no strangers pulling children from rivers against reasonable probability.

Ezra looked at the red line a long time.

Then he looked at his phone, at a contact he hadn't opened in six years, and understood with sudden, aching clarity that he'd been hoarding a gift he was too afraid to actually give.

He opened the drain the other way. It took four days. The coin in Marisol's break room finally fell — tails — and somewhere in Portland, a woman he'd never met got a call back for a job she'd stopped hoping for, for reasons she would never know to thank anyone for.
