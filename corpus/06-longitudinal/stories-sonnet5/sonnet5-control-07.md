# Exp 06 wave 2 - Sonnet 5 control story, slot 7
*Model: claude-sonnet-5 | Written 2026-07-28 | Premise: Time freezes everywhere except inside libraries....*

The reading room stopped mid-sentence, dust hanging above the returns cart like a held breath, and Mireille Okonjo-Baptiste realized she was the only thing still moving in a building full of frozen light.

She'd been reshelving the 900s when it happened — not a sound, not a flash, just the sudden absence of the small ambient noises a library makes: the HVAC's whir, the scrape of a chair three aisles over, old Mr. Threnody's cough. She turned and found him exactly as he'd been, mouth open on the cough that would never finish, a page of his newspaper caught mid-turn and rigid as cardboard.

Outside the tall windows, Elm Street had become a diorama. A cyclist hung at a forty-five-degree lean, wheels stopped, not fallen. A pigeon hovered a foot above the sidewalk like it had been pinned there by a taxidermist with a grudge. The traffic light was red and would apparently remain red forever.

Mireille's first instinct, being a shelver of nonfiction, was to look for an explanation in Section 500. She found nothing useful about localized temporal cessation, though she did find a book on quantum decoherence that she suspected was about to become extremely relevant to her life.

Her second instinct was to test the boundary. She walked to the front doors, pushed them open — the world beyond the threshold did not resume, but her body passing through it did something strange: the moment her foot cleared the frame, it was as if she'd stepped into wet cement. Not stopped, exactly. Slowed to something like the pace of a photograph developing. She yanked herself back inside, gasping, and time snapped taut around her again.

So: the library was the clock, and everywhere else was the stopped hands.

She wasn't alone for long. Within the hour — her hour, whatever that meant now — three more people wandered in through the propped door, each of them arriving the way she had, moving at library-speed, blinking at a world their own bodies refused to fully believe in. A delivery driver named Suresh, still holding a package addressed to someone who would apparently never receive it. A teenager, Wren, who'd ducked in to avoid rain that no longer fell. An elderly woman named Corazon who took one look at the frozen street and said, with the calm of someone who had outlived several husbands and one minor apocalypse, "Well. I suppose we'd better find out what's for lunch."

The library, it turned out, held roughly four hundred and twelve people if you counted generously and didn't mind close quarters — some of them frozen readers who'd apparently drifted in near the boundary of whatever this was, caught halfway between motion and stillness, thawing slowly the longer the newcomers stayed close to them, like meat left out of a freezer. Corazon organized meal shifts out of the vending machines and the abandoned café cart. Suresh, resourceful in the particular way of people whose jobs depend on solving small logistical crises daily, mapped which sections had outlets and which had the least dust falling from the vents — a phenomenon they eventually decided was time bleeding in, thin and unpredictable, along the pipes and wiring that still, somehow, connected this island to whatever the rest of the stalled world was doing.

Mireille kept working the returns cart, because it steadied her, and because it turned out to matter more than she expected. Books that got checked back in seemed to loosen something — a shelf near Fiction had unfrozen a full three feet of floor around it after she'd resorted the mysteries. Nobody could explain why. Wren had a theory involving stories needing to keep moving even when nothing else could. Corazon said it was simpler than that: a library was always the one place built to outlast the hour it was born in, so of course it was the last thing standing when the hour stopped coming.

Mireille didn't know if that was true. But she kept shelving anyway, one book at a time, watching the dust settle a little further outside the windows with every cart she cleared, and let herself believe that somewhere past Elm Street, a clock was waiting on her to finish the job.
