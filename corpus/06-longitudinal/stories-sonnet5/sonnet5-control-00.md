# Exp 06 wave 2 - Sonnet 5 control story, slot 0
*Model: claude-sonnet-5 | Written 2026-07-28 | Premise: A city wakes up one day to discover every person's...*

The rain hadn't touched the city in eleven days, but that Tuesday something else fell instead: silence broke open, and every skull in Portsmouth stood roofless.

Maren noticed it on the 7:14 bus. The man across the aisle wore a gray suit and a face arranged for a job he hated, and above his head — not literally above, but somehow there, the way a smell is there — hung the thought *if she leaves me I don't know what I'll do*, worn thin as a coin from handling. The teenager by the window was thinking about a boy named Dev in three different tenses at once. The driver's mind ran a metronome of *don't hit the dog don't hit the dog* though there was no dog, had never been a dog, just the fear of one.

By noon the radio called it the Unveiling, because someone always names the thing before anyone understands it. Scientists had nothing. Priests had everything, and none of it useful. The mayor's office issued a statement — *maintain calm, avoid public gatherings* — while the mayor's own private thought, visible to the three aides in the room, ran underneath it like a subtitle: *please God don't let them see the account in Zurich*.

At first it was carnage. Marriages didn't survive the afternoon. A bakery on Fentress Street closed because the woman behind the counter could no longer stand within ten feet of her business partner, whose thoughts about her had been circling the drain of something unspeakable for six years and were now simply there, mid-air, smelling faintly of shame. Fights broke out over thoughts that were never meant to become actions — a flicker of *I could just push him* outside the bar, gone within a second, but a second was long enough to be seen and answered with a fist.

Maren's daughter, eleven years old, stopped talking altogether. She didn't need to. Maren could read *I hate the new baby, I hate that everyone loves the new baby, I hate that I hate it* scrolling above her daughter's head at the dinner table, and instead of the sharp retort she might have given a spoken version of that thought, she found herself only reaching over and putting a hand on the girl's small fist, because the thought was so plainly, achingly a child's thought, the kind adults have too but have learned to disguise even from themselves.

That was the part nobody predicted: how much of what floated above people's heads wasn't cruelty but grief wearing cruelty's coat. The man who'd snapped at his barista wasn't thinking about her at all — his mind was a loop of his brother's hospital bed. The woman who cut every line in the pharmacy was replaying, on an endless track, the words her mother said to her thirty years ago and never took back. People had built whole cathedrals of performance — politeness, competence, cheer — over rooms that, once you could see into them, were just full of the same three or four scared animals pacing.

By the second week something shifted, quiet as the first change had been sudden. People started reading each other before speaking, and it made them gentler, not because the truth was flattering but because it was exhausting to hate someone whose fear you could see so plainly. The bus driver's *don't hit the dog* became almost sweet once you understood it was really *don't let today be the day I fail at the one thing I'm good at*. Strangers began nodding at each other on the street the way people nod at animals they've decided not to be afraid of.

Maren still didn't know if it was a mercy or a wound. She only knew that on the fourteenth night, lying beside her husband, she could see quite clearly, hovering just above his sleeping face, the thought *I hope she still loves who I actually am* — and for the first time in years, she reached over in the dark, and let him feel her hand answer it, since apparently now, that was the only kind of privacy left.
