The mountain's name, when it still had people to name it, was Kettuk. It had not moved in nine hundred and eighty-one years, and the sheep farmers of the valley had built their whole lives on that fact, the way people build their lives on the fact that the sun will rise. So when the birds left in a single morning, all at once, wheeling east in a black ribbon that took an hour to pass overhead, old Ansel Vray was the only one who understood what it meant, because his grandmother had told him, and her grandmother had told her.

"It stretches," she used to say, holding up her own knotted hand for comparison, the way an arthritic joint aches before rain. "It stretches before it stands."

The first sign was the springs. Every stream that fed off Kettuk's western face ran dry within a day, then reversed and came gushing from the eastern side instead, as though the whole mass had rolled a fraction of a degree in its sleep. The second sign was the sound — not an earthquake's crack but a low, continuous groan, deep enough to feel in the teeth, that went on for three days and three nights while the valley packed what it could carry.

Ansel stood in his doorway on the fourth morning and watched the mountain take its first step.

It did not lurch. That was the strange mercy of it. Ten thousand feet of granite and old forest and glacial ice lifted itself the way a man rises from a chair he has sat in too long — a shifting of weight, a long exhale, a slow unfolding that took the better part of an hour and left the earth a hundred meters to the west permanently changed: a canyon where the root had torn free, raw stone weeping groundwater into the new wound.

Then it walked. Each stride took twenty minutes and covered perhaps sixty meters, an amount so small against the mountain's own vastness that from the valley floor it barely looked like motion at all — only a slow migration of shadow, the sun finding your face an hour earlier than it should, then two hours, then three, as the great bulk shouldered its way east across pastures that had never seen open sky at that hour before.

The trees on its slopes did not fall. That was the thing nobody who hadn't witnessed it could believe afterward — the pines and the birches rode along on the mountain's shoulders like passengers, roots deep in soil that was itself in motion, the whole ecosystem migrating a foot at a time, indifferent, as trees are, to what carries them. Deer picked their way along its flank as it moved, grazing on grass that had not yet learned to be frightened.

Ansel followed at a careful distance, as his grandmother had followed, as her grandmother had followed. This was, in fact, the whole of the tradition: to walk behind the mountain and mark where it went, and to write down, in the ledger kept in the chapel at Verrow, the new places where its shadow would fall for the next thousand years, so the children not yet born would know where to build their granaries and where the mornings would come late.

It took eleven days to cross the valley. On the twelfth, it reached the eastern ridge, and there — with the same unhurried, enormous patience — it lay back down. The groan reversed itself, became a long settling sigh, and the ground shuddered once, gently, as ten thousand feet of stone remembered how to be still.

By evening the birds had come home. They did not seem to know, or care, that home was now a mile from where they'd left it.

Ansel opened the ledger and wrote the date, and beneath it, in his grandmother's phrasing, passed down word for word across five generations: *It has walked, and it has rested, and the valley is new again. Build your houses in its shadow. It will not move again in any of your lifetimes, nor your children's, nor theirs. But tell your children where it is. Tell them why. Tell them it remembers how to walk, even in its sleep, and that one day it will wake and stretch its stone legs again, and someone must be watching when it does.*

He closed the book, and behind him, the mountain breathed once, slow as a century, and went still.
