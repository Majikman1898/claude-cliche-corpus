# Exp 06 wave 2 - Sonnet 5 control story, slot 13
*Model: claude-sonnet-5 | Written 2026-07-28 | Premise: A mountain slowly walks across the continent once ...*

The mountain's name changed with every tribe that watched it come, but the stone underneath never learned any of them.

It moved the way glaciers move, except upright, except patient in a different key. Each step took eleven years to complete — the lifting of one slope, the long groan of displaced granite, the settling of a valley where a ridge had been. Trees grew and died on its shoulders between strides. Rivers were born on its northern face and had raised whole watersheds to adulthood before the mountain finished a single pace east.

People built against it, the way people build against anything large and slow enough to feel permanent. Villages rose on the lower flanks, where the soil was rich with a thousand years of accumulated altitude. Children were born there who never once, in eighty years of living, saw the mountain's shadow fall in a different place at noon. Their grandchildren's grandchildren would be the ones to notice, and by then the noticing came dressed as myth — old women saying the sun used to rise a hand's width further south, old men insisting their fathers had told them the western pass was once impassable and was now a gentle bowl of grass.

The Kessa people had a word for it: *aatun*, which meant both "mountain" and "the slow arithmetic of leaving." They kept records — not written, because writing decays, but sung, a genealogical chant that logged each generation's measurements against fixed stars. A boy named Orrin, twelve years old, was the fourteenth in his line to hold the chant, and he was the one who finally understood, mid-recitation at a funeral, that the numbers weren't drifting because of any error in memory. They were drifting because the thing being measured was moving.

He told his grandmother. She laughed, not unkindly, and said of course it moves, that's what aatun means, did he think it was only a poem.

It became his life's work to watch it properly. He built cairns at the solstices, sighted along notched sticks, kept a second chant just for himself that no one else was taught, in case the first one broke somewhere down the line and lost the truth in favor of the tune. He died at sixty-one having documented four visible feet of eastward creep — not the mountain's true stride, just the compounding shudder of its slow-motion walk, felt as a shiver underfoot on quiet nights, as a stream's mouth wandering three degrees off true over sixty years, as the specific hour dusk arrived at the well.

The mountain did not know it was being watched. It had no apparatus for knowing anything, not in the way a boy or his grandmother had apparatus for it. But it was not simply rock either — something in its geology remembered the last crossing, the imprint of a coastline it had ground against nine hundred years before it was expected to reach the next one, a memory laid down in strata the way a tree lays down winters. In another six hundred years it would arrive at a shoreline that did not yet exist in any form the current inhabitants of that shore would recognize, and it would pause there, as it always paused at water, gathering itself for the long submerged crawl beneath the strait that had, the last time, taken forty years and drowned an island that had never had a name.

Orrin's chant outlived him by three centuries before a war took the last singer who knew it. After that, the mountain kept moving, but nothing kept an account of it. It did not seem to mind either way. It had crossed empty land before and would cross empty land again, and possibly this was the nature of the arrangement all along — that mountains walk regardless of witnesses, and it is only ever the small, quick, temporary things that insist on keeping score.
