The tide had been going out for three years before anyone admitted it wasn't coming back.

At first the harbormasters blamed sediment, then currents, then the moon itself, as if Luna had simply grown bored and drifted off her mark. But the water kept receding, an inch a day, then a foot, exposing first the barnacled skirts of the piers, then the rusted hulls that had gone down in living memory, then things that had gone down in no memory at all.

Mara found the city on the ninetieth day, walking the new mudflats behind her father's boat with a headlamp because the moon offered no help anymore — it hung fat and wrong-colored over a horizon that kept moving away from her. The spires came up first, black and ribbed like the ribs of something that had swallowed a cathedral. They did not look drowned. Drowned things softened, grew fur of algae, wore their ruin openly. These towers were sharp. They looked built yesterday, in a style no century owned.

She should have turned back. She told herself that later, many times, the way you tell yourself things after they've already stopped mattering.

Instead she walked the causeway of pale stone that had unspooled itself from the tower's base, and the mud did not suck at her boots the way mud should. It held her print and released her foot too easily, like the ground had decided courtesy was owed. Windows without glass breathed a smell of hot copper and crushed violets. In one of them something coiled — not a shape she could hold in her eye, more a suggestion of coiling, the way a word on the tip of your tongue is a suggestion of a word — and then it was gone, and the window was just a window, and she was aware of her own pulse in a way she hadn't been a minute before.

Deeper in, the ecosystems started. That was the word the biologists used later, on the news broadcasts that grew shorter and stranger each week: ecosystems. As if a garden had simply been asked to wait its turn. Gardens of stalked eyes on stems of white cartilage, turning to track her not with malice but with the mild curiosity of sunflowers. Reefs that pulsed light in patterns almost like speech, almost like a greeting, almost like a warning, and she could never decide which and that not-deciding felt like the actual danger, the thing that would keep her coming back.

Because she did come back. Every low-tide morning for a month, farther each time, past the outer towers into a plaza where the paving stones were inlaid with symbols that made her eyes water if she looked too long, that made her hands, unbidden, want to trace them. She met a man there on the eleventh trip, an old fisherman named Costa, and neither of them asked why the other kept returning. They only nodded and walked their separate directions into the ruin, the way people once nodded to each other on the way into church.

The scientists came with their instruments and their funding and their brave first-week language, and one by one they stopped publishing. The instruments recorded numbers that made no sense and then, after enough exposure, recorded nothing at all, as if the numbers themselves had decided to withdraw like the sea. A few researchers didn't come back up the causeway. Nobody organized a search. It seemed, somehow, like an impolite thing to insist on.

Mara stopped telling her father where she went. She stood most evenings now at the edge of the plaza, watching the spires drink the last of the light, and she understood — not with fear, though fear was in there somewhere, folded small — that the ocean hadn't been hiding these things from the world.

It had been hiding the world from them, patiently, for longer than the word "ocean" had existed to describe what did the hiding. And now, for reasons that belonged to no science she'd ever be taught, it had simply gotten tired of the job.

She went in anyway. The city, in its slow strange way, had started to feel like an answer she couldn't quite hear yet. She wanted to get close enough to hear it.
