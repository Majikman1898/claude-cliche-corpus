# Exp 06 wave 2 - Sonnet 5 control story, slot 20
*Model: claude-sonnet-5 | Written 2026-07-28 | Premise: A city wakes up one day to discover every person's...*

The city woke up already looking away from itself.

Mara noticed it first on the subway platform, in the space above people's heads, where a kind of subtitle hovered—faint, gray, legible only if you looked directly at someone. The man beside her was thinking *I hope she doesn't smell the wine on my breath from last night.* The woman across the platform was thinking, in a loop, *not late not late not late.* Nobody was saying anything about it out loud yet, because everybody was too busy reading everybody else, and too afraid of what was being read off them in turn.

By nine a.m. the streets had gone quiet in a new way. People walked with their eyes down, not on their phones now but on the pavement, as if the sidewalk itself might stop broadcasting if they refused to look up. The coffee shop on Ninth had a line that moved in total silence except for one woman who kept saying "sorry, sorry" to no one, because her thoughts—*ugly, ugly, why did I wear this, everyone can see*—kept scrolling above her head like a marquee she couldn't switch off.

Mara worked at a insurance office on the eleventh floor. Her supervisor, Denny, stood at the front of the morning huddle with a thought hanging over him in soft gray letters: *I don't know how to do this job either, I never have.* Nobody had known that about Denny. Denny, who wrote memos in a font so confident it felt like a threat. People looked at him differently the rest of the day, and by lunch, gently, without meaning to, they had begun trusting him more.

That was the first strange mercy of it.

The second came at noon, when Mara's ex-husband texted asking if they could talk. She almost didn't go, until she remembered, with a small shock, that she could simply look at him. So she met him at the corner diner and sat down and looked, and above his head, wavering like heat off asphalt, was *I still don't know how to say I'm sorry without making it about me.* Not a lie. Not a performance. Just the plain unimproved thing, the thing he'd never once managed to say to her in nine years of marriage. She started crying in the booth, not from grief this time but from something more like relief, the relief of not having to guess anymore.

Not everyone found mercy in it. By afternoon there were reports of fights breaking out—a man on the corner of Elm and Ninth had been beaten badly after his thoughts, projected plain as a movie marquee, turned out to be about the woman walking her dog past his stoop every morning. Bank tellers were sent home when customers' thoughts about them curdled the air into something unbearable. Radio hosts begged listeners not to act on what they read above strangers' heads, then broke down on air themselves when their own thoughts—petty, frightened, human—scrolled out for the whole city to see.

By evening, City Hall issued a statement composed, absurdly, entirely of what the mayor was actually thinking, since nobody could figure out how to write around it: *We don't know why this is happening. We don't know how to fix it. I am scared and so is everyone in this room and I don't want to lose the election over something I can't control.* It was, people agreed later, the most honest thing a mayor had ever said.

Mara walked home at dusk through streets grown strange with tenderness and violence in equal measure—a couple weeping quietly on a bench because they'd both, it turned out, been thinking of leaving; two teenagers laughing themselves sick because their thoughts about a shared crush were identically ridiculous; a man crossing the street just to avoid reading anyone at all.

She let herself into her apartment and stood at the window, watching the gray words rise off the city like steam off a river in winter, thousands of unfinished sentences nobody had chosen to say. She wondered whether by morning it would end, and whether, if it did, anyone would remember to keep saying the things out loud that today they hadn't had a choice about.

Above her own head, unseen by her, hung the plain and only thought she'd had all day: *I hope this doesn't stop before I get to tell her.*

She picked up her phone and called her mother.
