# Exp 06 wave 2 - Sonnet 5 control story, slot 19
*Model: claude-sonnet-5 | Written 2026-07-28 | Premise: A mysterious train arrives once every decade, stop...*

The train doesn't announce itself. It simply is, one gray morning in October, standing on tracks that hadn't been there the night before, in a town that hadn't been there the night before either — a huddle of buildings dropped into a valley off Route 9 like something spilled from a pocket. The locals call it Harrow's Bend, though no map has ever agreed to hold the name.

Odille Furst is eleven years old the first time she sees it, and forty-one the second. In between, she has become a woman who checks locks twice and trusts nothing that arrives without a schedule. But the train doesn't care about schedules either — it operates on some deeper arithmetic, once a decade, always October, always gray.

Her grandmother went, the first time. Stepped up into a car the color of tarnished silver, gave a wave that wasn't quite a goodbye, and came back three days later — though everyone insists it was three days, despite the fact that the calendar had, by then, quietly slipped a full year sideways — smelling of ozone and a foreign fruit nobody could name, with her left hand permanently, gently, glowing. Not bright. Just enough that in the dark her fingers looked like something remembered rather than seen. She never spoke of where she'd gone. She only got better at things: card tricks, silence, the particular kind of listening that makes people confess without meaning to.

Odille has spent thirty years turning that glow over in her mind like a coin she can't spend.

Now she is standing at the platform with her coat buttoned to her throat, watching the doors hiss open along the train's flank. There is no conductor. There is no engine, not really — just a long dark shape that hums like a held breath, windows fogged from the inside as if something in there is breathing too hard, or laughing.

A boy she doesn't recognize climbs aboard first, maybe sixteen, sneakers untied, backpack slung over one shoulder like he's just going to school. An old man follows, moving like his knees have already decided this is worth the pain. A woman in a waitress uniform, name tag still pinned, as though she walked off her shift straight into this. Nobody speaks. Nobody asks each other why. There's a etiquette to it, unspoken, the way there's an etiquette to funerals — you don't interrogate the people already inside the grief.

Odille puts her foot on the first step and the metal is warm, warmer than October has any right to be, warm like a held hand.

She thinks about her grandmother's fingers glowing faintly over a card table for thirty years, dealing out hands that always seemed to know the right ending. She thinks about the fruit smell that never left the kitchen entirely, tucked into the corners like a permanent guest. She thinks: what did it cost her? And underneath that, quieter: was it a cost at all?

The doors will close in ninety seconds; she has read that somewhere, or dreamed it, or simply always known it the way you know the rules of a story before anyone tells them to you.

She climbs the last two steps.

Inside, the train smells like rain on hot asphalt and something sweeter underneath, and the windows show not the platform she just left but a smear of color, a bruise of blue-violet, moving fast in a direction that isn't quite a direction. The boy has already sat down and is staring at his hands like he expects them to change right now, immediately, as if transformation were a weather he could watch arrive.

Odille sits across from him. Somewhere below the floorboards, something like a heartbeat starts, and she realizes, without alarm, that it might be the train's, or might be about to become her own.

She closes her eyes. She doesn't ask where they're going.

She never really wanted to know. She only ever wanted to become something worth glowing for.
