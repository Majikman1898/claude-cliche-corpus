# Exp 06 wave 2 - Sonnet 5 control story, slot 14
*Model: claude-sonnet-5 | Written 2026-07-28 | Premise: Memories can be planted like gardens....*

The garden was on the third floor of a building that officially didn't exist, in a room with skylights instead of windows, so the light that fell on the beds was always the light of the day being planted rather than the day outside. Mireille had worked there for eleven years and still felt a small vertigo every time she pressed a memory-seed into the soil and watched the first tendril curl up, translucent as glass, carrying inside it someone else's Christmas morning or first kiss or the smell of a mother's kitchen.

Her current client was a man named Auguste Faure, eighty-three, who had commissioned a memory of a daughter he'd never had. Not a false memory of an actual person — the Guild was strict about that, it warped the mind's architecture to plant a person who should exist but didn't leave a ragged hole where they used to be inserted. Instead they grew composites: a girl assembled from a thousand real daughters' real days, harvested legally, anonymized, blended until she belonged to no one and therefore could belong, gently, to him.

Mireille tended the bed for six weeks. She fed it fragments — a child's laugh recorded in a hundred different registers and averaged into one sound; the weight of a small hand pressed into an adult palm; a birthday cake with candles counted up through the years, four, then seven, then twelve. She pruned out anything too specific, anything that might snag on the wrong emotional root and cause confusion later. Memory gardening was mostly subtraction. You didn't build a childhood. You suggested one, and let the mind's own soil do the rest, the way real memory always half-invents itself anyway.

The morning it bloomed, she almost didn't recognize what she was looking at. The plant had gone strange in the night — not wilted, but doubled, throwing two stalks from one root where there should have been one. She called her supervisor, a tired woman named Odile who had seen everything twice.

"Cross-pollination," Odile said, barely glancing up from her tablet. "Happens sometimes when two beds share irrigation lines. Whose is the other one?"

Mireille checked the register. Bed 14, two rows over — a woman named Sabine Delacroix, who had commissioned a memory of a father who forgave her for something she wouldn't specify.

"They're intertwined at the root," Odile said. "You'll have to separate them by hand or graft them as a matched pair. Sometimes it's actually better. Two strangers, linked in memory, never meeting — makes both gardens more stable somehow. Loneliness roots poorly. Companionship holds the soil."

So Mireille didn't separate them. She let the two stalks grow together, the imagined daughter and the imagined forgiveness twining at the base like ivy around a single stake, and when both memories were finally harvested and installed — Faure waking with the warm ache of a life he'd never lived, Delacroix waking absolved by a father who'd never spoken those words to her real self — neither of them would ever know that somewhere in a room with skylights, their inventions had grown up together, sharing water, sharing light, before being cut apart and carried off into two separate skulls to be believed.

Mireille swept the cuttings into the compost, where old fictions went to become soil for new ones. Nothing here was wasted. Grief broke down into fertilizer for joy; joy broke down into fertilizer for peace. She had planted four thousand childhoods, nine hundred reconciliations, an uncountable number of last words finally said correctly. She had never once planted anything for herself.

That evening, walking home past actual gardens — the unglamorous kind, tomatoes and marigolds, real dirt under real fingernails — she thought, as she always did, about what she would grow if she let herself. Not a daughter. Not a forgiveness. Something smaller. Her mother's hands, maybe, doing something ordinary. Braiding hair. Peeling an apple in one long spiral.

She had the seeds. She had access to every bed in the building.

She had simply never learned how to ask the soil for something true.
