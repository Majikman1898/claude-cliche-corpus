# Exp 06 wave 2 - Sonnet 5 control story, slot 21
*Model: claude-sonnet-5 | Written 2026-07-28 | Premise: The world's luck is finite, and someone has begun ...*

The luck ledger read zero-point-three-one when Maren checked it that morning, and she almost didn't notice, because the number moved so rarely that most people had stopped looking. It had held steady near one for eleven years — the number the actuaries at the Bureau of Global Fortune had settled on after the Reckoning, when everyone finally agreed the world's luck was a fixed and countable thing, drawn down by every near-miss and every impossible catch, replenished slow as oil.

Point-three-one meant something was wrong. Point-three-one, extrapolated, meant the world ran out of luck in nine days.

She was a fortune auditor, third tier, which meant she watched dials and flagged anomalies for people more important than her, and in eleven years she had flagged nothing, because nothing had ever moved. Now she sat in her cubicle in Geneva watching the number tick down in increments too small to see and too large to ignore, point-three-one becoming point-three-zero-nine, and she did the thing she was trained to never do alone. She traced the draw.

Luck theft left a signature, the training manuals said, a directional pull, like water finding a drain. She built the vector by hand, cross-referencing catastrophe-averted logs against unclaimed windfalls, plotting where luck should have landed and hadn't. The line she drew went east, then south, then converged, over eleven hours of work, on a single tower in Manila, floor forty, a private trading desk registered to a man named Costa Ibarra who had not lost a single position in four years.

Not one. Four years. In a market. That wasn't skill. That was withdrawal.

She flew there herself, because the report would take a week to move through channels and channels moved slower than a man could drain a planet dry. She found Ibarra's office at 2 a.m., paid a cleaner to let her in, and found in the back room not a computer but a wheel — an actual wheel, hand-built, brass and bone, wired into a lattice of copper filament that hummed even unplugged, even in the dark, like it was still eating.

He was there. He hadn't gone home in three days, he told her, because the wheel needed watching now, it was accelerating on its own, feeding on its own draw the way a fire feeds on the wind it makes.

"I didn't build this to hurt anyone," he said. "I built it because I was tired of losing. Do you understand what that's like? Being unlucky your whole life, watching everyone else's coin land right?"

"You emptied the tank for everyone else to fill your own cup."

"I only took the overflow."

"There's no overflow left. You saw the number."

He had, and it had frightened him more than he'd expected, enough that he let her look, enough that between them they found the release valve was a single filament, the first one he'd soldered, load-bearing for the whole apparatus. Cut it and the wheel would either stop or unravel entirely, dump everything back at once, which the manuals said could be its own catastrophe — four years of stolen fortune returning to the world in one convulsive instant, luck as flood instead of luck as tide.

"Slow bleed instead," she said. "We meter it back out. It'll take longer than it took you to steal it."

"How much longer."

"Longer than either of us live, probably."

He looked at the wheel like a father looking at a fire he'd started, and nodded, and together they began the unglamorous work of giving it back — not a decision, not a single brave cut, but a dial turned down one careful notch at a time, for the rest of that night and, though neither of them said it aloud, for the rest of their lives, watching a number climb toward one so slowly that no one else would ever know how close it had come to zero, or how ordinary the reason had been.
