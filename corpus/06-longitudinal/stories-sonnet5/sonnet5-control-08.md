# Exp 06 wave 2 - Sonnet 5 control story, slot 8
*Model: claude-sonnet-5 | Written 2026-07-28 | Premise: People can swap memories like possessions...*

The trade window on Fenwick Street opened at nine.

Maren got there early, the way she always did, and stood outside watching frost fog her breath while the woman ahead of her negotiated a childhood over the counter — someone's fifth birthday, cake with too much frosting, a dog that died the following spring. The trader weighed it in her palm like a coin, held a loupe to it, said something about texture. Maren couldn't hear the price. She never could, from outside.

Inside, the shop smelled like ozone and rain. Corwin manned the register the way he'd manned it for eleven years, sleeves rolled to the elbow, silver bands winking on both wrists where the read-heads clipped in.

"You again," he said, not unkindly.

"I want to sell my mother," Maren said.

He didn't blink. People said stranger things across that counter daily — I want to sell my divorce, my father's funeral, the year I spent believing in God. Corwin had a rule about not asking why. It kept the shop civil.

"Which part of her."

"All of it. Every memory I have."

That got a blink. "That's not a memory, love. That's a person."

"It's memories of a person. That's all a person is, once they're gone." Maren set her hands flat on the glass counter, over the trays of loose recollections beneath — somebody's first kiss on a Ferris wheel, somebody's war, tagged and bagged like coins at a numismatist's. "She's been dead three years. I carry her into every room I walk into. I want the room back."

Corwin fitted the read-band to her temple anyway, because refusing was worse for business than complying, and because some tiny mercantile part of him still believed people should get what they paid for, even when what they paid for was forgetting. The machine hummed. On the little screen between them, thumbnails bloomed — her mother's hands over dough, her mother's voice reading a bedtime story with the wrong ending on purpose, her mother in a hospital bed asking whether the garden needed water, as if that were the thing worth worrying about at the end.

"Full extraction leaves scarring," Corwin said. "You'll know something's missing. Won't know what."

"Good."

He didn't buy it that day. He never bought grief-lots outright — nobody wanted them, and inventory sat on his shelves rotting soft at the edges until a rare collector paid pennies for "authentic maternal loss, well-preserved." Instead he offered escrow: he'd hold the memories, sealed, for ninety days. If she came back and wanted them, they'd slot right in. If not, the shop would take title and she'd get her thirty pieces of silver, so to speak, though it was never silver, it was numbness, clean and immediate as anesthesia.

She signed. The band did its slow theft. She walked out onto Fenwick Street feeling lighter and wronger, the way a mouth feels after a pulled tooth — tongue finding the gap over and over, expecting resistance, expecting pain that had already left.

Eighty-nine days later she stood outside the shop again, in the rain this time, watching a teenage boy trade away his stutter, his humiliations, his whole flinching adolescence, for enough money to leave town. The trader weighed it the same way she'd weighed Maren's mother. Everything the same weight, in that light.

Maren went in on the ninetieth morning, before the window even opened, and knocked until Corwin came down in his undershirt, annoyed.

"I want her back," she said. "Even the hospital. Even the garden."

He didn't say I told you so. He just went to the vault, found the sealed lot under her name, and fitted the band once more — this time to give instead of take. It hurt going back in, the way swallowing something too large hurts, and then it didn't, and then her mother's hands were in the dough again, right where she'd left them.
