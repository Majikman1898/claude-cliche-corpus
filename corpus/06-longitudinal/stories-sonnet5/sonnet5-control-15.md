The rain started at three, and by four Marisol was talking to the cat.

Not talking near it, not talking about it the way she talked to her stuffed rabbit when nobody could hear — talking to it, and the cat, a fat grey tom named Soup who had never done anything more communicative than knock things off counters, was talking back.

"You're standing in the wet part," Soup said, from the windowsill, in a voice like a door that needed oil.

Marisol did not scream. She was eight, and eight-year-olds have a higher tolerance for the impossible than their parents give them credit for. She said, "Why haven't you ever said anything before?"

Soup's tail moved once, slow, the way it did when he'd decided a question was beneath him. "Ask something else."

She asked something else. She asked several something-elses over the following weeks, and got the same tail-flick every time, or a yawn, or once — memorably — Soup simply got up and left the room mid-sentence, the sentence being hers, not his.

It wasn't only Soup. It wasn't only cats. Within a month every child under twelve on the block had a confidant with fur or feathers or, in Kayvon's case down the street, a bearded dragon that spoke in a clipped, formal way like a substitute teacher. The dogs were the worst gossips and the best secret-keepers simultaneously, somehow. The crows organized. Nobody under the age of thirteen could get a crow to shut up, and nobody over the age of thirteen could get a crow to say a single word, because the moment a kid turned thirteen — this was the part that made the news, the part that sent linguists and biologists and a great many increasingly frantic parents into the streets with recorders — the animals went silent for that person again. Forever, as far as anyone could tell. A door that opened only from one side and shut itself behind you as you left the room.

The adults tried everything. They tried bribing children to ask specific questions and report back verbatim. They tried hiding microphones in birdcages. They tried, in one infamous case in Ohio, hypnotizing a nine-year-old to relay a conversation with a parakeet under trance, and got nothing but the kid reciting, with total sincerity, that the parakeet thought purple was "a lying color."

The animals answered everything except the one question that mattered. What are you. Why now. Why only us. They'd discuss weather, and grudges, and which neighbor's yard had the good grubs, and — disturbingly, coming from a Labrador — the precise sensory experience of loneliness. But say the word why in a certain shape, aim it at the real target, and every animal in the world, apparently by agreement, went quiet, or changed the subject, or in Soup's case simply and pointedly began grooming.

"You could just tell them," Marisol said to him one evening in October, the two of them alone in her room, rain again, always rain that fall. "The scientists. They'd leave you alone if you just told them once."

Soup considered this with his whole slow cat body. "They wouldn't leave us alone," he said. "They'd never leave any of you alone again, if they had the why. You'd spend the rest of your lives proving you still deserved to hear it."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Soup agreed, and for once there was nothing smug in it, something almost gentle instead. "It's a kindness. You'll understand it when you're thirteen and I go quiet on you, and you'll hate me for about a year, and then you'll understand that too."

She did hate him, when the year came. Almost exactly a year, her birthday in September, and Soup on the sill the next morning looking at her the way he must have looked at her the very first day, before any of it — blank, ordinary, magnificently uninterested — and no matter what she said, no matter how she shaped the words, he only yawned and went back to sleep, keeping, with total fidelity, the last promise he'd ever make her.
