# Exp 06 wave 2 - Sonnet 5 control story, slot 4
*Model: claude-sonnet-5 | Written 2026-07-28 | Premise: Memories can be planted like gardens....*

The garden was three inches of soil in a terracotta pot, and Mira had learned to plant her mother in it.

The technician called it "substrate encoding," which was the sort of phrase people used when they didn't want to say what it actually felt like — soil, warm and dark, and a seed that hummed faintly if you held it to your ear. You pressed the seed into the dirt with your thumb, said the memory out loud once — just once, that was the rule — and then you watered it and waited.

Mira's mother had been dead eleven months. What was left of her fit into six seeds in a velvet pouch: the smell of her cardigan, the specific way she said *Mira-bell* when she was pretending to be annoyed, the sound of her humming while she did dishes, her laugh at the punchline of a joke Mira could no longer remember the setup to, the weight of her hand on Mira's forehead when she had a fever, and one Mira hadn't planted yet, because she was saving it, the way you save the last bite of something you love.

She planted *Mira-bell* on a Tuesday. It grew into a small blue flower with a voice caught somewhere in its stem, and when she brushed the petals it said her name in that exact cadence, that exact exasperated fondness, and she sat on the kitchen floor and cried until the flower closed its petals for the night, the way the marketing materials said it would — memories needed rest too, apparently, just like the people who'd once held them.

The trouble with garden-memories, the trouble nobody put in the pamphlet, was that they didn't grow the way you expected. You planted a laugh and got, alongside it, faint tendrils of what came before the laugh — the smell of a hospital hallway, a joke about the ugly wallpaper, a nurse's clipboard. Grief, it turned out, was not a seed you could isolate. It came up through everything, a weed with very deep roots, twining itself around whatever you tried to grow.

Mira's neighbor, a widower named Walcott who'd had his garden three years now, told her the trick was to let it be a whole garden and not just a shrine. "You can't only plant the good ones," he said, leaning over the fence with dirt under his nails. "Otherwise you're not growing a person. You're growing a highlight reel. And highlight reels don't hold soil — first winter, they blow right out."

So Mira planted the hard ones too. The seed of the last conversation, the one where she'd been short with her mother over something now embarrassingly small — a canceled dinner, a scheduling fight — because some clinic somewhere had extracted it from her along with everything else, offered it to her in the same velvet pouch, no separate bill for the bad ones. She almost didn't plant it. She sat with it in her palm for three days.

When she finally pressed it into the dirt, it grew into something thorny and unlovely, a bramble with small hard berries, and touching it gave her the sting of her own voice saying *fine, whatever, I have to go* and her mother's voice, quieter, saying *okay, sweetheart* in a way that Mira now understood — with the terrible retroactive clarity grief specialized in — had been her mother already forgiving her before Mira even knew there'd be nothing left to forgive.

The garden filled in over the following spring. Flowers and brambles, some memories that opened only at dusk, one that only bloomed in rain, as if her mother had left instructions for exactly which weather suited which version of herself. Mira stopped visiting on schedule and started visiting the way you'd visit a person — sometimes joyful, sometimes reluctant, sometimes just to sit on the bench and not touch anything at all, letting the whole tangled bed exist in her peripheral vision like a person breathing in the next room.

She still had one seed left, unplanted, in the bottom of the pouch. She knew what it was without checking — the last thing, the very end, the hand not on her forehead but held in hers, cooling. She wasn't ready. She might never be ready. But she noticed, turning the pouch over in her hands on the anniversary, that not-ready no longer felt like refusal. It felt like tending. Some things you let grow when the whole garden is strong enough to hold them, roots enough already down in the ground to keep the new grief from washing everything else away.

She put the seed back in the drawer, beside the trowel, and went outside to water what had already come up.
