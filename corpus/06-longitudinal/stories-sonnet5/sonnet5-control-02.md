She wakes to find a smooth grey stone on her pillow, still faintly warm, and understands without checking her phone that it must be Tuesday, because Tuesdays are the days she dreams about the ocean her grandmother described but never showed her.

Mira has kept a shoebox under her bed since she was nine, back when the first object appeared — a single brass key that fit no lock she owned, resting in the crook of her arm like it had always lived there. Her mother called the doctor. The doctor called it sleepwalking, some elaborate somnambulant craft project, though no one could explain the tarnish already worked into the metal, the decades of grime a nine-year-old couldn't fake in eight hours of unconsciousness.

By eleven she'd stopped asking anyone to explain it. By twenty-six she had a system.

The stone goes in a drawer with eleven others just like it, each one slightly different in weight, in the particular grey of it, as though the ocean dream sends back a new sample every time to prove it isn't repeating itself. She catalogs them in a notebook: date, object, dream fragments she can still hold onto past the coffee. Most mornings the notebook is more honest than the dream ever was. She'll wake with only a bruised feeling of loss or velocity, and the object does the remembering for her — a torn movie ticket for a film that doesn't exist, a child's shoe two sizes too small, a spool of red thread that unspools forever if you let it, though she never lets it, not since the time she did and found herself weeping into her cereal for a reason the thread refused to give back.

There are rules she's learned. Don't try to sell them — a man on the internet offered good money for a dream-object once, a music box that played a tune no one else could hear, and when she mailed it to him the tune apparently stopped, then the box came back through her own mail slot a week later, waiting on the mat like a dog that had found its way home. Don't throw them away either. She'd tried, early on, tired of the clutter, tossed a rusted birdcage into a dumpster three blocks from her apartment. She dreamed about it for six straight nights, the specific ache of a small trapped thing, until she went and dug through garbage in her pajamas to bring it back, and the dreams stopped, and she never tested that rule again.

What she has never solved is the why of it. Some nights the object is obviously a metaphor doing overtime — a set of keys the week she signed her divorce papers, a paper boat the week her father's chemo started working. Other nights it is nothing at all, unattached to any waking grief she can locate, just an orange marble or a single roller skate, evidence of a life she's living somewhere else while her body sleeps, one she's not permitted to remember except through what it leaves in its pockets.

Today, stone in hand, she goes to the window like she does every ocean-Tuesday and holds it up to the light, though she knows already there's nothing to see, no barnacle or hidden note, just rock worn down by water that may or may not exist anywhere but in her. She used to think that if she gathered enough of them — enough keys, enough shoes, enough smooth grey stones — she'd eventually assemble the shape of the other life whole, lay it out on the bedroom floor like a jigsaw and finally see the picture.

Eleven years and a drawer full of proof later, she understands it doesn't work that way. The objects aren't pieces of a picture. They're the only tuition she'll ever get for a class she's not allowed to remember attending, a receipt for a place she has clearly, urgently, repeatedly been.

She puts the stone in the drawer with the others. She goes to make coffee. Somewhere, she assumes, the ocean keeps every ticket stub she's ever handed over, and keeps no record of her name at all.
