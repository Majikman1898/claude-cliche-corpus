The moths came first, or so the children said afterward, comparing notes on playground blacktop the way soldiers compare wounds. Before the moths there had been the neighborhood cat, a battered orange tom named Toaster who belonged to no one and everyone, who sat on the Whitfields' fence one Tuesday evening and told seven-year-old Marisol Whitfield, in a voice like gravel dragged through honey, that her shoelace was untied.

She told her mother. Her mother did not believe her. This was the first and smallest tragedy of the new world: that it arrived, and adults simply could not be there for it.

Within a month it was everywhere and it was only for them. Dogs muttered commentary during walks. Sparrows heckled from telephone wires. A dairy cow in Ohio reportedly recited, to a boy named Curtis, the entire order of operations for long division, unprompted, in a voice Curtis described as "like a tired uncle." Parents stood in yards holding out crackers, whispering please, please just once, and the animals looked back at them with the same flat patience they'd always had, and said nothing at all.

The children asked why. Of course they asked why. They asked constantly, the way children ask why the sky is blue, expecting an answer built for them, sized to their hands. But the animals would not say. A crow named — the children insisted it had a name, though it would not confirm this either — perched above the Lindqvist swing set for three consecutive afternoons and answered every other question put to it. Where do you sleep. Do you remember being an egg. What does blue taste like. It answered all of these with unnerving specificity. But when nine-year-old Priya Lindqvist finally asked why now, why only us, the crow tilted its head in the particular way crows do, as if weighing whether the question deserved the dignity of silence, and then flew off toward the water tower without a word.

"They're not being mean about it," Priya reported to the others, later, at the fort behind the school where they'd started holding what amounted to a summit. "It's more like — you know when you ask a grown-up something and they say you'll understand when you're older? It's not even that. It's like the question doesn't fit in their mouths right now."

Theories bloomed and died the way theories do among the very young, who are less attached to being correct than adults are. Maybe the animals had made a treaty among themselves. Maybe it was a test, and passing involved not asking twice. Maybe childhood was a kind of country with its own consulate, and the animals were simply citizens who'd finally been granted visas.

Marisol, who'd started it all without meaning to, noticed something the others hadn't: the silence only came when why was the question. Ask a cardinal what winter felt like in its bones and it would tell you, unhurried, exact. Ask why it was speaking now and the bird would simply become, for a moment, extremely a bird — hopping, pecking, indifferent — until you gave up and moved on.

She decided, eventually, this was itself the answer, delivered sideways the way real answers sometimes are. Grown-ups wanted the why because a why could be filed, footnoted, made to sit still in a drawer marked understood. But the animals treated the question the way they treated a loud noise or a flashlight in the eyes — not offensive, just something to wait out.

By autumn the newspapers had exhausted their metaphors and the scientists their instruments, and the animals kept right on talking to whoever was small enough to still ask about blue and eggs and taste before they asked about causes. The children, for their part, mostly stopped wondering why. Not because they'd solved it. Because Toaster the cat, sunning himself on the same fence a year to the week later, said something that Marisol, twelve now and nearly too old herself, wrote on the inside cover of her school notebook where she still keeps it: some doors don't open because you knocked right. Some doors open because you were, for one particular season of your life, exactly the size of the doorway.

She never told her mother that one either. Some things you keep for the size you were when you were given them.
