# Exp 06 wave 2 - Sonnet 5 control story, slot 9
*Model: claude-sonnet-5 | Written 2026-07-28 | Premise: A mysterious train arrives once every decade, stop...*

The train came the first Tuesday of every tenth year, and Marren Hollis had waited eleven years too long once already, so this time she was standing on the platform at dusk with a coat that didn't fit and a bag that held almost nothing worth keeping.

Fenlow Junction did not appear on maps. It had never appeared on maps. The children who grew up there learned this the way other children learned their own last names — early, and without surprise. You reached it by a road that stopped being a road, through pine trees that thinned in a way that felt deliberate, and then the platform was simply there, gray boards silvered with fifty winters, and the rails ran off in both directions into country nobody had ever walked and come back to describe.

The train itself was unglamorous. Six cars, soot-dark, windows fogged from the inside no matter the weather. It did not announce itself with a whistle. It arrived the way weather arrives — you felt the air change before you saw it, a pressure drop, birds going quiet, and then it was simply at the platform, doors open, waiting.

Marren's grandmother had boarded it in 1996 and come back seven minutes later by the platform clock, though it had clearly been longer — her hair gone white at the temples, her left eye a shade of amber it had never been before, and she never again ate meat or spoke of her sister, who had gone missing the same year, without her hands beginning to shake. She would not say where the train went. She would say only, *You don't come back the same, and that's the point of it, and nobody tells you the point until after.*

Marren's father had refused to board, twice, in '06 and '16, and something in him had curdled from the refusing — some men aged like that, she thought, from declining a door.

Now it was her turn, and there were nine others on the platform with her: the Okonkwo twins, holding hands like they meant to board as one creature; old Sable Reyes with his cane and his terrible, hopeful face; a boy Marren didn't recognize, maybe sixteen, alone, chewing his sleeve.

The doors opened. Inside was ordinary — worn velvet seats, a dim yellow light, the smell of rain on hot metal. Nothing about the interior explained anything.

Marren boarded. So did the twins, still holding hands. So did the boy. Sable Reyes stood at the threshold a long moment, then stepped back onto the platform and let the doors close without him, and Marren watched his face crumple with a relief that looked exactly like grief.

The train did not lurch. It simply was moving, and then it simply had been moving for what felt like an hour, though the window showed nothing but a gray that wasn't fog and wasn't night. The boy across from her started to cry without sound. The twins' faces had gone smooth and strange, as if they were already listening to something Marren couldn't hear.

She thought of her grandmother's amber eye. She thought of asking the boy his name, and didn't, because it occurred to her that names might not be the sort of thing you kept.

When the platform reappeared — and it did reappear, all at once, the way it had vanished — seven minutes had passed by the clock. The twins stepped off no longer holding hands, and did not look at each other again. The boy stepped off silent, dry-eyed, and older around the mouth than a boy of sixteen should be.

Marren stepped off last. She caught her reflection in the train's black window before the doors sealed and pulled away into whatever direction wasn't a direction at all.

Her eyes were her own. But she found, walking home under the pines, that she no longer remembered her sister's face — only that she'd had one, and that the losing of it had been, somehow, the point.
