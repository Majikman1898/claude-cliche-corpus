The water withdrew the way a held breath finally lets go — not a wave but an unveiling, three feet a day, then thirty, then more than anyone could track without disbelieving their own instruments.

Mira watched it from the cliff station at Punta Silva, where she'd logged tide heights for eleven years and had never once needed a word like *impossible* in her reports. Now she needed it hourly. Beneath the retreating waterline, the seafloor rose up scarred and strange, and on the ninth day the city appeared.

It sat where deep trench should have been, a basin no sonar had ever fully mapped, its towers black and fluted like burnt coral, streets laid out in spirals that hurt to trace with the eye. No barnacles crusted it. No centuries of silt. As if the ocean had been keeping it new, keeping it *waiting*, rather than burying it the way water buries everything else.

The forests came next — kelp, if kelp could grow four hundred feet and hold its shape without water's buoyancy to hold it up, standing now in the open air like cathedral columns, still faintly moving though no current touched them. Mira drove down with the survey team and stood at the edge of one such grove and felt the fronds lean, fractionally, toward her, the way a sunflower leans, though there was no sun involved in the gesture.

The creatures were the part the reports struggled with. Not fish out of water — those died fast, flopping and rotting in ordinary, forgivable ways, and the world grieved them in the ordinary fashion, with photographs and elegies. It was the other things. Things with too many joints for their size, colored in wavelengths the survey cameras rendered as static. Things that did not decompose, did not flee, did not do anything but continue, patiently, whatever it was they had been continuing to do in the dark for longer than Punta Silva's headland had existed.

One of them — long, sinuous, ringed like a stack of coins — lay draped across the spiral street of the black city, motionless, and when the drone got close enough Mira saw that its eye, singular and lidless, was open. Not dead. Waiting, the way the city waited. The way, she began to suspect, the whole retreating ocean was waiting, exhaling everything it had swallowed across some span of time too large for the word *ancient* to hold properly.

Her supervisor wanted measurements. Her supervisor wanted, specifically, to know when the water would return, as if this were tide and not testimony. Mira gave him numbers because numbers were owed. But at night she went back to the cliff, alone, past the checkpoint tape the government had strung up two days too late to matter, and looked down at the new geography of the drained world, lit by emergency floodlights that could not reach a fraction of it.

She thought about depth. Not as a measurement — she'd spent a career on that kind of depth — but as a kind of mercy. All her life the ocean had been a lid, and she had called it beautiful without once asking what the lid was for. Now the lid was off, and what lay underneath did not look built for eyes, did not look built for air, did not look built, in any sense she recognized, for a world with a sky in it.

The coin-ringed thing's eye tracked the drone until the battery died and the machine dropped from the air like something suddenly disbelieved in.

Mira wrote in her log, for the first time in eleven years, a line that was not a number: *the sea was not empty. it was a door, held shut.*

Then she watched the horizon, where somewhere past the new black spires the water still stood in its diminished blue wall, and waited, along with everything else exposed and everything patiently, endlessly not-dead, to learn whether the tide meant to come back in.
