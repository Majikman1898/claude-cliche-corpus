The house always chose the same corner of the kitchen, the one behind the pantry door where the light didn't quite reach. That was where Marta found them each morning: the objects.

Sometimes it was a spoon that didn't belong to any set she owned, tarnished at the bowl like it had been dipped in something that ate silver. Sometimes it was a folded scrap of paper covered in handwriting that wasn't hers but felt like it should have been. Once, memorably, an entire glass jar of buttons, none of which matched, all of them warm to the touch as if they'd just been poured from a body.

She'd stopped telling people about it after the second month. Her sister had laughed, assumed it was a bit, one of Marta's little performances, and when Marta didn't laugh back the conversation had curdled into something neither of them wanted to repeat. So she kept a shoebox under the bed instead, and every morning she added to it, and every night she dreamed, and every morning the box got heavier.

The dreams themselves she barely remembered. That was the cruelest part of the arrangement, whatever unfair physics governed it — the memory dissolved by the time her feet hit the floor, but the tax remained, sitting there behind the pantry door with the smug permanence of a bill already paid. She would stare at a rusted key or a single child's shoe and try to work backward, the way you might reconstruct a room from its shadow, and get nowhere. The object was evidence of a crime she could no longer recall committing.

Her therapist — a mild, unbothered woman named Dr. Achebe who took the shoebox in stride the way she took everything in stride — suggested Marta start photographing the items before she disposed of them. "Even the things we can't consciously access want documentation," she said, and Marta had nodded because it sounded true and vaguely spiritual, and also because it gave her something to do besides lie awake dreading whatever her subconscious was about to manufacture next.

The photography helped, a little. Patterns emerged the way constellations emerge from stars that have no actual relationship to one another. A run of dreams that left keys — seven of them across two weeks, none fitting any lock she tried them in — coincided, she realized only afterward, with the period she'd spent debating whether to leave her job. The buttons had come the week her mother called to say she was selling the house Marta grew up in. The spoon, she eventually decided, had shown up the night after she'd eaten dinner alone for the first time in a long while and hadn't hated it as much as she expected to.

It was not exactly comfort, understanding this. It was more like reading her own diary written by someone who despised complete sentences.

The night everything changed, she dreamed — she assumed she dreamed, though as ever there'd be no way to confirm it — of nothing at all, a long gray nothing like a held breath. She woke certain the streak was finally broken, that for once the pantry corner would be empty.

It wasn't. There was a second shoebox.

Identical to the one under her bed. Same brand, same water stain on the corner from the time the pipe had leaked two winters ago. She carried it to the kitchen table with the particular care of someone carrying something that might be a bomb or might be nothing, and when she lifted the lid she found, packed in tissue paper, every object she had already thrown away. The rusted keys. The buttons. The spoon, tarnish and all.

She sat with it a long time, the box open in the gray early light, and understood, without any of the memory that should have accompanied the understanding, that she had spent months quietly convinced these things were debris — silt kicked up by a mind clearing its throat in the dark. But nothing that returns wants to be called debris. It wants to be called an inventory.

She got a second shoebox of her own that afternoon, a proper one, cedar-lined, the kind meant to keep moths out of good sweaters. She didn't know yet what she was keeping in it, only that she intended, from now on, to ask.
