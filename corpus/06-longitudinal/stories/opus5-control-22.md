The kettle had boiled itself dry by the time Marguerite came down, which meant she'd dreamed about her mother again. Steam dreams always did that. She turned off the burner and looked around the kitchen for what the night had left.

It was on the windowsill: a brass button, the kind that came off a coat sleeve, tarnished green in the grooves. She turned it over. Nothing engraved. She put it in the jar on the counter with the others.

The rule, as far as anyone had worked it out, was one object per dream, deposited somewhere within twenty feet of the sleeper, appearing in the last minute before waking. Nobody knew what selected them. The relationship between the dream and the object was the great unsolved problem of the age, and had produced three competing schools, a quarterly journal, and a good deal of divorce.

Marguerite had dreamed of her mother — specifically, of her mother standing in a doorway saying something inaudible over the noise of a train — and gotten a button. She had dreamed of drowning, twice, and gotten a wooden spoon and a pair of reading glasses in a prescription that matched nobody she knew. She had dreamed of nothing at all, a gray flat nothing, and woken to find a live moth in a jar that had been empty and sealed.

Her upstairs neighbor Bo kept a spreadsheet. He had four thousand entries and a theory that the objects were not symbols but *residue* — that dreaming was a manufacturing process, and these were the offcuts, the shavings, meaningless the way sawdust is meaningless. Bo was a machinist and thought in those terms. He was probably right, Marguerite thought, and it did not help at all.

The trouble started when the objects began to match.

It was Bo who noticed. He came down one Tuesday with his laptop under his arm and a look on his face like he'd found a crack in a load-bearing wall. He'd been cross-referencing with a forum, thousands of people logging their morning finds, and he'd found clusters. On the night of the eleventh, two hundred and six people across four continents had all woken to a small chip of blue-glazed pottery. On the fourteenth, three hundred and eleven people found a single dried pea. Not similar objects. Identical ones — same glaze, same chip pattern, same wrinkle in the pea's skin.

"They're not copies," Bo said. "I got two of the eleventh-night people to mail me theirs. Look."

He put them on the table. Two chips of blue pottery. They fit together along a broken edge, and the fit was perfect, and Marguerite understood without wanting to that somewhere out in the world there were two hundred and four more pieces and that they made a bowl.

"So we're all dreaming the same thing," she said.

"No. That's the part. I interviewed forty of them. Completely different dreams. A woman in Osaka dreamed about a job interview. A kid in Lisbon dreamed about a dog he doesn't have. There's no common content."

"Then what's assembling the bowl?"

Bo didn't answer, because there wasn't one, and they both sat with that for a while.

Marguerite went home to her jar. She poured it out on the table: the button, the spoon, the glasses, seventeen keys, a thimble, a dozen small ordinary things. She'd always assumed they were hers — that her sleep had made them out of her own material, the way a body makes a fingernail. But if the pottery came from somewhere else, then so did the button, and it had belonged to a coat, and the coat had belonged to someone, and the someone had had a doorway and a train.

She thought about her mother in the doorway, saying something she couldn't hear.

She held the button up to the window. The tarnish was real. The wear on the shank was real, thirty or forty years of being pushed through a hole and pulled back out. Nobody had made this last night. It had simply *arrived* last night, the way a letter arrives, having been written long before.

She went to bed early, and lay there a long while, hoping to be sent the rest of it.
