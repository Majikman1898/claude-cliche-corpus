The crows started it, which surprised no one who knew crows.

Maya was seven and eating a cheese sandwich on the low wall behind the library when the big one landed beside her and said, "You should eat the crusts. They're the best part." She didn't scream. Later, adults would find this the most alarming detail — that not one child screamed.

"Why?" she asked.

"More flavor," said the crow. "Toasted by the oven from both sides."

"No. Why are you talking?"

The crow tilted its head so one black eye held her whole. Then it flew off, and Maya finished her sandwich, crusts included.

By September it was everywhere. A golden retriever in Milwaukee narrating the smells of a park to a four-year-old. Rats under Warsaw discussing the weather with a boy who'd run away and been found. A pod of orcas off the Washington coast that sang, in accented English, the names of six children on a whale-watching boat, then went silent when the adults leaned over the rail.

The refusal was total and it was the same everywhere. Ask an animal anything — what's your name, do you like being a horse, is my grandmother okay now — and it answered, sometimes at length. Ask *why can you talk*, or *why only us*, and you got nothing. Not evasion. Not a change of subject. A pause, a look, and then the conversation resuming somewhere else entirely, as though you'd said something in a language it had decided not to hear.

Dr. Aline Ferreira ran the largest study. Two hundred and forty children, thirty species, eighteen months. She built a soundproofed room with a one-way mirror and hid four graduate students behind it; the goats spoke freely to the children and said nothing about the mirror. She tried scripts. She gave a girl named Odile a card that read WHY WON'T YOU SAY WHY and had her hold it up to a raven.

The raven read it. Everyone agreed the raven read it — the eye moved left to right, twice.

"Odile," it said, "your shoelace is coming undone."

The papers got scientific for a while and then got religious, which is what papers do. There were theories. A test we were failing. A test we were passing. Something ancient waking up and choosing, sensibly, to negotiate with the only humans who hadn't yet decided what the world was. A parent who tells a child *because I said so* is not withholding a reason; often there isn't one that would survive translation.

What no one wrote about much, because it made poor television, was how ordinary it became. Children grew up asking the neighborhood cats about the neighborhood. Farm kids had opinions about their animals that were, unbearably, the animals' opinions too. Slaughterhouse enrollment collapsed in a single generation without a law being passed, because the workforce had all been children once and remembered the pigs.

And then, at some point between eleven and fourteen — it varied, it was never sharp — it stopped. Not the animals. Them. A boy would notice his dog had gone quiet and would realize, with a lurch, that he couldn't remember the last thing it had said. The words were still there in memory, but flattened, the way a dream is flat by noon.

Maya was thirty-one, a veterinarian, when she found the crow with the broken wing behind the same library. She wrapped it in her jacket. She said, out of a habit she had never quite discarded, "You should eat the crusts."

The crow said nothing, because it could not, because she was thirty-one.

But it looked at her with one black eye, and she thought — and admitted later she had no evidence for this, and would have failed her own board exam for saying it — she thought it was making sure she knew that the silence was not a punishment. That the door had not closed. That she had simply grown into the kind of creature that asks *why* first, and that this was fine, this was necessary, someone had to build the roads and the hospitals.

She set the wing. She let it go in the spring.

That night her daughter came downstairs, four years old, furious.

"Mama," she said, "the crow outside says you did it wrong."
