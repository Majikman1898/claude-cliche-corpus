The tide had been going out for eleven years.

Not tides in the old sense — those still came and went, twice a day, obedient to the moon. But underneath them the whole ocean was draining, a bathtub with a slow leak somewhere no one could find. Three inches a year at first. Then a foot. Then nobody bothered measuring, because the coastline moved faster than the surveyors could walk.

Ivo Sarkany walked it anyway. That was his job: municipal cartographer for a town that had been a fishing port and was now, improbably, landlocked. Every Tuesday he took the old harbor road out past the rotting piers and kept going, down the exposed shelf, marking where the water met the mud. Every Tuesday the mark was farther out.

The first city appeared in the fourth year, off what had been the Balearic shelf. Everyone assumed it was Atlantis, because everyone always assumes it's Atlantis. It wasn't. It had streets, but the streets had no corners — they curved into one another like the whorls of a fingerprint, and no matter which way you walked, you ended up at the same low structure at the center, which had no door and no roof and no purpose anyone could name. The stone was not stone. It was something denser, warmer to the touch than the air around it. Carbon dating returned a number that made the lab technician resign.

By the seventh year there were forty-one such cities. They did not match each other. They did not match anything.

Ivo did not care much about the cities. He cared about the shallows, the new-exposed slope where the old abyssal plain was becoming a plain plain, and the things left stranded in the pools there.

They were not fish. Some had the general courtesy to resemble fish, but even those had too many symmetries, or a lateral line that pulsed with light in a rhythm that was not a heartbeat but something more deliberate, more like counting. Others resembled nothing. He found one in year nine that was, as best he could describe it in his notebook, a hole in the shape of a jellyfish — a region where the pool water simply declined to be. He watched it for six hours. It moved. When the sun went down it stopped moving, and by morning the pool had filled the gap and there was nothing there at all.

He stopped submitting reports after that. The reports came back annotated by people who had never left a laboratory, correcting his terminology, insisting on names for things that had refused names.

In the eleventh year the water pulled back past the last shelf and revealed the trench.

Ivo came to the edge of it at dawn. The drop went down and down, past the depth where light had ever gone, and at the bottom of it — visible, dry, in ordinary morning sun — was a plain of pale sediment. And laid out across that plain, in an arrangement he could see whole from where he stood, were the cities. All of them. The forty-one and hundreds more, and they were not separate cities, they were districts. Curving streets flowing into curving streets, and every one of them spiraling inward toward a shape at the center that he could not resolve because his eyes kept sliding off it, the way they slide off a word in a language you have never learned.

He understood, standing there with his surveyor's chain and his ruined boots, that the ocean had not been water. Or had not only been water. It had been a lid. Something had been set over the top of this, deliberately and a very long time ago, by someone who understood that certain things are best kept unlit.

And the lid was coming off.

Behind him, back up the exposed shelf, the pools were drying. The counting lights had gone out one by one over the past months and he had assumed the creatures were dying.

Now, in the trench, something down in the unresolvable center began to pulse. Slow. Patient.

Counting.

Ivo took out his notebook, and wrote the date, and after some consideration wrote nothing else, and closed it, and started walking back toward a town that no longer had a sea.
