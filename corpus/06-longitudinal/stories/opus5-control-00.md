The change came at 4:12 on a Tuesday morning, though nobody in Halvorsen City agreed on that later. What people agreed on was the color: a faint amber shimmer above the head, like heat off asphalt, and inside it the words, moving.

Ruth Abaya, who drove the 6 AM 44 bus down Cordell Avenue, was the first person she knew of to see it. She had stopped at a light and glanced at the man crossing in front of her, a man in a suit carrying a cake box, and above him she read: *don't drop it don't drop it don't drop it she'll cry if you drop it.* She sat through the green light. Behind her, horns. Above the horns, in amber: *MOVE*, and *what is wrong with her*, and from the car three back, *I think my father is dying and I have to go to work anyway.*

By seven the city had stopped functioning in the ordinary way. Not because of chaos — there was less shouting than you'd expect — but because everyone was reading. People stood in intersections with their heads tipped back like tourists. The financial district emptied itself out onto the sidewalks. A woman in a gray coat sat down on the steps of the courthouse and did not get up for four hours, because everyone who passed her was a novel.

The predicted catastrophes mostly did not happen. Some did. Three marriages ended before noon on Ashfield Street alone. A city councilman was found in his car with the engine running and the garage door open, which was the mercy in it. But the great fear — that the city would tear itself apart over what it learned — didn't quite arrive, and afterward people argued about why.

Ruth had a theory. She'd spent eleven years watching faces come up the steps of her bus, and she had always assumed she knew what was behind them: irritation, mostly, and impatience with her, and the low contempt that people have for the person operating the machine they're stuck inside. That's what the faces said. But that first morning, as they filed on and paid and looked at her, she read: *she has kind eyes*, and *is that the same driver from before, I like her*, and *God I hope I remembered to lock the door*, and *my back hurts*, and *my back hurts*, and *my back hurts.*

Almost nobody was thinking about her at all. And the ones who were, were thinking something soft.

That was the thing that nobody had prepared for. Everyone had braced for the cruelty and the lust and the petty math of judgment, and yes, all of it was there, amber and shameless above every head in the city — *she's gotten heavy*, *he's an idiot*, *I want*, *I want*, *I want.* But it turned out that cruelty was a smaller share of the total than anyone had feared, and that it was almost never the main thing, and that it passed. A man would think something vicious about the woman next to him on the platform and then, eleven seconds later, think about a dog he'd had in 1994. The vicious thought had felt, from the inside, like a verdict. From the outside it looked like weather.

What people could not get used to — what broke them, when they broke — was the loneliness. The sheer volume of it, hanging over the crowd like smog. *Nobody knows this about me. Nobody knows this about me. Nobody knows this about me*, ten thousand people thinking it at once in the same station, each one certain of it, each one wrong.

It lasted nine days. When it stopped, the city went strange and quiet for a week, and then, mostly, back to normal. People stopped talking about it. There was a period where strangers were unbearably gentle with each other and then that faded too, the way it does.

But Ruth kept a habit from it. When somebody came up the steps of her bus, hunched, avoiding her eyes, she made a point of saying good morning, out loud, every time — because she had read the smog and she knew exactly what was in it, and she knew that saying anything at all cut a small hole in it, and that the hole closed fast.
