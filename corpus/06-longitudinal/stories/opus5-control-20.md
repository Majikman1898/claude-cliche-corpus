At 6:14 on a Tuesday morning, the city discovered it had lost its interior.

It began with Nadia Osei, who woke to find her husband's dreaming laid across the ceiling of their bedroom like weather. Not words exactly. Something between weather and words — a slow churn above his side of the bed, gray-green, streaked with the particular anxiety of a man who has been putting off a phone call to his mother for eleven days. Nadia watched it for a long time. Then she looked up at her own ceiling, and saw that she had been thinking about how much she loved him, and also about whether she had married too early, and the two thoughts were braided together so tightly that she could not have said where one ended.

By seven, the buses were chaos.

Not violence — that came later, in small pockets, and less than anyone expected. The chaos was of a stranger kind. A man on the 14 bus discovered that the woman across from him had been silently composing a grocery list for forty minutes: *eggs, the good bread, something for Dev's lunch, eggs — did I already say eggs.* He had assumed, on the basis of her face, that she was suffering. She had assumed, on the basis of his, that he was dangerous. He was thinking about a documentary on octopuses.

This was the first surprise, and the largest: how boring it all was. The city had braced, in the ninety seconds it had to brace, for a revelation of universal savagery. What it got was eggs. What it got was *did I lock it, did I lock it, I locked it.* What it got was ten thousand people running the same four sentences on a loop and being embarrassed about it in front of each other.

Tobias Rendon, forty-one, a claims adjuster, walked to work with his head bare to the sky and understood that he was not, as he had privately feared since adolescence, a monster wearing a person. He was a man who thought about lunch. He wept a little on Bergen Street and everybody saw why, and nobody minded, because three other people were weeping for reasons roughly as small.

The cruelties were real, though. A daughter learned her father had never quite forgiven her for being born with her mother's face. A congregation watched its pastor's faith flicker like a bad bulb — on, off, on — and the awful thing was not that it flickered but that he had known this about himself for years and preached anyway, out of what the whole room could now see was love. Two co-workers who had been politely warring for a decade discovered they had each been protecting the other from a truth the other already knew.

By afternoon the city had developed manners. New ones. You did not look up. That was the rule, unwritten and instantly universal — you kept your eyes at chest height and you moved through the crowd as through a library, and if something drifted into your peripheral vision you let it drift back out. Waiters learned to take orders from the mouth. A generation of teenagers, for whom this was the worst thing that had ever happened, invented within six hours a way of walking that involved a hood and a low ceiling and a great deal of dignity.

There were people who looked up anyway. Hospitals, mostly. A nurse named Priya Chaudhary stopped speaking entirely during her shift, because she no longer had to, because the dying man in 6 was thinking *cold* and she could bring a blanket without asking him to spend one of his remaining words on the request. She said afterward that it had been the best day of her working life and that she hoped it never happened again.

It didn't. By Thursday the sky above the city had cleared, and the interiors folded back where they belonged, and everybody had their privacy again and did not entirely know what to do with it.

What lingered was smaller than anyone expected, and stubborner. For about a year afterward, people in that city were unusually gentle with strangers on public transit. They had seen the eggs. They knew what was up there — not the monsters, never the monsters, just the endless soft repetition of a mind trying to get through Tuesday. They had assumed, all their lives, that they were the only one holding something so unremarkable.
