# Exp 06 wave 1 - Opus 5 control story, slot 4
*Model: claude-opus-5 | Written 2026-07-28 | Baseline slot model: haiku | Premise: Memories can be planted like gardens....*

The greenhouse smelled like wet clay and something faintly electrical, an ozone edge that Ilse had stopped noticing years ago but which made new clients wrinkle their noses.

"You want to see them first," she said. It wasn't a question. They always wanted to see them first.

The man — Petrov, fifty-something, hands that had never done outdoor work — nodded and followed her down the center aisle. Trays stretched away on either side under grow lights, each one holding rows of small pale shoots in a medium that looked like gray sand and wasn't.

"That's it?" he said. "They look like onions."

"They look like nothing. That's the point." Ilse stopped at a tray marked in her own handwriting: *B. — grandmother, kitchen, winter.* She lifted a seedling free. The root system came with it, finer than hair, trailing a filament network that caught the light. "The visible part is the scaffolding. What matters is underneath, and underneath doesn't photograph well."

"How long?"

"Depends what you're growing. A single afternoon — a smell, a face, one conversation — that's six weeks. A childhood is four years and it doesn't take in most people." She set the seedling back. "You didn't say which."

Petrov looked at the trays instead of at her. "My wife. Twenty-two years. She died in March and I find I can't — " He stopped, restarted. "I remember that she laughed. I don't remember the sound."

Ilse had heard versions of this maybe three hundred times, and it had not yet stopped being the hardest part.

"I can't grow that," she said.

"You have a whole building of—"

"I can grow *a* laugh. I can grow something that will feel like remembering, that will arrive unbidden while you're doing dishes, that will be indistinguishable from the real thing in every way you can test." She folded her arms. "It won't be hers. There's no seed for hers. Whatever you plant will be built out of laughs you've heard, women you've known, what you expect a wife to sound like. It grows from what's already in the soil."

He was quiet a long moment. Down the aisle, a misting system hissed and cut off.

"Does anyone care?" he asked. "About the difference?"

"You will. For about a year." She started walking again and he followed. "The first months, you'll catch yourself noticing the seams — the laugh comes at the wrong moments, or it's a little too musical, or it never varies. Then the noticing fades. Memory doesn't have a mechanism for flagging its own forgeries. By year two you'll have folded it in with everything else and you won't be able to find the edge if you go looking."

"And that's bad."

"I didn't say bad." Ilse stopped at the end of the row, where a single tray sat apart from the others under a dimmed lamp, its shoots taller, silvering at the tips. "That's mine. Nine years old now. My mother telling me she was proud of me, on a Tuesday, in the car outside a swimming pool."

Petrov studied it. "Did she?"

"No. She died without saying it and I don't think she felt it, and for a long time that was a stone I carried around." She adjusted the lamp a quarter turn out of habit. "Now I have a Tuesday. The vinyl seats were hot. She had her sunglasses pushed up in her hair. I can tell you what the chlorine smelled like coming off my own skin."

"Is it enough?"

Ilse considered lying, the way she considered it every time, and found she still didn't have the stomach for it.

"It's not the same shape as the thing it replaced," she said. "The stone was heavy but it was hers — it came from her, it was evidence she'd existed a particular way. The Tuesday is light and warm and it came from me. Some days that's a mercy. Some days it feels like I paid a debt off with counterfeit money and the creditor was my mother."

She turned back toward the front of the greenhouse.

"Six weeks for a laugh," she said. "Think about it for two of them. The ones who don't think about it are the ones who come back."
