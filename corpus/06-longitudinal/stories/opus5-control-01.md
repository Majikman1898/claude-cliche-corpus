# Exp 06 wave 1 - Opus 5 control story, slot 1
*Model: claude-opus-5 | Written 2026-07-28 | Baseline slot model: haiku | Premise: The world's luck is finite, and someone has begun ...*

The lottery in Hesperia County was won three hundred and eleven consecutive times by tickets purchased at a gas station off Route 9, which is where Delia Ostrander started, because that is where the actuaries start when the numbers break.

She was not an actuary. She had been one, for eleven years, and then she had been the woman who noticed the shape in the data and would not stop saying so at meetings, and then she had been unemployed. Now she kept a ledger. In it she recorded, in a hand that had grown small and careful, every improbability she could verify: the four aircraft that landed after total hydraulic failure in a single April; the eight children pulled unhurt from a collapsed school in Novgorod; the man in Lisbon who survived a fall from the eleventh floor onto a parked van and walked away complaining about his hat.

The good luck was clustering. That was the first thing. Luck, properly understood, is a fine dust that settles evenly, and this had begun to drift — into certain rooms, certain postal codes, certain names.

The second thing was the debt.

Because the school in Novgorod had collapsed. Because the aircraft had lost their hydraulics in the first place. Because for every miracle in her ledger there was a page facing it, and on that page were the things that had never used to happen: the bridge in Ohio that failed on a windless Tuesday, inspected the month before, every bolt within tolerance. The pediatric ward in Cardiff where four separate machines stopped in four separate rooms in the same hour, and no engineer could find a fault in any of them, because there was no fault. There was only an absence of the thing that usually makes machines keep working, which nobody has a name for, and which everyone relies on.

The gas station off Route 9 had a clerk named Emil who was seventy-two and had a cough.

"Same fella," he said. "Every Tuesday. Buys one ticket, hands it to whoever's in line behind him. Never keeps it."

"And they win."

"Every time." Emil laughed, and the laugh turned into the cough, and the cough went on a while. "Figured he was some kind of saint."

Delia waited three Tuesdays. On the third, a thin man in a grey coat came in at 4:40, bought a single ticket, and turned to hand it to her.

She did not take it.

"How much have you moved?" she asked.

He lowered his hand. Up close he looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep — the tiredness of something running at a loss for a very long time.

"You're the one with the notebook," he said.

"I'm the one with the notebook."

"Then you already know it isn't stealing." He set the ticket on the counter. "It was never distributed fairly. It pooled. It sat in vaults and bloodlines and it did nothing. Some families have been drawing on inherited fortune for two hundred years and calling it character. I only opened the valves."

"The ward in Cardiff."

"Yes."

"Four children."

"Yes." He said it without flinching, which was worse than flinching. "And eight in Novgorod. And nineteen on the ferry at Kos, and the crew of the Sofia Marie, and I have every one of them written down too, in a book like yours but longer. I am not pretending it's free. I'm saying the ledger was always running. Someone was always paying. The only thing I changed was who."

Delia looked at the ticket on the counter. Outside, the light was going the particular orange of late autumn, and somewhere in the world, she understood, a coin was landing wrong for someone who had never asked to be part of the arithmetic.

"You can't decide that," she said.

"No," he agreed. "But it was being decided. It was just being decided by nobody, which everyone had agreed to call fair."

He went out. The bell over the door rang, and the door caught on the frame, and stuck, the way doors do.

Delia stood in the fluorescent hum with the ticket in front of her and did not touch it, and behind her Emil coughed, and coughed, and then stopped.
