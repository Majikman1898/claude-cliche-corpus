The dog told Maisie first, on a Tuesday, while she was tying her shoe on the back step.

"Not that lace," he said. "The other one's coming loose."

Maisie looked at Bergen, who was eleven years old and had never been much for anything but sleeping in the warm rectangle by the sliding door. His mouth had moved. She had watched it move.

"You can talk."

"Fix your lace."

She fixed her lace. Then she said, "Why can you talk?"

And Bergen turned his grey face away and looked out at the fence line, and said nothing at all, and did not say anything else for the rest of the day, though she followed him around the yard asking until her mother called her in.

It happened everywhere that week. In Lagos a girl's parakeet asked her to move its cage away from the window because the light hurt in the mornings. In Ontario a boy walking the treeline heard a crow tell him, matter-of-factly, that his uncle's truck had a bad tire. In hospitals the therapy dogs began saying names, and the children in the beds said the names back, and the nurses stood in doorways with charts going soft in their hands.

The adults could not hear it. That was the part that made the news. A father would stand three feet from his daughter while she carried on a full conversation with the cat, and he would hear only the ordinary noises of a cat, and his daughter's half of something.

They tried everything. Microphones caught nothing. High-speed film of a horse's mouth showed a horse's mouth. A team in Uppsala put a seven-year-old and a goat in a room with forty-one sensors and got forty-one flat lines and a transcript the child wrote out afterward in pencil, which said mostly that the goat was bored and wanted to go outside.

The children were interviewed, gently and then less gently. They all reported the same thing: the animals would talk about anything — weather, food, whether a person was kind, what they remembered of last winter — but the moment you asked *why*, they stopped. Not evasion. Not "I can't say." Just a door closing, quietly, and the animal going back to being an animal until you talked about something else.

Maisie asked Bergen ninety-one times over four months. She kept a tally in a notebook with a horse on the cover.

"Why do you talk to me?"

Silence, and the tail going slowly.

"Is it because I'm small?"

Silence.

"Is it because you're going to die soon?"

Bergen put his chin on her foot. "That's a rude question."

"Sorry."

"It's alright. You're allowed rude questions. You're the right size for them."

She wrote that down. Everyone was writing things down that year. There were committees. A theologian in Rome said one thing and a different theologian said the opposite, and both of them were fifty-eight years old, and neither of them had heard so much as a syllable from a pigeon.

What nobody wanted to say out loud, in the end, was the obvious thing, which the children mostly figured out on their own and mostly didn't bother explaining, because explaining was an adult habit: the animals were not refusing to answer. They were refusing to answer *them*. The question "why" belonged to a way of talking that the whole arrangement was built to leave outside. To answer it would have been to hand the thing over to the people it wasn't for.

Bergen died in November, in the warm rectangle, which had moved across the floor as the sun got low and lazy.

Maisie sat with him a long time. She was nine and she knew what she was looking at.

"You never told me why," she said.

He was very tired. His eyes were mostly shut.

"No," he agreed.

"Will you tell me now?"

And Bergen breathed out, and did not answer, and she understood that he wasn't being cruel, and she put her hand on his ribs and felt them go up, and go down, and go up.

She was fourteen when she stopped hearing them. It happened the way it happened to everyone — not a moment, just a gradual thinning, until one day she said something to a sparrow on a fence post and the sparrow was only a sparrow.

She didn't mind as much as she expected. She had already been told the important things. She just hadn't been told why, and by then she had come around to thinking that was probably the point.
