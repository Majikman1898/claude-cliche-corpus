The tide went out in 1997 and never fully came back.

At first it was called a statistical anomaly. Then a trend. Then, after the Sargasso shelf stood up out of the water like a drowned man rising, they stopped calling it anything and just watched.

Mira's grandmother had been a fisherwoman in Puerto Deseado. Mira herself had never touched living seawater. By the time she was old enough to want to, the Atlantic had pulled back forty kilometers from the old coast, leaving a plain of grey silt and rusting hulls and, further out, the things.

She surveyed them for the Commission. That was the job title: Surveyor, Exposed Benthic Heritage. In practice it meant she drove a tracked rover across salt flats for eleven hours a day, photographing structures that had no business existing and filing reports that no one seemed to read.

The city she was mapping that autumn had no name yet. The Commission called it Site 340. It sat in what had been the Argentine Basin, four thousand meters below the old surface, and it was built of something that resembled coral the way a cathedral resembles a pile of bricks. Towers spiraled. Streets ran in patterns that were not quite grids and not quite spirals and made her eyes water if she tried to follow them for too long. Nothing in it was sized for a human body. The doorways were low and wide. The stairs, if they were stairs, rose in increments of nine centimeters.

She had been told, in the briefing, that there was no consensus on whether it had been made. Geology had theories. So did biology. The theories did not agree, and the men who held them had stopped speaking to each other in 2019.

What no one talked about, in briefings or papers, was that the city was not empty.

The first one she saw was in a courtyard, if it was a courtyard. It had the general shape of a sea star scaled up to the size of a delivery van, with a hide like wet slate, and it was moving — slowly, so slowly that she had watched it for six minutes before she understood that the shadow beneath it had shifted. It was not dying. That was the thing. Everything at that depth should have burst or boiled or simply stopped when the pressure left. This one had reorganized. Its underside had grown a kind of bellows.

She reported it. The Commission thanked her and asked her to continue mapping the eastern quarter.

There were more, after that. A ribbon of translucent tissue thirty meters long that draped itself over a tower and pulsed at intervals of roughly four hours. Something in a flooded basin that had eyes arranged in a ring and did not appear to use them. Colonies of small pale things that built structures out of their own shed material, structures that were, at a scale of one to two hundred, unmistakably the city itself.

Mira wrote that observation down twice and then deleted it both times.

In November she found the sound. It came up through the rover's frame more than through the air — a slow beat, subsonic, spaced about nine seconds apart. She parked and sat with her hand on the dash and felt it in her back teeth. It went on for two hours. When it stopped, every pale colony in view had turned to face the same direction: east, toward what was left of the water.

She thought about her grandmother then, hauling nets in the dark, cursing the cold, throwing back what was too small. A woman who had spent forty years on the surface of a thing and never once wondered what the thing might have been doing underneath her.

The sea kept going out. The models said it would stop. The models had said that in 1999, and 2004, and 2031.

Mira filed her report that evening — coordinates, dimensions, photographs, the sound described in flat technical language with a note that the source could not be localized. She sent it to the Commission. Then she sat outside the rover in the enormous silence and looked east, where the horizon was and where the water had gone, and she understood, without any particular fear, that the retreat was not an emptying.

It was an uncovering. And uncovering is a thing done with intent, by someone, for someone else to see.
