# Exp 06 wave 1 - Opus 5 control story, slot 17
*Model: claude-opus-5 | Written 2026-07-28 | Baseline slot model: opus | Premise: Time freezes everywhere except inside libraries....*

The clocks stopped at 4:17 on a Tuesday in November, and nobody noticed for a long time, because nobody was looking at a clock.

Marisol noticed because she was shelving. She had a cart of returns and she was halfway down the 900s when the light coming through the high windows stopped being light that moved. It had been the low slanting kind, dust turning over in it. Then the dust simply hung. She watched a mote sit in the air like a nail driven into nothing.

She went to the front doors. Outside, a man on the steps was frozen mid-sneeze, coat flaring, one hand not quite to his face. A pigeon hung a foot above the pavement with its wings down. The traffic on Ferrand Street was a solid thing, a sculpture of itself.

Marisol pushed the door open and the door opened. She put her hand out past the frame and her hand stopped. Not painfully. It simply arrived at a place where there was no next moment for it to occupy, and hung there like the pigeon, and she pulled it back and it was warm again and hers.

There were eleven people in the library.

That was the first count. She did it twice more over the following days, when days still meant something, and got eleven each time, and then she stopped counting because it seemed unkind — as though she were confirming an inventory rather than knowing a group of people.

Old Terrence had been asleep in the periodicals and stayed asleep for what Marisol guessed was two hours. When he woke he took the news with terrible calm. He'd been a merchant sailor. He said the sea did things like this, or near enough, and asked whether the coffee machine still worked. It did. Everything inside worked. The lights, the taps, the toilets, the ancient photocopier. Whatever had frozen the world had drawn a careful line around them and left the utilities on inside it, the way you'd leave a lamp burning for someone who'd gone out.

The food was the trouble, and then it wasn't. The staff fridge held four yogurts, a sandwich, and half a birthday cake for a woman named Priya who was not there. They rationed it. On the fourth day, when they went to the fridge expecting the last of the cake, there were four yogurts, a sandwich, and half a cake. Nobody said anything for a while. Then Terrence said, well, and took a yogurt.

They made a life. This is the part Marisol thinks about most, later. They made a life with astonishing speed. The two teenagers who had come in to use the computers organized a lending system so that no one hoarded the good novels. A woman named Ada, who had been an accountant and had come in only to escape a phone call, taught herself Portuguese and then taught it to three others, badly, and they spoke it to each other at meals as a joke that stopped being a joke. A man whose name Marisol never learned — he asked to be called Mole and would not explain — read every book on the third floor, in order, and reported on them nightly from the reference desk like a war correspondent. The children's section became the sleeping room, because of the carpet.

Nobody tried to leave after the first week. You could stand in the doorway and put your face into the stopped air and feel the enormous patience of it, and you came away understanding that it wasn't a wall. It was simply where things ended.

Marisol read more in that time than in the thirty-one years before it. She read the way you eat when you have been hungry a long while and did not know it. She read on the floor between the stacks with her back against the cold metal. She found a book about glaciers and learned that ice appears to be still and is not, that it moves at a pace which requires a different word than moving.

She thought: that's us out there. That's the whole world out there, moving so slowly it looks like stone.

And in here, she thought, we are the only fast thing left. All eleven of us, burning through our hours in a building full of dead people's sentences, and the sentences are what's keeping us lit.

She never learned why. That bothered the others more than it bothered her.
