# Exp 06 wave 1 - Opus 5 control story, slot 3
*Model: claude-opus-5 | Written 2026-07-28 | Baseline slot model: haiku | Premise: A mountain slowly walks across the continent once ...*

The mountain's name, in the language of the people who had lived longest at its foot, was simply Grandmother, and for nine hundred and ninety-two years it had been a mountain like any other. Basalt shoulders. A cap of snow that thinned each summer and never quite vanished. Goats on the western scree. A road that switchbacked up to a pass where the wind made a sound like a woman clearing her throat.

Ilsa Verrant had spent eleven years measuring it.

Her instruments were bolted into bedrock at forty stations, and every one of them said the same thing, which was nothing. The mountain sat. Rock does creep — glaciers of stone, whole ranges sagging a millimeter a century under their own weight — but that was not walking, and Ilsa had not come here for that.

She had come because of the ditches.

They ran across the plain to the east, eight of them, parallel and enormous, silted to the brim and grown over with saltbush so that from the ground they were merely a stripe of greener scrub. From the air they were unmistakable: a track, four kilometers wide, running west-northwest for eleven hundred kilometers, terminating at Grandmother's eastern flank. The geological survey called them relict fluvial channels. Ilsa had spent a year proving they were not. Water does not carry basalt boulders uphill. Water does not leave a trench with a bottom pressed flat and glassy, the mineral grains flowed and refrozen the way sand fuses under a great and patient weight.

Someone had asked her, at a conference, what she thought had made them.

"A foot," she said.

She did not go back to that conference.

The local histories were no help and every kind of help. There were songs about Grandmother going to visit her sister, which the ethnographers had catalogued as a fertility motif, and there was a particular grammatical construction in the old dialect — a tense used only for things that happen once in a life you will not live to see twice. It was applied to three subjects: the great flood, a certain comet, and the mountain's traveling.

Ilsa's calculations, done badly at first and then very carefully, put the interval at 1,014 years and the last event at 1,009 years ago, plus or minus a decade she could not narrow.

She told no one. She wrote grant applications about slope stability.

In the autumn of the eleventh year, the goats left. Not dramatically — they simply were not there, and the herders said the animals had gone down to the low pastures early and would not be driven back. Then the springs on the north face ran cloudy. Then, in November, every one of Ilsa's forty stations recorded, simultaneously, a rotation of eight-thousandths of a degree.

It took eleven months to complete the first step.

There was no thunder. There was no cracking open of the earth. The village at the foot had four seasons' warning and the villagers, who had been told by their grandmothers and had believed it the way one believes in a distant war, moved their houses two valleys over with the unhurried competence of people following a plan drafted long ago. The road's switchbacks stretched and then failed, the pass migrating northward at the speed of a fingernail growing.

Ilsa stayed. She camped on the moraine and watched the horizon change by degrees too small to see and too large to argue with, and she understood, sometime in the second year, that she had been asking the wrong question. Not *how*. Not even *why*. The question was what it meant that a thing could move so slowly that the only creatures capable of noticing were the ones who wrote things down, and that this — the writing down, the eleven years of instruments, the tense reserved for the once-seen — was perhaps the only reason the walking was witnessed at all.

Grandmother would arrive somewhere, in four centuries or six. Ilsa would not see it. Her data would, if anyone kept it.

She built a cairn on the moraine, and cut into the largest stone the date, and the bearing, and a single sentence in the old dialect, in the tense for things you see once.

Then she went down to the low pastures, where the goats were, and where the light in the evening came from a direction that was, very gradually, no longer quite the same.
