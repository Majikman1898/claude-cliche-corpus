# Exp 06 wave 1 - Opus 5 control story, slot 8
*Model: claude-opus-5 | Written 2026-07-28 | Baseline slot model: sonnet | Premise: People can swap memories like possessions...*

The exchange happened in the back of a laundromat on Tolliver Street, behind a curtain of hanging bedsheets that smelled like other people's fabric softener.

Mira had brought her mother's death.

She'd kept it seven years, and it had done what such things do — spread. It had gotten into her coffee, into the way she answered the phone, into her tolerance for people who complained about their parents. The broker, a soft-handed man named Ozols who wore reading glasses on a beaded chain, had asked her three times if she was certain.

"It's a strong one," he said, turning the ampoule under the lamp. The memory inside looked like nothing. They all looked like nothing. "Somebody will pay well. But strong ones leave a socket. You'll feel around for her and find a hole with the edges filed smooth."

"That's the idea."

"Some people say that's worse."

"Some people had better mothers."

That wasn't true — Mira's mother had been fine, had been good, had made her lunches with the crusts left on because crusts build character — and that was precisely the trouble. The dying had been eleven weeks of a very good woman becoming a small yellow animal in a rented bed, and it had eaten the other forty years the way a fire eats a photo album, from the edges inward. Mira could no longer picture her mother's face without the hospice light on it.

Ozols slid a second ampoule across the table. "Trade or cash?"

"What's the trade?"

"Somebody's honeymoon. Croatia. Nineteen ninety-six. Very clean, very warm, sold by a widow who found she couldn't hold it."

She thought about that. Somebody else's happiness, wearing her like a coat. "Cash."

He counted it out in the unhurried way of a man who has watched a great many people sell their pasts and does not judge any of them, and then he pressed the ampoule to the soft skin behind her ear, and there was a sensation like a tooth being drawn from a place that had never had teeth.

She walked home. It was raining, unremarkably.

The socket was where he said it would be. She reached for the eleven weeks and found instead a kind of clerical summary: *your mother died, you were there, it was hard.* Facts with no temperature. She kept probing it the way a tongue goes to a gap, and each time there was that smooth filed edge, and each time a small relief.

It took four days for her to notice what else had gone.

She was making tea and she reached for the memory of her mother teaching her to test the water — *listen, when it stops sounding like rain and starts sounding like wind, that's boiling* — and it was there, but flat. A card in a catalogue. Because the eleven weeks had not been a separate room. They had been the hallway. Everything her mother was ran through that dying and came out the other side changed, deepened, terrible and hers, and she had sold the hallway and now all the rooms were sealed off, intact, unreachable.

She had kept every good memory of her mother and lost the ability to be moved by a single one.

She went back to Tolliver Street. Ozols was eating a sandwich over wax paper and did not look surprised.

"Buy it back," she said.

"Sold Tuesday."

"To who?"

"That's not how it works, and you knew that when you signed." He folded the wax paper into a neat square. "For what it's worth, it went to a man whose own mother is going next month. He wanted to know how it's done. How you stand in the room and don't run."

Mira sat down across from him. Outside, a dryer buzzed and someone swore at it.

"Did she teach me that?" she asked. "In it. Was that in it?"

Ozols looked at her with the mild, unbearable kindness of a man who deals in what people throw away.

"Everyone asks that," he said. "Nobody ever asks before."
