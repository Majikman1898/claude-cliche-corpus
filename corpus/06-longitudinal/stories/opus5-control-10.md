# Exp 06 wave 1 - Opus 5 control story, slot 10
*Model: claude-opus-5 | Written 2026-07-28 | Baseline slot model: sonnet | Premise: A city wakes up one day to discover every person's...*

The message came at 6:14 a.m., though nobody sent it.

Ilse Rahman noticed it first on the 4 train, or rather noticed everyone else's, which she read the way you read a billboard you didn't ask for. The man across from her, gray coat, thermos: *if she looks up I'll pretend I was checking the map.* And then, unspooling beneath it like a second track, *the dog will be fine, Marcy said the dog will be fine, the dog is fourteen.*

It wasn't sound. It wasn't quite sight either. The thoughts occupied a place in the head where you keep the memory of a smell. You couldn't shut them out and you couldn't point at them.

By seven the trains had stopped, because the conductors could not concentrate.

The city responded, at first, the way cities do: with commerce. Someone on Flatbush was selling foil-lined hats by eight-thirty, and the hats did nothing, and people bought them anyway, and the seller knew they did nothing, and everyone could see him knowing it, and they bought them anyway. That was the strange discovery of the first hours — knowing a lie was a lie changed less than you'd expect.

Ilse walked to the school because walking was the only thing that felt like a decision. Along Nostrand, the noise of it. A woman scrubbing a window: *my sister's voice, I hate my sister's voice, I would die for her.* A cop on the corner had gone very still, and around him a bubble of open sidewalk had formed, twelve feet of it, because whatever he was thinking made people veer without deciding to. Ilse veered too. She was not proud of it later.

At the school there were forty-one children in the yard and no adults except Ilse and Ms. Obuya, who taught fourth grade and had come in on a Saturday for reasons neither of them mentioned, since mentioning had become redundant.

The children were fine.

That was the part the news didn't get to for two days. The children were fine. They played a complicated game involving a tennis ball and the fence, and their thoughts poured off them in an unembarrassed racket — *I'm going to be the fastest, I want the red one, I don't like Devon's face today, I love Devon* — and none of it landed as an accusation, because a thing only wounds you if you believe it was meant to be hidden. They had not yet learned that thinking was a private act. Some of them, the youngest, seemed to think this had always been true and the adults were being odd about it.

Ms. Obuya stood beside Ilse at the fence. Ilse could see her thinking *she's going to ask me why I came in,* and Ms. Obuya could see Ilse seeing it, and the whole apparatus of the question collapsed under its own weight, and what was left was just two women at a fence with nothing owed between them.

"My husband left in March," Ms. Obuya said out loud, which was, Ilse understood, a kind of courtesy — the choosing of it, the effort. The words were slower than the thought and clumsier, and that was the gift.

"I know," Ilse said.

"You knew before today."

"Yes."

They watched the ball go against the fence. Ilse was thinking about her mother in the room in Bensonhurst, about the visits she had been shortening for a year, about the particular arithmetic of leaving at four instead of five, and she let it come, and Ms. Obuya's hand arrived on her arm without either of them narrating why.

The condition lasted nine days.

Afterward there were commissions and lawsuits and a great deal of writing about what it had revealed regarding the human character, most of it wrong. What Ilse remembered was not the exposure. Everyone expected the exposure; that was the cheap part, the part any fool could have predicted, the shame and the shouting and the marriages that ended on the third day.

What she remembered was the ninth night, when it thinned out and went, and the city was suddenly full of people who had to say things again. The absolute clumsiness of it. Ten million people relearning the sentence. And how many of them, in that first silence, chose to.
