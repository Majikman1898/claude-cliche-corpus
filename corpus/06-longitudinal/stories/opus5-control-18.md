# Exp 06 wave 1 - Opus 5 control story, slot 18
*Model: claude-opus-5 | Written 2026-07-28 | Baseline slot model: opus | Premise: People can swap memories like possessions...*

The exchange was in a room off Dorset Street, above a shop that sold reconditioned kettles. Naomi had been there four times before, always as a seller.

"You know the terms," said the broker, a heavy woman named Ilsa who wore reading glasses on a chain and had a way of not blinking. "Once it's out, it's out. We don't do returns. People come back crying about it, and I tell them the same thing I'm telling you now."

"I know the terms."

"Say what you're selling."

"August 1998. My father teaching me to swim at Ravensworth. About forty minutes of it. The full thing — the water, him holding my chin up, what he said afterward in the car."

Ilsa wrote it down in a ledger with actual pen and paper, which Naomi had always found reassuring, though she couldn't say why. "What did he say in the car?"

"That's part of what I'm selling."

"I need to know it's not damaged goods. Half these swimming lessons come with a drowning attached."

Naomi looked at the window, which had a crack in it shaped like a river delta. "He said I was brave. He said he'd been afraid of the water until he was twenty-six and he was glad I wouldn't have to carry that."

Ilsa nodded slowly and wrote something else. "That's good stock. That's clean. Four hundred."

"Six."

"Four fifty, and I'm being generous because it has a father in it. Fathers move."

Naomi took the four fifty. The extraction took nine minutes and afterward there was a smooth place where the thing had been, not painful, more like the feeling of a tooth pulled long enough ago that the tongue has stopped searching.

She spent the money on rent and on a coat, and in November she came back.

"Buying this time," she said.

"What are you after?"

"Something with snow in it."

Ilsa brought out the case. This was the part Naomi had never seen from this side — the little glass ampoules with their handwritten labels, each one somebody's afternoon. *Christmas 1974, four children, one dog.* *First snow after moving to Alberta, age 31.* *Sledding, unspecified year, includes broken wrist.*

"That last one's cheap for a reason," Ilsa said.

Naomi picked up the Alberta one. Two hundred and twenty. It came from a woman named or once named — the labels didn't say — and when it went in, it went in sideways, the way they do, arriving already remembered, already old.

She stood in a doorway on Dorset Street and had it: the flat white land, the shock of the cold in her nose, the strange elation of being somewhere with no one who knew her. It was a good memory. It was better than most of hers had been. And it was completely, irreducibly not hers, in the way a beautiful coat bought secondhand still holds the shape of another woman's shoulders.

The trouble came later, the way Ilsa always said it would, though never in the way people expected.

It was March. Naomi was in a swimming pool in Peckham because a friend had a membership and a spare guest pass, and she got into the shallow end and found that her body knew what to do. It knew how to put its face in. It knew how to keep the chin tilted just so.

She stood in four feet of water for a long time and understood that she had sold the wrong half. The memory was gone — she could not see her father's hands, could not hear whatever he'd said in the car, had no image of Ravensworth at all except the fact of its name in a ledger. But the swimming had stayed. The bravery had stayed. It was in her arms.

She swam a length badly and got out and sat on the tiled edge, dripping, thinking about the woman in Alberta who was somewhere right now not remembering snow, and who probably still, without knowing why, felt something lift in her chest when the temperature dropped.

That was the thing nobody put in the ledger. You could sell what you remembered. You couldn't sell what it had made of you. The memories went out into the world and got worn by strangers, and meanwhile every person walked around shaped by a thousand afternoons they'd sold off years ago, kind for reasons they'd forgotten, afraid of water they'd never seen.

Naomi dried off. She kept the guest pass.
