The kiosk at the Fortuna Street station had been dispensing scratch tickets for eleven years, and in that time it had produced exactly one winner. Mrs. Adeyemi knew this because she had bought a ticket every Tuesday for eleven years and the winner had been hers: forty dollars, 2019, spent on a coat.

She was buying her Tuesday ticket when the man in the gray anorak stepped up beside her, and she felt something go out of her the way heat goes out of an open door.

It was not painful. That was what she would tell people afterward, when nobody believed her. It was only that the world got slightly harder. Her key, which had always turned in her front door on the first try, needed three tries that evening. The bus she never missed pulled away as she reached for it. Her ticket, when she scratched it, showed three lemons and a cherry — not a loss, exactly, but a shape that in eleven years she had never once seen, the shape of a machine that had stopped bothering to pretend.

The man in the anorak was named Vessel, or that was what he called himself, and he had begun small. A pinch from a bookmaker. A skim from a casino floor, where the luck lay thick as grease and nobody noticed a little missing. He had discovered the trick at thirty-one, in a hospital corridor, waiting on a scan that came back clean when it had no business coming back clean. He had felt the borrowed weight of it settle into him and understood, with the clarity of a man reading his own name, that it had come from somewhere. That someone, elsewhere, had received worse news than they had earned.

Nothing is made, Vessel thought. Everything is moved.

So he moved it.

By the second year he could walk a subway platform and come off it swollen. The effects were dispersed, deniable — a spike in near-misses, a bad month for a neighborhood, an inexplicable cluster of dropped catches on a Sunday league pitch. The actuaries noticed before the mystics did. Somewhere in a beige office, a woman named Priya Raghunathan was writing a memo about anomalous tail risk that nobody would read for another nine months.

What Vessel did with the luck was the part nobody could have guessed. He did not gamble. He did not buy lottery tickets or court women or angle for promotions. He rented a room above a laundromat and sat in it and did nothing at all, and this was because he had learned that luck, once taken, does not stay. It seeps. It wants to be elsewhere. Holding it was like holding a fistful of seawater, and every hour it drained out of him, back into the world, but *scattered* — dispersed evenly across seven billion people, thinned to nothing, worthless.

He was not a thief so much as a leak.

He had done the arithmetic. He had been doing it for years, on the backs of envelopes. Every gram he pulled from Fortuna Street would be back in the world within a day, but it would be *everywhere*, and everywhere is the same as nowhere. The luck of a woman who bought a Tuesday ticket for eleven years was concentrated, was *hers*, was a real thing with a shape. Redistributed, it became a millionth of a millionth of an increment in the odds of a stranger in Manila not stubbing his toe.

Vessel understood this. He kept doing it anyway. That was the part he could not explain, not even to himself, on the nights when the room above the laundromat filled up with the smell of other people's clean clothes.

Mrs. Adeyemi came looking for him in the fourth year. She had followed the thinning the way you follow a draft to a broken window: bad Tuesdays, then bad streets, then a bad building. She climbed the stairs and knocked and he opened the door, gray anorak, unremarkable face, and she saw at once that he was not swollen with anything. He was the emptiest man she had ever seen.

"Give it back," she said.

"I can't." He stood aside to let her in, which surprised them both. "It doesn't come back. It only spreads."

She looked past him at the bare room, the envelopes, the arithmetic.

"Then why?"

Vessel considered the question for a long moment, as though it had never occurred to him that it might have an answer.

"Because it was never fair," he said, "that anyone had any."
