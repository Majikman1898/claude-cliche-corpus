The mountain was called Threshold, and it had been in the valley for nine hundred and ninety-one years.

Ilse's grandmother had taught her to measure it. Not the height — anyone could do that with a stick and some arithmetic — but the lean. Every autumn, on the first morning of frost, you drove a brass pin into the stone of the old chapel step and sighted along it to the notch on Threshold's eastern shoulder. You wrote down the number. That was all. The Kessler women had been writing down the number since before the chapel had a roof.

The numbers went in a book, and the book lived in a drawer, and the drawer smelled of tallow and mouse.

"It's not walking," said Bertrand, who ran the mill and considered himself a rational man. "Mountains don't walk. What you're measuring is your own eyes getting old."

"My eyes are twenty-six," said Ilse.

"Your grandmother's weren't."

She let him have it. It was easier than explaining that the lean had been consistent across four generations of eyes, that it accelerated in a curve you could plot, that in the last decade the notch had crept a full thumb's width against the pin and the creep was getting faster. Bertrand did not want a curve. He wanted flour.

But others came. The old ones, mostly, who had grown up with the story the way children grow up with a scar they don't remember getting. They came to the drawer and asked her to read the numbers aloud, and she did, and they nodded, and they went home and began the slow quiet work of deciding what could be carried.

In the spring of the nine hundred ninety-eighth year, the wells changed. Not dried — changed. The water came up cold and tasting of iron, and in the deep shaft behind the tannery it came up warm. Ilse climbed the eastern slope with a rope and a lamp and found what her grandmother's book had described in a hand grown shaky: the seams. Long horizontal fissures in the rock, packed with a grey clay that was not clay, that flexed under her thumb like the sole of a boot.

She sat with her hand pressed to it for a long time. It was not warm. It was not cold. It was simply not stone, and she understood, in a way that had nothing to do with the numbers, that the mountain was not a place. It was a thing that had stopped in a place, the way a person stops on a road to rest, and had been resting for as long as anyone had bothered to look.

The village argued for two years. This was reasonable. Threshold sheltered them from the north wind; its runoff filled their cisterns; its shadow set the hours of their working day. Some said the movement was so slow that a house could be jacked and rolled a handspan a year and never miss a harvest. Some said the movement would not stay slow — that a thing which had gathered a thousand years of intention would not spend it politely.

Ilse did not argue. She measured. She made copies of the book. She taught the brass-pin method to eleven children, including three from the town downriver who thought it was a game, and she made each of them recite the numbers back until the numbers lived in them.

In the autumn of the thousandth year, the frost came early. She drove the pin. She sighted. The notch had moved not a thumb's width but a hand's, in a single season, and along the base of the mountain the ground had begun to fold like cloth pushed across a table.

She wrote the number down. Then she closed the book, carried it out into the square, and rang the chapel bell — not the alarm pattern, which would have caused panic, but the slow tolling used for weddings and departures, which meant only: *come, and bring what you meant to bring.*

They went east, toward the sea, in no great hurry, in a line four miles long.

Behind them, over the following eleven years, Threshold left the valley. It took the chapel with it, and the mill, and the drawer that smelled of tallow. It went south-southwest at a pace no one alive would have called a pace, and it did not look back, because it had no way of doing so, and because there was nothing in the gesture that would have been useful to it.

The children downriver kept driving pins.
