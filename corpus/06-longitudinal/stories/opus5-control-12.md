The lamp came first, on the morning after Ines dreamed of her grandmother's kitchen. She woke with the smell of scorched milk still in her sinuses and found, on the pillow beside her, a small brass oil lamp with a wick that had clearly burned.

She was thirty-one, and this had never happened before, and she assumed for most of that first day that someone had broken in.

By the end of the week she had a chair with one leg shorter than the others, a fistful of unfamiliar coins, a swimming medal engraved for a meet she had not attended, and a live moth in a jar, beating itself against the glass. The moth she let out the window. The rest she stacked in the corner of the bedroom, because what else do you do.

She read about it later, once she started looking. It had begun in March, all over, at slightly different hours depending on longitude. Nobody had explained it. There were physicists and there were priests and there were people selling drawer organizers, and none of them had explained it. Every night the world made a little more of itself, and every morning there was more to carry.

The economics went strange fast. A woman in Osaka dreamed a violin and it was a Stradivarius, or near enough that the auction house wept. Governments tried to induce productive dreaming and produced instead an enormous quantity of anxious paperwork and half-remembered corridors. Landfills filled with objects that had never been manufactured. There was talk of a heat problem, a mass problem, the universe quietly running a tab.

Ines mostly thought about her apartment.

It was small. She had known it was small when she signed the lease, but that had been a private knowledge, negotiable, and now it was a fact enforced nightly. The stack in the corner became a wall. The wall became a second room she could not enter. She sold what could be sold — the coins were Ottoman and worth something, the medal was worth nothing — and gave away what could be given, and still the bedroom shrank around her like a fist closing slowly over the course of a year.

The trouble was that she could not stop, and she could not choose. She tried, some nights, lying rigid, thinking hard about flat things, thin things, things that stack. She got a bicycle. She got a door with no frame, which she leaned against the wall and which stood there for months looking like an accusation.

What she wanted, and never got, was her father.

He had died in February, a month before the world began emptying its pockets, and she dreamed of him constantly. They were not good dreams. In them he was usually busy, or on a train, or telling her something important through a closed window. And every morning she woke and looked and found a hat, a receipt, a set of keys to a car he'd sold in 2004, once a single black glove that fit neither of her hands and had a hole worn through the thumb exactly where he wore his through.

Never him. Never even a photograph.

She kept those things separately. Not in the wall — in a box under the bed, which was itself an act of category, of deciding what the world owed her and what it merely gave. The glove she kept on top.

In the second year she moved out to her mother's, where there was a barn.

Her mother had been dreaming of horses and receiving horseshoes, hundreds of them, rusted, of no use to anyone, and had taken to nailing them over the doorways for luck, until the house was so lucky it groaned. They sat in the kitchen and drank tea and did not mention that in one of them the barn was filling and in the other the barn was filling, and both barns were the same barn.

"It's not so bad," her mother said. "It's proof you were somewhere."

Ines thought about that for a long time afterward. It was the only explanation she ever heard that she could stand, and it wasn't one — it was just a way of holding it.

That night she dreamed of nothing she could recall, and woke to find a smooth grey stone, the kind you'd pick up on a beach and put in your pocket and forget about, and turn up years later, and have absolutely no idea where it came from.

She put it in the box. It was, she decided, the correct box.
