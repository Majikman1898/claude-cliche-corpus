The mountain's name, in the old survey records, was Hesper Dome. It sat in the northeast for eleven hundred years, and then it began to move.

Nobody agreed on the exact day. The Geological Service later placed the start at some point in early March, based on the rate of drift and where the anchoring shale had cracked. But the people in the valley towns knew earlier than that, because their dogs stopped sleeping and their wells went sour, and because Alma Reyes, who had lived at the foot of the north slope for eighty-one years, walked out one morning and said the shape of the sky was wrong.

It moved about four inches an hour. Slower in the mornings.

The first year was mostly panic, and then mostly paperwork. Three towns were relocated. Two highways were cut, rerouted, and then cut again when the projections were revised. A tent city of scientists sprang up at the trailing edge, where the earth closed behind the mountain like water behind a hull, and they drilled and sampled and argued about whether there was anything down there doing the moving or whether the moving was simply something the mountain did, the way water freezes, the way a seed splits.

Nobody found anything. There was granite, and then more granite, and beneath that the hot patient dark that is beneath everything.

By the fourth year it had become ordinary. A woman in Kettle Springs opened a diner at the leading edge and moved it twice a season on skids. Children on field trips were photographed against the enormous slow face of it, laughing, holding out their palms as though pushing. Cattle grazed on the flanks and were driven off in wide unhurried arcs. The trailing edge became a tourist attraction — a raw wound of upturned soil three miles across, steaming faintly in cold weather, full of things that had not seen light: fossilized shells, a Spanish stirrup, a rusted 1954 Chevrolet that had been lost and was now found, folded almost gently into the shape of a leaf.

Alma Reyes did not relocate. She was given a stipend and a place in a town called New Halloway and she went there for eleven days and then came back and sat in a lawn chair on the ridge above her old house, watching the mountain go.

Her granddaughter Beatriz drove up on weekends. They didn't talk much. The mountain made talking feel small.

"It'll be past the river by winter," Beatriz said once.

"Mm."

"They say it might reach the coast. Not for eight hundred years. But eventually."

Alma thought about that. Eight hundred years was a length of time she had no use for, and yet here she was, sitting in its path, sharing an afternoon with it.

"It isn't going anywhere," she said.

"It's going west."

"That's not the same as going anywhere."

Beatriz didn't understand that until much later, in a hospital corridor, waiting.

The mountain crossed the continent over the following centuries. It shouldered aside a small city that had refused, on principle, to move. It buried a war memorial and, four decades later, set it down again on the far side, tilted but intact, the names still legible. It changed a river's mind. It made a lake and then unmade it. In the state archives there are seventeen thousand photographs of it, from every angle, in every season, and if you flip through them fast the mountain does not appear to move at all — only the things around it, the roads and the trees and the fashions and the faces, flickering and rearranging like weather.

It reached the sea in the year the last person who remembered Alma Reyes died.

It did not stop at the water. It walked in, and kept walking, and for eleven years there was an island where no island had been, and gulls nested on it, and then there wasn't, and the water closed over the summit the way it closes over everything.

Then the deep dark, and the long crossing, and somewhere on the far continent, in nine hundred years or so, a morning when the dogs won't sleep.
