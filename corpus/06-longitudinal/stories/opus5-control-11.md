The bureau of luck accounting occupied the ninth floor of a building that had never won anything.

Ilse Marchetti had worked there eleven years, long enough to know the sound of the ledger before she saw it. Luck moved like weather across the continents, high-pressure systems of fortune drifting from Kerala to Lisbon, a low forming over the American Midwest. It sloshed. It never left. That was the first law: the total was fixed at creation and could only be redistributed, never made or unmade. A woman winning a lottery in Osaka meant a hundred thousand near-misses elsewhere, spread so thin nobody felt the theft.

In March the numbers began to bleed.

She noticed it in the coincidence rate first — the ordinary background hum of the world, the reunited friends, the found keys, the bus arriving as you rounded the corner. Down four percent globally. Then six. Then eleven. Meanwhile no region gained. The luck was simply going somewhere the instruments could not follow, the way water leaves a bathtub with an imperfect seal.

"A leak," said her supervisor, Odum, who had a policy of preferring explanations that required no action.

"Leaks go somewhere," Ilse said. "Show me the somewhere."

They gave her the deep archive and a junior named Teodor who chewed his cuticles. Together they built a map of absences. Every unlikely disaster of the past eighteen months, every improbable failure — the three unrelated bridge collapses, the vaccine batch that spoiled in four countries at once, the fire that took the seed vault in Svalbard's shadow. Plot them and they made a rough torus, a doughnut of misfortune with a hole at its center. Something at that center was drinking.

The center was a house in the Cévennes.

She went alone, because she was fifty-eight and had learned that the useful things happen to people who go alone. The house was small, stone, roses climbing the south face in a season roses had no business climbing. Inside, everything worked. That was the thing that frightened her — not opulence, but function. Every hinge silent. Every fruit in the bowl perfect. A clock that had not lost a second since 1908.

The man who opened the door was ordinary in the specific way of people who have arranged never to be looked at twice.

"You're from the ninth floor," he said. "I wondered when."

"You're stealing it."

"I'm holding it." He gestured her in, and the chair received her back like it had been built from a mold of her spine. "Sit. You'll want to hear the arithmetic, you people always do."

His name had been Aurel. He'd been a hydrologist. Twenty years ago his daughter had drowned in a river he had personally certified safe — a one-in-nine-thousand confluence of rain and a failed gauge and a boy who could not swim well enough to reach her.

"One in nine thousand," he repeated. "Do you understand? The universe reached into its pocket for a number that improbable and spent it on my daughter. So I thought: it's a currency, then. And currencies can be held."

"Held for what?"

"Until it's safe." He looked past her at the roses. "I'm building a reservoir. When it's full — truly full, all of it — nothing unlikely will ever happen anywhere again. No lightning finding the one man in the field. No tumor in the child of two healthy parents. No river. The world will run on the mean. Boring, and merciful."

Ilse thought of the coincidence rate. The bus arriving. The friend on the far platform, turning.

"And nothing good will happen either."

"Nothing good happens," Aurel said, with terrible patience. "Things merely fail to go wrong, and we congratulate ourselves."

She did not argue with him. She had learned, in eleven years, that you cannot argue a man out of a ledger he balanced with his own child. Instead she asked to see the reservoir, and he took her down to the cellar where it hung in the dark, vast and quiet and shivering — every unspent kindness of a decade, every rescue that had not come.

She had brought nothing. No tools, no authority, no plan.

But the latch on the cellar door had been oiled to perfection, and it swung shut behind them without a sound, and in a house where nothing unlikely could occur, no one would ever come looking.
