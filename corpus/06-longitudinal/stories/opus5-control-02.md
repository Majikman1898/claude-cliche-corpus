The lamp came first, on the morning after Mara's mother died.

It was a brass thing with a green glass shade, sitting on the kitchen floor beside the refrigerator where nothing had been the night before. She recognized it before she remembered dreaming it: the lamp from the hall of the house in Ellsworth, the one her mother read under, the one that had been sold at an estate sale in 1998 for eleven dollars.

She did not tell anyone. This was the first mistake, or possibly the only correct decision she ever made.

The objects came every night after that. A dinner plate with a hairline crack. A blue hair elastic. Half a bicycle, sheared clean down the seat tube, the pedal still turning slightly when she found it in the bathtub. Some mornings the object was small enough to fit in her palm — a pill, a stone, a molar. Some mornings she had to open the front door and stand in the yard and reckon with a thing the size of a boat.

She learned that other people had this too, once she started paying attention. It was in the way strangers apologized for the smell of chlorine on them, or carried a folding table nobody had asked them to bring. The city had a whole department for it. Two men in gray came with a truck, on Tuesdays, and took away whatever you left at the curb, and never once asked what any of it was. That was the mercy of the arrangement. Nobody asked.

But there were people who kept them. Mara met one at a party, a man named Devlin who had a storage unit in Revere and drove out to it on Sundays. He described it the way other men describe boats.

"Fourteen years," he said. "Every single one."

"What's it like?"

"Loud," he said, and laughed, and did not explain.

She thought about that for a long time. She had been leaving hers at the curb — the plates, the elastic, the enormous folded map of a city with no name, the sixty-three identical teacups from the week she had the flu. Every Tuesday the gray truck took them. And every Tuesday she felt something in her chest go a little quieter, a little further away, and she had assumed that was healing.

In March she dreamed of nothing she could remember, and woke to find a small wooden box on the pillow beside her, warm as bread.

Inside was a note in her mother's handwriting. It said: *Don't forget to eat something.*

She read it eleven times. Then she put it back in the box and put the box in the closet, and did not put it at the curb, and that Tuesday she stood at the window watching the gray truck take the rest and felt, for the first time in months, something like a hand on the back of her neck.

She started keeping them after that. Not all — she was not Devlin, she had a one-bedroom — but the ones that came with weight. She built a shelf. The lamp came back to her, or a lamp like it, and she plugged it in and it worked, and she read under it at night the way her mother had, and understood that she was dreaming forward as much as back.

In the fall she met a woman named June who worked at a bakery and had, on her forearm, a small tattoo of a key.

"That's a strange thing to get," Mara said.

"It's not a tattoo," June said. "It's a dream. Some of them come out on you instead of on the floor. Nobody knows why."

"What does it open?"

June looked at her with an expression Mara would spend the next several years trying to describe to herself, and failing.

"I've been waiting eleven years to find out," she said. "But I keep the door."

They were married the following spring. On the morning after the wedding Mara woke to find, on the floor at the foot of the bed, an ordinary door, unattached to any wall, standing upright and perfectly balanced, with a lock in it.

June was already awake, sitting up, holding her own arm, saying nothing.

Neither of them moved for a long time. Outside, it was Tuesday, and the gray truck was coming up the street, and they let it pass.
