# Exp 06 wave 1 - Opus 5 control story, slot 9
*Model: claude-opus-5 | Written 2026-07-28 | Baseline slot model: sonnet | Premise: A mysterious train arrives once every decade, stop...*

The last train came through Alder Hollow on a Tuesday, which nobody expected, because the last time it had been a Sunday, and the time before that a Sunday too, and people had built a whole theology on that.

My grandmother saw the 1976 train. She was nineteen and stood at the crossing with her shoes in her hand, and she did not board, and she talked about it for the next forty-four years the way other people talk about a war they were too young for.

The town isn't on any map. I've checked. I've checked the county surveys, the plat books, the satellite imagery where our four hundred acres of houses and grain elevator show up as a smear of unresolved green. The mail comes anyway. The mail always comes. But the train comes only every ten years, and it comes with its own weather — a sort of pressure behind the eyes, a taste like a struck match — and it stops where there is no station, only a length of rail running out of the corn and back into it.

I was thirty-two the Tuesday it came. I had a truck with a bad alternator and a mother in the ground and a job at the elevator that I could describe completely in one sentence.

Half the town walked out to the crossing. That's tradition. You go and you look and you don't board, and then you go home feeling like you've been faithful to something.

The cars were the color of old nickels. No markings. The windows weren't dark exactly — they were full, the way a glass of water is full, and looking into one was like trying to read through a page held too close.

The door opened. It didn't make a sound doing it.

Delia Ferris boarded. Everyone gasped, and I remember thinking how stupid that was, how we'd all come out there specifically to see whether anyone would, and then acted scandalized when someone did. She was sixty-one and had run the pharmacy since her husband died, and she went up the steps without her purse, which is the detail that people still can't get over. She left her purse on the gravel.

Tom Vasquez boarded. The Aldritch twins boarded, holding hands, which their mother screamed about for an hour afterward and then stopped screaming about very suddenly, as though she'd remembered something.

And I stood there with my hands in my jacket, doing arithmetic. Forty-two at the next one. Fifty-two after that. There was a version of my life where I stood at this crossing six more times and then didn't, because I'd be in the ground next to my mother, and the arithmetic of that was not complicated, and I did not board.

I want to be honest about why. It wasn't fear of where it went. It was that I'd have had to come back.

Because they do come back. That's the thing people outside would never understand, if there were people outside. Ten years later the train stops again and the door opens and they step down, and they are not older, and they are not younger, and they are not — this is the part I can't say right — they are not *fewer*. They're more. There's more of them than there was. Delia Ferris came down those steps in 2020 and she was still sixty-one and still Delia and she went back to the pharmacy the next morning, and every single person in this town has since found some reason to go in and buy something they don't need, just to stand near her for a minute.

You can't ask them. They'll answer, and the answer will be in words you know, and it will slide off you like water off oilcloth. Tom Vasquez told me it was "mostly a matter of getting the proportions right." He was crying when he said it. He looked happy.

I'm forty-two now. I've got two years.

I've started leaving things where they'll be found. I've been walking out to the crossing in the evenings, standing on the rail, feeling for anything in the metal. Nothing yet. But the corn goes quiet out there in a way it doesn't anywhere else, and I have begun to think that quiet is not absence.

I'm going to board.

I'm telling you now, while I still know how to say things the ordinary way. While I'm still exactly this much of a person and no more.
