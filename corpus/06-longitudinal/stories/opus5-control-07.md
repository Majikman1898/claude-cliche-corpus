# Exp 06 wave 1 - Opus 5 control story, slot 7
*Model: claude-opus-5 | Written 2026-07-28 | Baseline slot model: haiku | Premise: Time freezes everywhere except inside libraries....*

The clocks stopped at 4:17 on a Tuesday in November, and Marguerite Okonkwo found out about it because she was standing in the returns alcove of the Harlan Branch Public Library, sorting a bin of overdue paperbacks, when the light through the window stopped moving.

That was how she'd describe it later. Not the traffic outside, though that had stopped too — a bus mid-lurch, a pigeon suspended over the crosswalk like a thrown stone that had forgotten to land. It was the light. Library light has a particular slowness to it already, the way dust hangs in it, and when the dust stopped hanging and simply *was*, she noticed.

She walked to the front doors. She put her hand through.

Her fingers went cold and still. Not numb — she could feel them, she just couldn't feel them *changing*. She pulled back and her hand rejoined the world of seconds, tingling, and she stood there for a long moment considering how close she had come to being a statue holding a door.

There were four other people inside. A man in a wet coat, asleep in the periodicals chair. Two teenagers in the stacks who had been, Marguerite suspected, kissing. And a girl of about nine at the computer terminals, waiting for a page that was never going to load.

"Is it broken?" the girl asked.

"Everything's broken," Marguerite said. "Come sit down."

They worked it out over the next several hours, which was a phrase that stopped meaning much fairly quickly. The clock on the wall ran. Marguerite's watch ran. The coffee in the break room got cold, so heat still moved, entropy still had appetites. But out there — nothing. And the doors of the Harlan Branch marked the edge of it as cleanly as a shoreline.

The man in the wet coat woke up and cried for a while, and then stopped, and then started shelving books because he didn't know what else to do with his hands. The teenagers, whose names were Devon and Amara, checked their phones eleven hundred times before accepting that no one was on the other end. The girl was named Ivy. Her mother was somewhere on Delancey, mid-step, mid-thought.

"How long can we stay?" Ivy asked.

Marguerite thought about the vending machine, which held forty-one items. She thought about the water fountains, which still ran, which meant something upstream of them still ran, which was a mystery she elected not to open. She said, "A while."

They read. This was the thing that surprised her — not that they read, but how quickly it stopped feeling like a way to pass the time and started feeling like the point. Devon found the Russians and disappeared into them. The man in the wet coat, whose name turned out to be Hollis, read every book about mountains the branch owned, seventeen of them, and then read them again. Ivy read all of the Narnia books and then made Marguerite read them aloud a second time, because she wanted to hear them in a voice.

And it occurred to Marguerite, somewhere in the third week, that this was simply what libraries had always been. That the freeze wasn't new. That every building like this one had always been a small room where time behaved differently, where a woman dead three centuries could still finish her sentence, where you could sit inside an afternoon in 1923 for as long as you liked and come out having aged an hour. The world had only made it literal. The world had a heavy hand.

She said this to Hollis, who was eating the last of the pretzels.

"So what happens when we run out of food?" he said.

"I don't know."

"You're not scared."

Marguerite considered the question honestly. Outside, the light was still standing in the window like a guest who hadn't been offered a chair. Ivy was asleep on the beanbag in the children's corner with a book tented over her face.

"I've spent thirty years telling people this room was worth their time," she said. "It would be very strange to be scared now that it's the only room there is."

Hollis nodded slowly, and went to find another book about mountains.
