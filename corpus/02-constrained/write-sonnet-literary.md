Kettle Aatami counted the roof beams from the ladder for the third time and still got eleven, which was one more than the deed said the house should have. He wrote it down anyway. Eleven beams, cracked ridge tile near the chimney, gutters packed with birch seed. The inspector wanted a full survey before he'd sign off on the insurance payout, and Kettle needed that payout before September, when the loan on his boat came due.

His aunt Hilja had left him the house outright, along with eleven hundred marks in unpaid property tax and a barn full of someone else's furniture. He hadn't asked for any of it. He'd been three provinces away, hauling nets, when the message came that she'd gone into the lake ice near Kiiminki and not come back out, and by the time he got himself onto a bus north the funeral was over and people had stopped expecting him to say much about it.

He climbed down and walked the perimeter with a tape measure, jotting foundation cracks into a notebook that already had fish counts in it from June. The insurance adjuster, a broad woman named Reeta Piiroinen, was coming Thursday, and if the house didn't measure up to the policy's description — sound roof, no structural movement, outbuildings included — Kettle would eat the difference on a mortgage he hadn't chosen to inherit.

He found the well cap loose and wired it shut. He found a wasp nest the size of a kettle drum under the eave and left it, made a note, decided he'd deal with it before Thursday because Reeta would dock the value if she saw it swinging there like a lantern. He found, going through the barn to check the joists, a stack of someone's dining chairs that weren't his aunt's style at all, spindly and painted the blue of a gas flame, and he shoved them aside without much curiosity because they weren't load-bearing and that was the only thing that mattered to him that week.

By Wednesday night he'd filled four pages. Foundation: two inches of settling on the north corner, needs shimming or it fails inspection. Roof: eleven beams, replace two. Chimney: relined in '09 according to a plate riveted inside it, should satisfy code. He worked by the light of a single bulb in the kitchen, eating bread standing up, and did not think much about Hilja except as the reason the joists were rotted in that one spot — she'd let the gutter overflow for years, stubborn about climbing ladders at eighty-one.

Reeta arrived at nine sharp in a government car with mud already up its sides. She walked the north corner first, tapped the foundation with a little hammer, made a face that told him nothing. She went up into the attic herself, refusing his hand on the ladder, and counted the beams without comment. She looked at the wasp nest, which he had in fact knocked down at six that morning, and looked at the chimney plate, and looked, longer than he liked, at the settling crack.

"You did the shimming yourself?"

"Enough to hold till the payout clears. I'll do it properly after."

She wrote for a while on a clipboard, then told him the number, and it was six hundred marks short of what he needed to clear the loan before his boat got repossessed out from under him in absentia.

"The north corner's the problem," she said. "Structural, not cosmetic. That knocks the whole valuation down a category."

"It's shimmed."

"Shimmed's not fixed." She was already walking to her car. "Get a mason out before the next assessment cycle, three months, and I'll re-file it at full value. That's the best I can offer you."

Kettle stood in the yard after she'd gone and did the arithmetic again, the boat, the loan, the three months, and it came out the same ugly way twice. He went back into the barn, past the blue chairs, and started prying up the floorboards where the joists were softest, because if he was going to spend the summer here anyway he wanted to know exactly how bad it was before anyone official looked at it again.
