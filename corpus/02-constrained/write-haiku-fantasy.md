The baker's daughter had charred the loaves again, and Korvin watched the apprentice healer apologize for the third time that week. Ostara, the village's proper mage, had crafted a preservation charm on the ovens years ago—a simple binding that kept heat even when the fire went low. It should have lasted decades more.

"It wasn't me," Ostara kept saying, turning her weathered hands over, as if the problem lived in her palms. "I felt nothing break. There's no tear in the work."

Korvin had been mixing herbs in the back room when he noticed it: a wrongness he had no words for. Not a sound, not a smell. Something had been there and then wasn't. He'd looked up from the mortar and the sensation clarified into something like hunger satisfied, like waking from a dream where he'd been drowning. His chest contracted. His teeth felt cold.

He said nothing. Nobody gave it much thought. A charm failed. Charms failed. Ostara mixed a new binding while the baker grumbled about coin spent on flour-dust.

But three days later, Korvin felt it again.

The farrier's wife was wearing an amulet against joint pain—a common thing, blessed by Ostara each spring. Korvin was helping repair the smithy's chimney when the woman complained the amulet had stopped working. She unwound it from her wrist and looked disappointed. And there, in that same moment, Korvin felt the presence and then the absence. Something vanishing from the world that wasn't a sound or a sight. It was intimate, like losing a word on the tip of his tongue but in reverse.

He sat very still on the ladder, hands braced on soot-stained stone.

The third time it happened, he was standing near the village well where children gathered before school. He felt the gentle magic keeping the water clean fraying at its edges like a rope beginning to fray. Korvin closed his eyes and—not deliberately, but naturally, like breathing—he reached into that sensation. The wrongness became something he could touch. It tasted like static in his mouth, sharp and bright. He pulled it closer to himself, and the magic dissolved completely.

The well's water went murky by afternoon.

That night, Korvin did not go to Ostara. Instead, he walked to the forest line where the boundary stones sat half-buried in moss. He knew what he'd done: he'd unraveled the protection charm that circled the village. He knew why it was forbidden. The boundary stones kept worse things at bay—ancient contracts, agreements his grandmother's grandmother had helped forge with powers that didn't age or forget.

He could still feel the magic's structure in his chest, a knot of power that wouldn't close or release. It sat there humming, demanding to be consumed.

Korvin thought of Ostara, exhausted and confused, checking her work for flaws that didn't exist. He thought of all the people maintaining these spells without question because the elder mage told them to, because this was the cost of safety, the price of keeping the wild at bay.

He walked deeper into the forest. The boundary stones receded behind him.

When the wolves came that winter, when the sickness crept in from the wild places, when the village finally called for wandering mages to reinforce the borders at great expense—Korvin was already gone. He'd learned to swallow magic without burning. He'd learned that consumed spells left nothing behind to trace.

There were older powers in the deep woods. Forgotten things. They'd been patient a very long time, and someone had finally broken the lock.
