The tide pool at the end of the pier had a crack in its concrete lip, and Bittor Etxeberria was on his knees with a jar, trying to coax a stranded blenny back toward the water before the sun did the fish in. He didn't hear the bicycle. He heard the brakes, then the specific sound of a front wheel hitting the loose bollard chain, then a body meeting the boards a few feet from his hand.

The cyclist got up fast, the way people do when they're more embarrassed than hurt. She was maybe thirty, with a courier bag and a scraped palm she was already blotting on her jeans.

"You left the chain down," she said.

"I didn't touch the chain."

"Someone left it down." She looked at the bollard, then at him, then at the jar. "Is that a fish?"

"It was going to die in there." He tipped the blenny into the shallows. It flicked once and was gone. When he stood, his knees cracked, and he saw that her front wheel had gone out of true against the bollard. The rim wobbled visibly.

"That's my brake pad now grinding," she said. "That wheel's from a shop in Donostia. There is no shop here."

"There's Kepa's."

"Kepa fixes engines."

"Kepa fixes everything if you bring him coffee." Bittor said it to be helpful, and she heard it as a man deciding her problem was small. Her jaw set. She wheeled the bike away without another word, the rim ticking against the pad with every rotation, a sound that followed her the length of the pier.

He was the harbormaster's nephew, in town for a season of counting nets and mending the things the harbormaster's arthritis wouldn't let him mend. She was named Garazi, he learned two days later, because the pension where she was staying was the same pension where he ate breakfast, and the owner said it across the room without meaning to introduce them. Garazi did not look up from her map.

The wheel became the thing between them. He'd cost her a day, in her accounting — she was cycling the coast on a schedule tied to a ferry booking, and the ferry did not care about a bent rim. Kepa had ordered the part. The part was coming from Bilbao on a truck that ran twice a week. Until then she was stuck in a town of four hundred people, one of whom she blamed.

Bittor could have let it sit. Instead he did the stupid, obvious thing: he went to the bollard and found that the chain latch was broken, the pin sheared clean, so that the chain could not in fact be raised at all. He photographed it. He found her at the pension the next morning and put his phone on the table beside her coffee.

"I didn't leave it down," he said. "It doesn't go up. It's been broken maybe a year. I wanted you to see."

She looked at the photo for a long moment. "So it was nobody."

"It was a broken pin."

"That's worse, somehow." But something in her shoulders came down. She turned the phone to look at the timestamp, then at him. "You went back to check."

"I don't like being told I did a thing I didn't do."

"No," she said. "Nobody does." She pushed the second chair out from the table with her foot, which was not an apology and not forgiveness, but was a chair.

The part came on the Thursday truck. Kepa trued the wheel in his oil-smelling shed while Garazi held the coffee she'd been told to bring, and Bittor stood in the doorway pretending to be useful. When the wheel spun clean and silent, she paid Kepa and did not immediately leave.

"The ferry's gone," she said. "The one I booked. There's another Sunday."

"I know the man who runs the Sunday one."

"Of course you do." She swung her leg over the bike, testing the pedal, the rim humming true now under her weight. "Show me the tide pools before then. The ones that don't have fish dying in them. I want to see if there's anything you didn't already rescue."

He was still working out whether that was a joke when she rode off toward the water, no hands for a second, both arms out, and turned at the end of the pier to see if he was following.
