The moss-scraper snapped in her grip, and Belen swore under her breath. Half the ledger tiles behind the shrine were furred over again, which meant another morning on her knees with a dull blade while the older women pretended not to watch. She was seventeen and still doing the child's task, because her mother had bartered her into the shrine's keeping when the fever took the goats, and a girl with no dowry scraped moss until someone decided otherwise.

The tiles recorded debts. That was the whole purpose of the shrine in Otxandio: not gods, but grudges. Who owed whom a cartload of turnips, whose grandfather had never repaid the loan of an ox. The village kept its accounts in stone so no one could argue them. Belen had scraped these names so many times she knew the debts better than the debtors did.

She pressed her thumb against a tile to steady it, and the carving moved.

Not the stone. The letters. Under her skin the grooves flexed like tendons, and where she pushed, the debt rearranged itself. IZAR OWES THE MILL FOUR SACKS became IZAR OWES THE MILL ONE SACK, the numeral resettling as if it had always been there, the moss-shadow around it undisturbed.

She lifted her thumb. Her heart went sideways. She looked at her hand as though it belonged to someone drowned.

Anyone who could edit the debt-stones could unmake the village. Every quarrel in Otxandio ran back to these tiles; the elders swore oaths on them; a marriage last spring had been settled by what one said. The penalty for defacing a tile was to be carried past the boundary stones and left there. She had watched it happen to a man named Gorka when she was nine. Nobody spoke his name afterward. The stones didn't record him because he had ceased, in the village's arithmetic, to exist.

So she told no one. That was the first thing, and it stayed the only thing for eleven days.

She practiced at night, when the shrine was hers to tend. She learned that the letters answered only to her bare skin, that they resisted like cold tallow and set fast, that she could not add a tile that had never been carved, only bend what was already sworn. She was careful. She changed nothing that mattered. She thinned a debt her own family owed the tanner by a single hide, then put it back, sickened at how easy it was to want more.

On the twelfth night she found she was not the first.

The tile for the widow Amaia's field had been altered before Belen ever touched it — the grooves under her thumb held a memory of two shapes, the true one and the lie laid over it, and the lie was old, older than Belen's keeping. Someone had done this and walked away and let the widow lose her acre by a debt she never carried. The village had believed the stone because the stone could not lie. Belen sat back on her heels in the dark and understood that the shrine had never recorded the truth. It had only recorded whoever last had hands like hers.

She could fix it. She put her thumb to Amaia's tile and felt the false debt soften, ready to go.

And then she thought about the older women who watched her scrape, and how one of them was old enough, and had hands, and had never once had to prove where the widow's acre went. She lifted her thumb away without changing anything. If she corrected it, she confessed. If she confessed, the boundary stones. If she stayed silent, she became the next set of hands that let it stand.

Belen wrapped her fingers in the hem of her apron so her skin would touch nothing. She sat like that until the tiles went grey with the first working-light, and the women came, and one of them told her the moss on the third row wasn't going to scrape itself.
