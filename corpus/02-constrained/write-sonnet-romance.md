Otu ran the deep-fryer at the chip shop on the harbor road, and the thing about deep-fryers is that they announce trouble before it happens: a smell, acrid and wrong, that meant the oil had gone. He caught it on a Tuesday morning, cursed, and started dragging the fryer basket out back to the yard where the gulls would fight over the ruined batch. That's where he first saw her, crouched by the seawall with a tide chart and a laptop balanced on a lobster crate, muttering numbers to herself.

"You're blocking my sun," she said, without looking up.

"You're sitting in the bin lane," he said.

She looked up then. Not at him — past him, to check whether a truck was actually coming. Satisfied there wasn't, she went back to her laptop. Her name was Ngozi, she told him eventually, though not that morning. That morning she just told him to move the fryer basket six feet to the left, which he did, mostly because arguing felt like more effort than the day deserved.

He didn't think about her again until Thursday, when she came into the shop soaked through and furious, holding a memory card in a plastic bag like it was evidence of a crime.

"Your sign," she said. "The one on the seawall. It's blocking the shot."

"What shot?"

"I'm surveying erosion for the county. Three weeks of baseline photographs from that exact spot, and Monday somebody's bolted a six-foot cutout of a fish onto the railing."

He recognized the fish. He'd helped bolt it there himself, at the previous owner's insistence, back before the shop was his. "That's been there two years."

"It's been there two years in the wrong spot. It's in every photo I've taken this week. I have to start the baseline over."

He offered to move it. She said moving it now would just corrupt the second half of her dataset to match a corrupted first half, and did he understand what that meant for her report, and no, obviously he didn't, he fried potatoes for a living. He said that potatoes required more precision than she seemed to think. It went on like that for a while, neither of them backing down, until the rain got heavier and she left without buying anything, which stung more than the argument had.

He took the fish sign down that night anyway, alone, with a wrench and no ladder, standing on an upturned bucket in the dark. He didn't tell her. He told himself it was because he didn't want an apology, but it was closer to the opposite — he didn't want credit either, just wanted it handled.

She noticed on Friday. Came in dripping less than before, hair pulled back, and instead of thanking him she asked if he had any chips ready, because she'd forgotten to eat and her hands had started that specific tremble that meant blood sugar, not cold.

He fed her. She ate standing at the counter, and told him — unprompted, the way people tell strangers things they wouldn't tell anyone who mattered — that the erosion survey wasn't really about erosion, that the county had already decided to sell the seawall parcel to a hotel developer and just needed a report that looked thorough enough to justify it, and she'd known that going in and taken the contract anyway because rent existed.

He didn't have anything useful to say to that, so he said the fish sign could probably go up somewhere less scenic, if she needed the wall clean a while longer.

"A while longer" turned out to mean six more weeks. She came by most days that summer, not always for the survey, and he learned that she hated coriander and loved arguing about tide tables with the harbor master, who was wrong about nearly everything and enjoyed being told so. Otu learned to save her the end pieces, the ones nobody else wanted, crisp at both ends.

Neither of them said what the six weeks were leading toward. When her contract ended, she stood in the doorway with her equipment case and asked if the shop needed someone two evenings a week, unofficially, no pay, just to see what happened. He said the fryer only had room for one person behind it. She said she'd stand in front, then, and he'd have to figure out how to work around her.

He was still figuring that out, weeks later, elbow to elbow with her in a space built for one.
