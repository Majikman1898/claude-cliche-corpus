The maintenance log for the tug Ferrous Nine listed her cargo as forty thousand tons of smelted rail stock, and it was Guram's turn to walk the hull before the long burn. He'd been doing this job eleven years, since Tbilisi, since before the company painted over the old name on the bow, and he still checked the welds by hand because the sensors missed hairline stuff.

He found the anomaly instead by nearly floating into it.

It sat off the port strut, not on any scope readout, a patch of sky about four meters across where the stars didn't move. Everything else wheeled slowly as the Ferrous Nine rotated for thermal balance. That one patch held still, like someone had cut a hole in a photograph of space and taped it over the real thing.

"Dato, come to portside three," Guram said into the suit mic. "Bring the spectrometer."

Dato Abuladze arrived tethered and cursing about the cold, took one look, and stopped cursing.

"That's not a hole," he said. "Look at the edge. It's got parallax. It's close."

The instruments, once pointed correctly, agreed: the anomaly was solid, roughly nine meters at its long axis, drifting alongside them at their exact velocity. It had matched course with a forty-thousand-ton tug for at least six hours without anyone noticing, because it reflected nothing. No radar return, no thermal signature, just a companion-shaped absence.

The captain, a broad, unhurried woman named Nino Sturua, ordered a tether line and a sample probe rather than evasive maneuvers. "If it wanted us dead we'd know by now," she said, which Guram found less comforting than she meant it.

The probe returned readings before it returned itself: crystalline structure, layered like mica, laced through with threads of an alloy nobody in the crew's databases could name. Under magnetic scan, the object lit up with internal structure — chambers, conduits, a lattice too regular to be geological.

"It's a ship," Dato said. "Or was one."

Nino brought them in close enough for suited hands to touch the hull. Guram went out with the boarding party, three of them clipped to a line, and found the surface warm — not hot, but warm, three degrees above the vacuum around it, radiating from something still working inside. They found a seam. The seam found a way to open for them, sliding back with no sound to carry it, and inside was a chamber lined in the same dark crystal, holding forty-one identical shapes suspended in cradles of the mica-like stuff.

Cocoons. Not corpses — the scan showed slow metabolic activity, a heartbeat's rhythm stretched to the length of hours.

"They're not passengers," Nino said over the comm, once the images reached the bridge. "They're cargo. Look at the hold ratio. This whole vessel is built around keeping forty-one of something alive on a very long trip, and nothing else. No drive core we recognize, no life support for a crew. It's an ark, running on automatic, and its automatic systems latched onto us as an escort the second we came into proximity — matched our velocity because we were the largest mass nearby, probably reading us as another ark."

It took four days to work out how to wake one of the sleepers without killing it, using the ship's own systems once Dato found the interface — a resonance panel that responded to pressure and warmth, not electricity. The first occupant to open her eyes was smaller than a person, sinewed like something built for higher gravity, and she did not scream or attack. She looked at Guram's helmet, touched the glass, and made a sound the suit's software rendered, badly, as a question about distance.

They could not answer her in her own terms. But they could show her the stars, unmoving now that her ship had stopped hiding, and let her see they weren't alone in the black either. The Ferrous Nine radioed home with the coordinates, the readings, the woken sleeper's slowing pulse as she adjusted to a wider world, and Nino filed the report the same way she filed everything: plainly, factually, forty-one souls and one crippled ark now under tow, no longer invisible, no longer alone.
