The contractor stood in the kitchen, pointing at the water stain that mapped the ceiling like a bruise. "Roof's gone. Full replacement, at least forty thousand. Could be more once we get up there."

Sævar set down her coffee. Forty thousand. She had maybe twelve in savings, most of it borrowed against the inheritance she couldn't yet access. The will was in probate. The house—her grandmother's, left to no one else—had become hers only on paper.

She was supposed to grieve. People had sent flowers. Her mother had arranged a funeral at which Sævar had sat in the front row, numb as a tooth about to be pulled. But there was no time for that particular kind of missing. She had sixty days to repair the property well enough to list it, or the bank would foreclose on an equity loan she'd needed just to keep the taxes current.

"Can you give me a week?" she asked.

He glanced around—at the ancient wallpaper curling at the seams, the kitchen cabinets whose handles had turned green with age, the window that didn't quite close. "Week won't fix it."

After he left, she found herself in the master bedroom, a place she had avoided since arriving three days ago. The bed was still made. Her grandmother had died in a hospital fifty miles away, but the house had kept this room like a devotional. Sævar opened the closet and began pulling out clothes in plastic, shoes in careful rows. She wasn't looking for letters or meaning. She was clearing space. This room, emptied, could be restaged for photographs. The bedroom was the largest in the house. Good light during morning hours. Hardwood floors underneath that hideous carpet if she was lucky.

By evening, the room was a cavern. She called a friend in commercial photography. "I need photographs in two days. Professional. Listing quality."

The roof, though. She found herself on the porch in the dark, craning her neck to see where shingles had come loose. Her grandmother had lived here sixty-three years. Paid off the mortgage in 1987. Never fixed anything that still technically worked. The philosophy of a woman who'd survived the depression, who counted every coin.

Sævar called the contractor back. "What if I get a second mortgage on the place?"

He looked at her and named a price for emergency work, cheaper than the full estimate, acceptable to the appraisers.

That night, she signed documents digitally, committing income she didn't yet earn to damage accumulated for years. She ordered pizza from a place in town and photographed the bare master bedroom from four angles. The hardwood was intact. The walls were white underneath the ancient paper.

By morning, the contractors were on the roof, tearing out rot. She watched from the driveway, feeling nothing about it. No epiphany about inheritance. Her grandmother was dead and the house was broken and both required fixing.

The realtor arrived on Thursday. "Once the plumbing's updated, you'll do fine," she said.

Sævar nodded. She'd already researched plumbers. She had a spreadsheet now—roof, plumbing, electrical, paint, flooring—each item with its cost. Twelve weeks before she'd need to decide whether to keep it or let it go.

Her phone buzzed. A text from her mother: "How are you holding up?"

She didn't text back. The inspector's report had flagged the foundation—a crack needing monitoring. A year of checking, watching for worse. By then she'd be elsewhere, in whatever apartment she could afford, not inherited.

The afternoon plumber quoted nine thousand dollars for the main line. She authorized it. The number meant nothing. It was just a number on a list that wouldn't stop coming.

She went to the kitchen and made more coffee. The bucket was gone. The ceiling was patched, waiting for paint. A sample can of eggshell white sat on the counter, unopened. She could paint it herself, save money. But that required deciding she cared.

Through the window, the plumber stood checking the road connection, this stranger who would tear up her grandmother's yard, replace what had been in place for decades. He looked up and waved.

She didn't wave back. She turned on the faucet and let the water run, watching it go down, loud and clear, held by pipes that would soon be new.
