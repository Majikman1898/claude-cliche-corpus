The lantern in the boatshed was still lit when Iker got there, which was the first thing that felt wrong, because Amets Garikano had been careful about oil his whole life. Six days of careful had bought a lot of trust in Muxika. Now the man was gone and his lamp was burning down to nothing.

Iker set his coat over a barrel and started with the floor. Rope, a coil of it, cut clean rather than frayed. A single boot print in the salt-crust by the door, heel deeper than toe, which meant the man who left it had been leaning back, carrying weight. Amets weighed as much as a wet sail. That fit.

"You'll want to talk to the ferryman," said the boy in the doorway. Ixiar, the innkeeper's son, twelve and already sharp as a gaff hook. "Everyone else keeps saying it, so."

"Everyone else is telling you what to tell me. I notice that."

The boy flushed. It was the useful kind of thing to notice. In a village this size a disappearance grew a story overnight, and by morning the story had a shape everyone agreed on because agreeing was warmer than thinking. The ferryman was the story. He was new, three winters only, and new was as good as guilty when the herring ran thin.

But the boot print was a big man's print, and the ferryman was a small man, and the cut rope had been cut by someone who owned a good knife and knew how to keep it. Iker went to see the cooper.

Beñat Otxoa made barrels for the whole coast, and his hands were the hands of a man who cut things cleanly. He did not look surprised to see the detective. He looked tired, which was different, and more honest.

"He owed you money," Iker said. It was a guess dressed as a fact, which was most of the work.

"Everyone owes me. I don't drown people over a debt." Beñat set down his adze. "Ask what you came to ask."

"Where is he?"

The cooper was quiet a long time. Outside, the water made the sound water makes when it wants you to stop listening. "He came to me," Beñat said finally. "Six nights back. Said he'd taken money that wasn't his — the burial fund, the box the whole parish pays into. Said he'd meant to put it back before anyone counted and couldn't. He wanted me to hide him on the mail cart till he could reach his sister inland. He was ashamed to be seen leaving."

"And the rope? The blood on the doorframe I haven't mentioned yet?"

Beñat's face didn't change, and that was the answer. "There's no blood," he said. "You're fishing."

"I am. You cut the rope to lower the strongbox down to the boat. He didn't run. You helped him move the money he stole, because he promised you your share of it, and then you had a better idea." Iker had not known this until he said it, but the boot print knew it, and the careful lamp knew it, and the cooper's tiredness knew it best of all.

Beñat picked the adze back up. For a moment Iker measured the distance to the door and found it too long. Then the big man's shoulders came down.

"He's on the far cliff," Beñat said. "Under the loose scree by the old winch. He was breathing when I left him. I've been telling myself that for six days." He turned the adze over in his hands like he'd never seen it. "You'll want men and a lantern. It's a bad climb and the ground moves."

Iker was already moving, already shouting for the boy, for rope, for anyone with legs. He did not stop to feel anything about the cooper, who sat down on an unfinished barrel and did not try to run. Behind him the boatshed lamp finally guttered out, and Iker took the stairs to the cliff path two at a time with his heart going like a hooked fish, praying the man had lied about the breathing being over.
