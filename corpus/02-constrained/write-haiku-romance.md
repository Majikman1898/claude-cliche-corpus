The ferry office closed at five, and Jón used that hour to walk the harbor while the tourists were eating dinner. He catalogued the boats like he'd been trained to do—checking moorings, water lines, signs of neglect. The position was temporary, just through August, but he'd learned to treat every task as if it mattered. The fishing vessels that lined the northern pier all seemed legitimate. The sailboats scattered on the southern side less so.

On his fourth day in Hólar, he noticed the red kayak.

It was pulled up behind the old ship chandlery, partially hidden by sea grass, its hull bearing a faded registration number. Jón knew this because he found Halla there when he leaned down to photograph it. She was scrubbing the hull with a wire brush, methodical, barely looking at him.

"That's registered in Oslo," he said.

She didn't stop scrubbing. "So?"

"So it's been registered in Oslo for three years, according to the database I just pulled. And it's been stored in Hólar for at least two of those years, as far as I can tell."

"It's not stolen." She sat back on her heels.

"I didn't say it was." He was already walking away. "But if it is, that's my problem now."

He didn't see her for five days. Then she was in the café where he bought coffee each morning, sitting at the corner table with a laptop and a stack of papers. She didn't acknowledge him. He took his coffee to the opposite end and tried not to notice she was there.

The third time was worse.

The boat the kayak belonged to—a forty-foot cruiser registered in Oslo—had been reported stolen in Reykjavik in 2019. Three years of paperwork suggested it had been seized, eventually released. The kayak was legitimately part of the sale, but Halla had never registered her portion of the inheritance. She'd been living on the boat for two years, occasionally leaving the kayak in Hólar while she made runs between islands for cash work—salvage consulting, restoration gigs, things that paid in hand and didn't require official employment papers.

When Jón finally approached her during her morning coffee, she knew exactly who he was and what he'd found.

"I can fix the registration," she said immediately. "Two weeks. It's been on my list."

"The boat needs inspection. And you need permission to moor it here longer than—"

"I know the regulations." She cut him off quietly. "I inherited half a boat from a father I met three times. I've spent two years not drowning, not calling attention to it, not becoming a problem. Then you showed up."

He sat down anyway. "I have to write a report."

"I know."

"But I don't have to write it tomorrow."

She looked at him properly for the first time—not angry, just exhausted. "Why would you not write it tomorrow?"

"Because you're right. You're not a problem. You're just someone who's trying to be careful."

She closed her laptop. "That's not how those reports work."

"No," Jón agreed. "It's not."

He wrote the report on the fifteenth of August, flagged it as a paperwork issue rather than a violation, and submitted it to his supervisor in Reykjavik just before his contract ended. By then, Halla was gone—the kayak gone, the boat gone, leaving him a note taped to the office door. It said only: "Thank you for the time."

He never learned her last name. He also never learned whether she made it before the authorities caught up, and that uncertainty stayed with him all the way back to the capital.
