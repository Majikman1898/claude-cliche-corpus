The coffee dispenser had jammed again. Rivas, the ship's engineer, was elbow-deep in its mechanisms when Ketil's voice came over the intercom: "We're picking up something. Port-side, about two light-seconds out."

Rivas pulled her hands free, wiping them on her suit. "Define something."

"That's the problem. Can't. It's not emitting radiation. Not moving. Just... there. Gravimetric sensor says it's massive, but it's registering as nothing else."

The Meridian Dawn was a survey vessel, the kind of small ship humanity sent to places nobody else wanted to look. Five crew. Three years from any real port. Rivas knew the sound of Ketil's voice when he didn't understand something, and this was it.

Captain Oberon ordered them to stations. Rivas moved to the main console while Ketil pulled up high-resolution scans. Indah, their astrophysicist, was already murmuring calculations under her breath.

The object appeared on screen as a geometric suggestion, more absence than presence. Rectangular. Fractured. Metal, maybe—something that read as artificial the way a pile of stones wouldn't.

"It's a ship," Indah said quietly. "Or it was. Not ours. Not any design I recognize."

"How old?" Oberon leaned forward.

"Centuries. Maybe longer. The spacetime around it is... wrong. It's creating a fold. A very precise one."

Rivas felt the familiar itch—the one that meant trouble. "What kind of fold?"

"The kind that shouldn't be possible with conventional drives. Whatever this was, it was attempting to short-cut through space. Failed. Now it's locked in a dimensional pocket. It's been slowly collapsing for a very long time."

The collapse, Indah explained as she worked, was reaching a critical point. The ship's failing containment would cascade in approximately four hours. When it did, the spatial distortion would expand outward in a wave. The Meridian Dawn was positioned such that the wave would hit them directly.

They had options. Leave, which would take seven hours—too long. Or approach and attempt to manually stabilize the containment field. Possibly die trying.

Rivas volunteered immediately. Ketil wouldn't let her go alone.

They suited up in the airlock without much talking. The shuttle flight was strange—the closer they got to the derelict, the stranger the space around them felt. The instruments couldn't quite agree on distance or velocity.

The wreck itself was worse than the scans suggested. It was peeling itself apart, literally—matter phasing in and out of normal space. They docked with difficulty against a section that looked only mostly real.

The door opened into a corridor where gravity felt negotiable. Rivas found the source of the distortion in what might have been an engine room: a crystal lattice the size of a person's head, pulsing with a light that hurt to see. Not broken, she realized. Designed to fail. A suicide vessel.

She didn't think about it much. She cut it from its moorings and fed it into the containment tube Ketil had brought. The moment it was sealed, the distortion stopped.

They were back aboard the Meridian Dawn when the derelict finally imploded—silently, cleanly, leaving nothing behind. In the cargo hold, the crystal stopped pulsing.

Indah ran analyses for hours before giving them an answer: it was a message. The ship that carried it had been broadcasting, trying to reach someone across the stars. The crystal had been keeping it here, alive, for three hundred and seven years.

"No one answered," Rivas said.

"No," Indah agreed. "But we're here now."

Rivas downloaded the message to the main console and let it play.
