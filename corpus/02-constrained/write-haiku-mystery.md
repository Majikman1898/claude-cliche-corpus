Sigrun set the stack of ledgers on the café table hard enough that the proprietor flinched. Three weeks since Haldor Jónsson's disappearance, and all anyone wanted to discuss was the mist hanging over Hvítá. The village's census book had been updated that very morning—she'd verified it herself—yet the man had vanished between midnight and dawn without leaving a trace.

"He was here on the eighteenth," the proprietor, Björn, said again, his voice flat with repetition. "Took his usual tea. Cinnamon, nothing added."

"And the nineteenth?"

Björn shook his head. His daughter, Steinunn, had been watching from behind the bar counter all this time. When Sigrun glanced up, the girl's attention snapped down to polishing a glass she'd polished twice already.

Haldor wasn't a man the village had reason to miss. Sixty-three years old, deaf in one ear, he kept livestock no one could afford to buy from him anymore. He'd lived alone in the cottage past the mill, the one with birch trees grown tall enough to block out the world from the windows. Sigrun found his stove still warm when she broke down the door on the twentieth, the interior neat, undisturbed, as if he'd simply walked out and decided never to return.

She'd expected the typical rural missing person: an old man wandered off confused, found three villages over with his memory beginning to fray. Instead she found something else entirely—fresh footprints leading away from his door, retreating backward into the property. Not arriving. Fleeing.

"Did he take anything with him?" Sigrun asked Björn.

"I wouldn't know," he said carefully. "We didn't keep track of his personal effects."

But Steinunn had turned around at the counter, overheard the question. "He had a box," she said quietly. "Blue, with dents along the side. He carried it in here once, months back. Said he was taking it to Garður."

Garður was a village fourteen kilometers south, built on an inlet facing the open sea. Sigrun hadn't thought to ask whether Haldor kept family or connections elsewhere. The dossier she'd been sent mentioned only estrangement and routine poverty.

She drove the coastal road, the mist so thick she could barely see the stone walls that marked the boundaries. Garður appeared gradually—harbor first, fishing boats like shadows, then the low houses climbing the slope. At the postal office, she found it.

He'd opened a mail forwarding service just before his disappearance, paid six months upfront. Everything addressed to Copenhagen. The next day he'd returned with that blue box, sent it along.

"Did he say where he was actually going?" Sigrun asked the clerk.

"He said he was finally leaving," the woman replied. "First time in thirty years I saw him look like he wanted to exist."

Sigrun drove back to Hvítá, filed her report: voluntary absence, case closed. But before leaving the village, she returned to his cottage. On the table was a note in his shaking hand. She read the first line—"I was never here anyway"—and tore it up without reading further.

The next morning, still packing, her phone rang. A woman named Lúna said she was Haldor's daughter from a marriage dissolved thirty years prior. She lived in Garður, had sent him money for the ticket, was helping him escape.

"He was ashamed," Lúna said. "The village kept him like something to be endured. He couldn't breathe there."

"Why are you telling me this?" Sigrun asked.

"Because you're still searching," Lúna said. "He's in my kitchen drinking real coffee for the first time in five years. He's not dead. He's just not there anymore."

The line went dead. Sigrun started her car and drove north. Behind her, Hvítá sealed itself back into fog. She thought about filing a second report, but Lúna's voice stayed with her: He's not dead. She drove on without looking back.
