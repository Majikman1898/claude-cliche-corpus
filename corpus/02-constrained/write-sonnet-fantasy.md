Otoo brought her grievance to the tavern the way she brought everything—loudly, and with her hands. "It ate half my seed corn," she said. "Half. Before I even planted it."

The rest of the counting-house crowd didn't look up from their tallies, because Ngozi Adaeze had this problem more days than not, and there was a particular flavor of unlucky that clung to some households no matter what.

Adaeze wasn't in the tavern. She was in her cellar, on her knees, staring at a jar of black beetles she had not put there and had not summoned, either, not on purpose. She had been reciting the binding-verse her grandmother taught her—the one for keeping mice from the grain—and instead the beetles had come, glossy and numerous, chewing through a sack that had taken her three months to fill. She scraped them into the jar with a wooden spoon, hands shaking, and did not tell anyone that when she said the final word of the verse wrong, on purpose, just to see, the beetles had frozen mid-bite. All of them. At once.

That was the part she kept to herself. Not the failure. The word.

She was nineteen and had been apprenticed to no one, because the village of Ihiala-on-the-Ridge had no resident mage, only a traveling one who came twice a year to bless wells and take a fee for it, and he had told her flatly, four years back, that her aptitude was "middling, maybe less," and to stop wasting his afternoon. She had believed him mostly. She still practiced anyway, in the cellar, because the alternative was weaving baskets for the rest of her life, and she was bad at that too.

The wrong word was called nso—a syllable that belonged to no verse she'd been taught, a corruption, the kind of thing her grandmother would have spat over her shoulder to hear. It didn't compel. It didn't ask. It simply told the beetles the truth about themselves, that they were made of the same small stopped moment as everything else, and for that moment they believed it and did not move.

She used it three more times that week. Once on a leaking bucket, which held. Once on her own trembling hands before threading a needle, which she immediately regretted, because stillness that total made her forget to breathe for a four-count and she came up gasping. Once, unwisely, on Otoo's dog, mid-bark, and the dog's silence afterward was so total and so wrong that Otoo crossed herself and refused to walk past the house for a week, though she never learned why.

Adaeze did not tell the village. She'd watched what happened to the miller's boy two seasons back, who'd shown off a minor luck-charm at the market and spent the following month fielding demands—bless my calving, bless my daughter's marriage bed, curse my brother's fence line—until he'd broken the charm apart out of sheer exhaustion and gone to work the quarry instead, where no one wanted anything from his hands but labor. She had no intention of becoming a bucket for other people's wants.

What she wanted to know, and could not ask the traveling mage, was whether nso would work on a person all the way, not just a hand or a held breath. She suspected it might. She suspected that was precisely why no verse she'd ever been taught contained it, why her grandmother's whole tradition swerved around that syllable like water around a stone, generation after generation, without ever once explaining why.

On the equinox, when the traveling mage came through again to bless the wells, she stood at the back of the crowd and watched him work his competent, unremarkable magic, and said the word under her breath, so quietly no one could have heard it over the well-song. Nothing happened. He kept singing.

She hadn't aimed it at him. Not yet. She only wanted to know if she still could, with witnesses close enough to notice, and now she knew: yes, and no one had noticed a thing, and that fact frightened her far more than the beetles had.
