Bàt Su smelled like tallow smoke and wet stone, and the woman who ran the mill wanted her husband found before the millrace froze solid enough that no one could drag it.

Investigator Ganzorig had been in worse places for less money. He'd been in better places for more. He stood in the doorway of the mill with his boots already soaked through and listened to Oyunchimeg list the ordinary facts of her husband's absence — gone eleven days, no coat taken, the dog left chained, the ledger open to a page of debts that made no particular sense.

"Everyone here says the marsh took him," she said. "They say it kindly, like it's a mercy."

"Do you believe that?"

"I believe the marsh doesn't take shoes." She showed him one boot, found on the towpath, laces still tied in a bow. Nobody walks out of their shoes and drowns politely.

He worked the village the way he worked all of them: not by asking who had a reason, because everyone always did, but by asking who had recently stopped needing money. The blacksmith's daughter had bought a horse. The chandler had settled a debt he'd carried for three winters. Small things. The kind of small things that meant somebody had come into cash and was being careful about it, spending in increments too modest to notice — except that Ganzorig noticed for a living.

He found the miller's ledger easier to read than Oyunchimeg had let on. The debts weren't debts at all but a private accounting of loans the miller had made under the table, at rates that would have gotten him run out of the valley if the borrowers ever compared notes. Half of Bàt Su owed him money nobody else knew about. That gave the entire village a reason to want him gone, which was the kind of fact that made an investigator's job harder, not easier — too many suspects usually meant none of them had done it, just as too few witnesses usually meant somebody had arranged for it to look that way.

The girl with the new horse hadn't killed anyone. Neither had the chandler. What they'd done was simpler and stupider: word had gotten out, quietly, that the miller's private ledger had gone missing along with the miller, and half the valley had spent the last week paying itself back out of his till before anyone official came looking. Petty theft dressed up as fate. The marsh got blamed because the marsh was a convenient shape to hang a disappearance on, not because anyone knew what had actually happened to him.

What had actually happened to him was simpler still. Ganzorig found the miller nine days after he'd been reported gone, two valleys over, registered at a coaching inn under his mother's maiden name, with a tin box of coin and a woman who was not his wife. He hadn't drowned. He'd left, deliberately, the way men sometimes do when the weight of being owed by an entire village becomes less interesting than the weight of a new life somewhere the ledger can't follow.

"You could have written to her," Ganzorig said, over a cup of the inn's bad tea.

"And say what? That I got tired of being the man everyone in Bàt Su hated and needed?" The miller turned his cup in his hands. "She'd have come looking either way."

"She did come looking. She hired me instead."

The miller had the good sense not to ask what happened to his ledger. Ganzorig didn't offer to explain that the debts were mostly spent now, scattered into horses and paid-off winters, and that dragging the money back out of the valley would cost more in ill will than it was worth. Some math wasn't his to do.

He rode back through Bàt Su once more before leaving the district, past the mill wheel turning slow and unwatched, past the blacksmith's daughter walking her new horse along the towpath in the cold morning light. Oyunchimeg took the news standing in her own doorway, and didn't cry, and didn't thank him, and asked instead exactly how much of the ledger he intended to put in his report.

He told her the truth: as little as he could manage and still get paid.
