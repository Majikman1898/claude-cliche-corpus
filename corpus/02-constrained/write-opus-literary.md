The house key stuck in the lock the way it always had, and Nkemdirim had to lift the door on its hinges with her knee to get the deadbolt to turn. That, more than anything, told her she was really back. The trick was in her body still, three years and an ocean later.

She had come for the survey pins. Her uncle's letter, the one from the solicitor, said the eastern boundary was in dispute — a neighbor named Okonjo claiming eleven feet of the orchard, and the sale could not close until it was settled. She had a plane to catch on Thursday. She wanted the pins found, the line walked, the papers signed, the whole thing turned back into money she could carry away from here.

Inside smelled of the same floor wax and cold ash. She did not stop to feel it. She went straight through to the kitchen, put her folder of documents on the table, and spread the plat map flat, weighting the corners with a salt cellar and two mugs. The property was drawn in confident black ink from 1961. Somewhere out past the pear trees there were supposed to be iron rods driven into the ground, and if she could photograph them against the map she could make the solicitor's problem disappear.

She found the wellington boots by the back step, cracked but her size, and went out into the orchard with a garden fork and the deed folded in her pocket. The grass had gone to seed and soaked her to the knee. She counted trees. The map said nineteen; she found twenty-two, which meant someone had planted after the survey, which meant the map lied a little, which meant everything was already harder than the solicitor believed.

She was on her knees in the wet, working the fork into the dirt where the corner ought to be, when a man's voice came over the hedge.

"You won't find it there. Your uncle moved it."

She sat back on her heels. An older man stood at the boundary with a dog, both of them regarding her without much warmth. Okonjo, she assumed.

"Moved a survey pin," she said. "That's not really a thing people do."

"He did it the year the council reassessed. Pulled the rod, set it eight feet west, poured a little concrete round it so it looked settled. I watched him do it from my kitchen." He said this flatly, as a fact about the dead, not an accusation. "He told me he'd sort it with me later. He never sorted anything later."

She looked at the fork, at the hole she'd made, at the confident 1961 ink in her pocket that was apparently a work of fiction. Her whole plan for Thursday depended on the ground telling the truth, and the ground had been tampered with by a man she had loved and not especially known.

"Show me," she said.

He hesitated, then unlatched a gate she hadn't noticed was a gate, and the dog came through first. He walked her eight feet west of nothing, to a place where the grass grew in a faint ring, and toed it. "Here. You'll want to break the concrete. I'll get the bar."

He was gone ten minutes. She knelt and pulled the sod away with her hands until she found the grey lump, and she was still crouched there, filthy to the elbows, when he came back with an iron bar over his shoulder and, unexpectedly, two enamel cups of tea balanced in his other hand. He set them on the flat top of the hedge post.

"Drink that first," he said. "The concrete's not going anywhere. Forty years it's waited."

She stood, and took the cup, and her hands left dark prints on the white enamel, and across the ruined boundary the two of them drank without saying anything, and then she picked up the bar and asked him where exactly to swing.
