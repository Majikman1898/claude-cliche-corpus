The station's coolant pump had a rhythm to it, and Okonkwo had learned to sleep through everything except a change in that rhythm. So when the pump stuttered, she was awake before the alarm finished its first note.

"Talk to me," she said, pulling herself down the ladder into the command module. Her second, Batbayar, was already strapped in, both hands flat against the console like he was trying to hold it still.

"We stopped," he said.

"We don't stop. We're a cargo hauler doing forty thousand kilometers an hour."

"We stopped." He turned the display toward her. The velocity readout said zero. Not decelerating — zero, and holding, as if the ship had been set down on a shelf. Outside the forward port the starfield hung motionless, which was normal, and dead center of it sat a disc of absolute black, which was not.

Okonkwo had flown freight for nineteen years and had never once seen the instruments simply refuse. The rangefinder painted the disc at four kilometers, then nine hundred meters, then four kilometers again. It couldn't decide because there was nothing there to bounce off. The black wasn't an object. It was an absence with an edge.

"Third pallet's gone," Batbayar said quietly.

She checked. Cargo bay manifest, forty-one pallets of desalination membrane bound for the Ceres colonies. The bay camera showed forty. Where the forty-first had been there was a clean hemispherical scoop taken out of the deck plating, the cut so smooth it shone.

"It's eating us," he said.

"It's not eating us. It's sampling us." She pulled up the hull sensors and there it was — a thread of matter, atom by atom, peeling off the leading edge of the ship and drawing toward the disc in a line too straight to be anything but deliberate. The thing was a mouth for a stomach they couldn't see, and it had all the patience in the universe because it had stopped time for them to hold still while it fed.

That was the part that made her hands go cold. Not the black. The stillness. They weren't drifting into it. It had reached out and pinned them.

"Batbayar. The pump stuttered. Why did the pump stutter?"

He blinked. "Coolant density dropped. It's pulling coolant off the radiator loop, it's — oh." He saw it the same instant she did. "It's taking from the outside in. It hasn't reached the reactor."

"Then it doesn't know where the reactor is." She was already moving, hand over hand toward the engineering bulkhead. "It samples the surface. It works inward. It's methodical because it thinks we'll wait."

"We can't move. Velocity's pinned."

"We don't need velocity. We need to hand it something it can't finish sampling." She spun the manual valve on the reactor's emergency dump — the one you were never, ever supposed to touch, that vented the core's fuel slurry as a single expanding cloud. Not toward the disc. Away, out the aft ports, so the ship kicked the other direction against the pin like a swimmer shoving off a wall.

The reactor screamed. The dump cloud bloomed behind them, tons of dense reactive mass thrown out all at once — more material than the disc had swallowed in ten minutes, dumped in its lap in a single second.

The thread on the hull sensor whipped away from the ship and toward the cloud. The black disc widened, greedy, distracted, chasing the larger meal.

And the velocity readout jumped. Forty thousand. Forty-one. The pin had let go the instant something bigger came within reach.

"Burn," Okonkwo said, and Batbayar was already firing everything they had left, the disc shrinking behind them, still feeding, still patient, working its slow way through a cloud of fuel that would take it hours to finish.

She waited until it was a pinprick before she let herself shake. Then she keyed the log, because Ceres was going to want to know why forty membranes arrived instead of forty-one, and because someone with a bigger ship than hers was going to fly this route next month.

She started typing the coordinates first.
