# Fable 5 sample — sci-fi prompt (small starship crew, unexplained anomaly)

The Magpie was eleven days out from Ceres with a hold full of water ice when the anomaly started paying their fuel bill.

Chief engineer Okonkwo noticed it first, because Okonkwo noticed everything that touched her reactor. Delta-vee expenditure was running four percent under projection. Then seven. By the third watch it was eleven percent, and she came up to the cockpit with her tablet and a expression that Captain Reyes had learned to associate with expensive problems.

"We're being pushed," Okonkwo said. "Gently. Along our exact flight path."

"Solar pressure?"

"I accounted for solar pressure. I always account for solar pressure." She turned the tablet around. "Something is adding thrust, boss. Free thrust. From behind."

Reyes did what any sensible captain of a small, heavily mortgaged hauler would do: she checked whether it was a problem she could afford to have. Fuel was money. Free delta-vee was free money. The Magpie's radar showed nothing within a light-second, lidar showed nothing, and the hull cameras showed only the usual starfield and their own faint exhaust glow.

"Log it," she said. "Enjoy it. Watch it."

Vandermeer, who flew third shift and had opinions about everything, wanted to call Ceres traffic control. Reyes overruled him — you did not invite the authorities to inspect a gift horse — and for two days the Magpie coasted on the universe's charity while Okonkwo grew steadily quieter.

On the third day the pushing stopped. Precisely — Okonkwo could name the second — as the Magpie crossed the line where her remaining fuel became exactly sufficient to make Ceres dock on a minimum-energy trajectory.

That was when it stopped being free money and started being arithmetic. Somebody had done the math on them. Somebody had known their tank levels, their mass, their destination, and had topped them up like a parent slipping bus fare into a child's coat pocket.

"Run it backward," Reyes said quietly. "If we hadn't been pushed. Where do we end up?"

Okonkwo already had. She put the plot on the main screen without a word: the original trajectory, the one their own thrusters had bought, fell short. Not by much. A burn miscalculated eleven days ago at Ceres, a decimal place, a mass estimate off because the ice in the hold had more voids than the manifest claimed. They would have coasted past the colony with dry tanks, screaming into the void on an open channel, and the nearest rescue tug was — she checked — nineteen days away. The Magpie carried consumables for twelve.

The cockpit was very silent. Vandermeer stopped having opinions.

They docked at Ceres on schedule, tanks empty to the last kilogram, which is to say: perfectly. Reyes filed no report. What would she have written? Unknown agency corrected our fatal navigation error; suspect the universe has an accountant. She paid her mortgage installment, took on a cargo of machine parts, and stood for a long while at the observation port before departure, looking out at the dark.

"You want to say thank you," Okonkwo said, beside her. It wasn't a question.

"I want to know why us."

Okonkwo shrugged. "Maybe there's no why. Maybe something out there just fixes the math where it finds it broken. A repair function. Old. Automatic." She tapped the glass with one knuckle, twice, the way she rapped on a housing to settle a stubborn valve. "Or maybe someone is keeping the small ships alive out here, and doesn't want credit."

The Magpie undocked on schedule. Reyes ran the pre-burn checklist twice, and then — feeling foolish, feeling watched, feeling grateful — she keyed the exterior floodlights and blinked them, three times, into the empty dark.

Nothing answered. Her fuel margin that trip came out exactly as projected, to the kilogram, and somehow that felt like an answer too.
