The frost came early to Brennoch the year Ilsa Verren turned seventeen, and everyone blamed her for it.

Not openly. The village had rules about that sort of talk. But she saw how the miller's wife pulled her children close when Ilsa passed, how old Tam Corrick spat twice into the snow after selling her candles. She was the hedge-witch's apprentice, licensed by the Circle at Dunmore to mend fevers and coax barley from tired soil, and nothing more. Small magic. Sanctioned magic. Magic that fit inside the little iron-bound book of Permitted Workings that Mother Aggeth kept chained to her table.

It was Aggeth's dying that changed everything. The old woman went in her sleep on the third night of the frost, and it fell to Ilsa to wash the body and burn her effects, as the Circle required. Everything a witch touched in her last year had to go to ash — that was law. So Ilsa fed the fire through the grey morning: bundles of herbs, wax tablets, a lifetime of small tools.

The floorboard under the bed was loose. She would tell herself later that she only stepped on it by accident.

Beneath it lay a book with no chain and no iron, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age. The cover bore a single word in the old script: *Innaith*. Ilsa knew enough of the dead tongue to translate. *The breath beneath*.

She should have burned it unopened. Instead she read until the candle guttered.

The Permitted Workings taught that magic was borrowed — drawn thread by thread from the world, paid back with fatigue and offerings. The hidden book said otherwise. It said the world itself was breathing, one long slow exhalation since the beginning of things, and that a mage did not borrow power but *joined* it, the way a swimmer joins a river. The Circle had forbidden the practice three hundred years ago. Not because it corrupted, the book's margins insisted, in Aggeth's own cramped hand, but because it could not be taxed, tracked, or licensed. *A river cannot be chained to a table.*

Ilsa put the book back under the floorboard and did not sleep.

Three days later, the frost took the Harrow family's well, and their youngest, a boy of six named Pell, fell through the ice sheeting the millpond trying to fetch water. They pulled him out grey and still. Ilsa arrived with her sanctioned charms — warmth-workings, breath-callings — and felt each one slide off the boy like rain off slate. Too small. Too slow. The Permitted Workings could warm a body by degrees, over hours. Pell had minutes, and then fewer than that.

The mother was screaming. The millpond ticked and groaned behind them.

Ilsa closed her eyes and did what the book described: she stopped pulling at the world and instead let go of the place where she ended and it began. The breath beneath rose to meet her — not like a river after all, but like a tide, patient and enormous, as if it had been waiting at her edges her whole life. She poured it through her hands into the boy's chest, warmth without limit, warmth without cost, and Pell convulsed and coughed pond water onto the snow and wailed.

The crowd went silent. Every villager there had grown up on stories of the old magic, and every one of them knew that no licensed hedge-witch could do what they had just seen.

It was Tam Corrick who spoke first — Tam, who spat when she passed. He looked at the boy squalling in his mother's arms, then at Ilsa kneeling in the slush, steam still rising from her fingers.

"Frost's been hard this year," he said slowly, to no one and everyone. "Don't recall anything worth reporting to Dunmore. Anyone else?"

One by one, the village shook its heads.

Ilsa walked home through the snow, the tide still murmuring at her edges, and understood that she now held two forbidden things: the power itself, and a whole village willing to lie for it.

The second, she suspected, was the more dangerous.
