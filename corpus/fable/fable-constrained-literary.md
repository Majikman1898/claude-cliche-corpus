The inspector found the knob-and-tube wiring in under an hour, and after that the buyer knocked fourteen thousand off his offer, and after that he stopped returning calls altogether, and that was how Choua Xiong came to be standing in her grandmother's kitchen on Jessamine Avenue holding a crowbar she was not entirely sure how to use.

She needed the money by the first of September. Not wanted — needed. Yang Properties was holding the commercial kitchen space on Payne Avenue for her, twenty-four hundred a month, and they would not hold it past Labor Day. She sold out of egg rolls at three farmers markets every weekend, working out of a church basement on a borrowed license, and every wholesale account she'd pitched — the co-op, two grocery stores, the university — had said the same thing: get a licensed kitchen and call us back.

Her grandmother had died in April and left the house to Choua alone, which had gone over at the funeral about as well as a grease fire.

"You will not sell her house to a flipper," Uncle Blong said. He'd let himself in with a key nobody had asked him to return, and now he stood in the doorway of the emptied living room in his good jacket, as if he'd come from somewhere official. "Your grandmother carried this family across a river. She cleaned offices for thirty years to buy this house."

"I know what she did."

"Then you know what it means."

"It means she left it to me," Choua said, "and it needs a new roof, a new panel, all new wire, and the basement wall is bowing in like a sail. You want it? Buy it. I'll give you the family price."

He didn't have the money. They both knew he didn't have the money. He stood there a while longer talking about respect, and she went back to pulling baseboard, and eventually the storm door hissed shut behind him.

Her cousin Pheng came Thursdays and Sundays. He was two years into his apprenticeship and would rewire the place for cost plus beer, off the books until the permit came through, at which point his journeyman would sign off and pretend he'd supervised. It would eat eleven weeks and most of her savings. It would also add forty grand to the price, and forty grand was the kitchen, the hood installation, the first insurance premium, a used delivery van.

The house fought her the whole way. Plaster dust in everything. A dead outlet that turned out to be live. Wasps in the soffit. Some nights, cutting into a wall, she'd catch a smell — white rice, tiger balm, the ghost of forty years of fish sauce in the joists — and her chest would go tight and strange, and she'd stand there with the saw idling until it passed. It never resolved into anything. It just passed, and she kept cutting.

In August, Blong came back. He didn't apologize and neither did she. He set a foil-covered plate on the counter, looked a long time at the open walls, the clean new romex stapled up the studs like tendons, and said, "Pheng did this?"

"Pheng and me."

"Crooked staple, there."

"It'll hold."

He ate standing up, told a story she'd heard forty times about the year the pipes froze, criticized her drywall order, and left without saying whatever he'd come to say. The second buyer's agent called the next morning: cash, quick close, contingent on the electrical passing final inspection Friday.

Friday. Choua looked at the last uncut wall between the kitchen and the back bedroom, the last run of old cloth-wrapped wire in the house, and called Pheng before eight.

By noon they had the fish tape threaded down through the top plate, and she crouched at the outlet box with her hand in the wall, dust in her teeth, sweat running into her collar.

"Ready?" Pheng called from the attic.

"Pull," she said, and he pulled.
