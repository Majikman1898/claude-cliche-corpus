# Fable 5 sample — literary prompt (returning to an inherited house after a loss)

The lawyer had called it a bungalow, which was generous, and "in need of some attention," which was a lie of scale. What Dana inherited, the November after her father died, was a house that had spent nine years learning to live without people the way her father had spent nine years learning to live without her.

She drove up on a Saturday with cleaning supplies she wouldn't use and a folder of estate paperwork she couldn't face, and sat in the driveway for twenty minutes listening to the engine tick.

Inside, the house was a museum of his stubbornness. The kitchen faucet still dripped on the four-count — she found her hand reaching to jiggle the handle before she'd consciously registered the sound, her body remembering a fix her mind had spent two decades forgetting. The hallway still had the pencil marks. DANA 1994. DANA 1996. They stopped at 1999, the year of the argument, the year she turned fifteen and the marks turned into a different kind of measurement: how long since. How far away. How much unsaid.

She had planned to be efficient. Sell the tools, donate the clothes, list the house by spring. Instead she found herself opening drawers like a burglar with no idea what she was there to steal.

In the garage, on the workbench, she found the birdhouses.

There were dozens of them. Cedar, mostly, some pine, one — she picked it up and turned it over — walnut, sanded to a softness that made her thumb ache. He had never mentioned birdhouses. Nine years of Christmas cards with nothing but his signature, and the whole time he had been out here making these small, patient shelters, each one with its hole drilled to a precise diameter she didn't understand until she found the notebook: wren, 1 1/8". chickadee, 1 1/8". nuthatch, 1 1/4". Page after page in his blunt carpenter's hand. Bluebirds fledged, June. Sparrows took no. 14, let them stay.

Let them stay.

Her father, who had not let her stay, who had said the thing he said in 1999 and then spent a decade too proud to unsay it, had been keeping a ledger of every small bird he'd ever sheltered. She sat down on his stool, and the grief finally arrived — not the official grief of the funeral, which had felt like reading a eulogy for a stranger, but the specific, ambushing kind: grief for the man who measured entrance holes to the eighth of an inch so that nothing bigger than a chickadee could get in and hurt what was nesting there.

He had gotten better at it, she realized. That was the unbearable part. The early birdhouses, the ones dated in the years right after she left, were crude — over-nailed, gaps at the joints, the work of angry hands. The recent ones were nearly seamless. Somewhere in those nine silent years her father had learned gentleness, one dovetail at a time, and had found no way to tell her, or had told her the only way he could: wren, 1 1/8". Let them stay.

Dana did not list the house by spring.

She was there in April when the first wrens came back, to the box on the fence post, the one his notebook called no. 3, reliable. She watched from the kitchen window with her coffee going cold, hand resting on the faucet she had finally fixed, and when the first bird disappeared into that small, precisely measured dark, she said it out loud in the empty kitchen, because someone in that house had to finally say something first.

"I'm sorry. I came back."

The wren, of course, said nothing. It went on carrying grass into the box her father built, one stem at a time, making a place to live out of what a patient man had left behind.
