Forty-one jars of pickled beetroot. That was the first thing Detective Itziar Aldekoa counted in the missing man's kitchen, because counting was easier than listening to the constable behind her explain, for the third time, that Bittor Etxarri had no enemies.

"Nobody has forty-one jars of beetroot," she said. "Not for themselves."

He traded them, the constable admitted. All over Getxarre. Beetroot for mackerel, beetroot for firewood, beetroot for a haircut. There was a ledger, even — Bittor had been a lighthouse keeper for thirty years and kept accounts the way other men kept grudges. She found it under the bread tin. Every trade balanced, initialed, dated. Except the last entry, nine days old: one jar, marked out to J.O., with nothing in the return column and a small pencil cross beside the initials.

Bittor had walked out on a Tuesday and not come back. The village had searched the cliff path, the shingle, the eel pools below the breakwater. Fog had sat on Getxarre all week like a lid on a pot, and the searchers kept losing each other, calling names into whiteness, apologizing to sheep.

J.O. was Joanes Olaeta, the school bus driver. Fifteen years in the village, a widower's quiet manner, a way of tipping his cap that made mothers trust him with their children on the coast road. Itziar rode along on his afternoon run and watched his hands on the wheel. Steady. Too steady, maybe — the deliberate steadiness of a man performing calm for an audience of one.

"Bittor gave you beetroot," she said, when the last child was gone.

"He gave everyone beetroot."

"He wrote down what he got back. From you, nothing. Just a cross."

Joanes smiled at the windscreen. "Maybe I still owed him."

That night she called the licensing office in Bilbao and read them the number from his bus company file. It came back clean. So she read it again, slowly, and asked the clerk to check the issue date against the format used that year. There was a pause on the line, then the sound of a chair being dragged closer to a desk. The number was clean because it had never existed. It was invented — plausible, well-made, and false.

It took two more days to find the man underneath. Twenty years ago, in a river town inland, a driver named Josu Ormaza had put a lorry through a market stall. A woman died. He served four years, lost his license for life, and evaporated. Bittor Etxarri had worked the harbor lights near that town in the eighties. He was old, but the ledger proved his memory was a filing cabinet. He'd recognized Joanes. And being Bittor, he hadn't gone to the police. He'd gone to Joanes, with a jar of beetroot, and given the man a chance to resign quietly and leave. The cross in the ledger was a debt called in.

They found Bittor in the disused ice house behind the smokehouse row, wrapped in bus company tarpaulin, three meters from where children queued every morning. Joanes didn't deny it. On the cliff path, he said. Bittor had turned his back to point at the fog rolling in, saying something about how a man can't hide from weather. He'd only pushed him to stop the words. Then he'd caught him, too late, and carried him half a kilometer rather than let the sea have him, because — and here he stopped making sense — because Bittor deserved a proper finding.

In the car to the provincial station, Joanes asked one question. "Who'll take the morning run?"

"Not your problem," Itziar said.

"The Arrieta girl gets carsick if she's not in the front seat."

She wrote it in her notebook, under the case number, because someone would need to know, and then she started the engine and pulled out onto the coast road, headlights boring two short tunnels into the fog.
