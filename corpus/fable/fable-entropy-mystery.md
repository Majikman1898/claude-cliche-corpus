The fog came up the estuary every evening that October, and by the time it reached the village it had swallowed the church, the harbor, and the little print shop where Daniel had worked for forty years. He was sixty-four, and he printed tide tables, funeral cards, and the parish newsletter, mostly by letting the old press do the work while he dozed beside it. People called him idle, and he was. They did not call him stupid, because he wasn't that either, though he spoke as little as possible. His vowels still carried the flat sound of the inland town he'd left at twenty, and forty years among coastal people had taught him to be ashamed of it.

The night Natalia disappeared, Daniel saw lights on the water.

He told this to Detective Yuki, who came out from the city three days later, and he told it badly. Yuki asked where he'd been standing, and Daniel, nervous, said he'd been in bed. Then he said he'd been up fixing the press. Then he said he'd stepped out for air. Three answers, one question. Yuki wrote all three down without expression, and Daniel watched his one true thing — the lights, low and moving, off the old slipway where no boat had docked in years — get filed among the lies like a banknote among counterfeits.

"I saw them," Daniel said, hearing his accent thicken the way it did when he sweated. "That part's true. Whatever else, that part."

Natalia had taught at the school. She walked the sea path every evening, fog or no fog. Adrian, the harbormaster, organized the search parties and spoke movingly at the vigil. His nephew Xavier ran the supply boat and said he'd seen nothing. Ryan, the publican, told Yuki confidentially that old Daniel would say anything when rattled — hadn't he once claimed a heron got into the shop and set the type wrong? The village settled on a story: she'd slipped from the path in the fog. The sea kept what it took.

Yuki did not settle. Yuki came back in December, and again in March, and each time stopped at the print shop, because Daniel — lazy, evasive, unreliable Daniel — noticed things no one else could. In March, Yuki laid a customs clearance form on the counter. Daniel touched it once and said, "Wrong paper." He'd printed the genuine blanks for the harbor office in 1998. This stock was heavier. The perforation was punched, not cut. It took him thirty seconds and no effort at all, which was the only way he did anything.

The forged clearances led to a warehouse ledger, and the ledger to a mooring fee paid in cash on the night of the lights. It was fourteen months, nearly to the day, before the truth stood up whole in a courtroom: Adrian and Xavier had been landing untaxed cargo at the old slipway for six years. That night in October a crate had gone into the water, and Natalia, on the sea path, had seen them fishing it out. Xavier swore her death was an accident — a struggle, a fall, the rocks. Adrian had weighted the body himself.

There was no scene when the verdicts came. Yuki did not smile. The divers brought Natalia home in the fifteenth month's first week, and the village that had spoken so movingly at the vigil now spoke of Adrian in the past tense, quietly, as though he too had drowned.

Ryan stood Daniel a drink he didn't ask for. Nobody said the word sorry.

Only Yuki, leaving for the last time, paused at the print shop door. "The lights," the detective said. "You told it wrong three ways, but you saw them. That was the thread."

Daniel bent over the press so his face wouldn't show, and answered in his flat inland vowels, not caring for once how they sounded.

"I know," he said. "I was there."
