The fog came to Marlow Fen the way it always did in October—up from the estuary at dusk, thick as wool, swallowing the church spire first and then everything else. Detective Inspector Ruth Calloway arrived on the third day of it, when the village had already stopped pretending anyone could see across the green.

The missing man was Edwin Tarr, sixty-one, keeper of the tide gauge at the mouth of the fen. He had walked out of the Anchor & Hope on Tuesday night, said goodnight to the landlord, and dissolved into the white. His cottage door stood open the next morning. His kettle was cold. His dog, a grizzled lurcher called Biscuit, was found sitting at the end of the causeway, facing the water, refusing to move.

"People wander off the causeway in fog," said Sergeant Pell, who had met her at the pub with tea she hadn't asked for. "Happens. The mud takes them."

"Does it," said Calloway. She had read the file on the train. Edwin Tarr had walked that causeway drunk, blind, and once—famously—during the flood of '09, carrying a neighbor's child on his back. He did not wander.

She spent the first day on doors. The fog made every interview strange; villagers appeared in their own doorways like photographs half-developed, faces looming out of grey. They all said the same things. Good man, Edwin. Kept himself to himself. Terrible, the mud. And each of them, she noticed, glanced at some point toward the old pumping station on the far bank—a glance quick as a flinch, then gone.

The second day she asked about the pumping station. Decommissioned, said Pell. Condemned, said the landlord. Flooded, said Mrs. Vane at the post office, in a voice that closed the subject like a drawer.

But the parish records didn't say flooded. They said *purchased*—eight months ago, by a holding company, along with the drainage rights to the whole eastern fen. Calloway sat in the freezing records room with her coat on and followed the paper. The company wanted the sluices shut. Shut sluices meant the eastern fields would drown, quietly, over two or three winters—and drowned farmland could be bought for nothing, and there was talk of a solar development, and the signatures approving the survey included half the parish council.

The one man who would have noticed the water rising—who measured it, logged it, and mailed his figures to the Environment Agency every month like a liturgy—was Edwin Tarr.

She found him on the fourth day, and he was not in the mud. He was in the pumping station, in the dry well beneath the old turbine floor, with a wound on his head that no fall could have made twice. His last logbook was missing. His dog had known, sitting at the causeway's end, facing not the water but the far bank.

Councillor Hale came quietly, in the end. He stood in his hallway with the fog pressing at the glass and said it had been an argument, only an argument, that Edwin was going to ruin everyone over a few wet fields. He said the fog had made it feel like a dream, like nothing that happened in it could be real. He seemed to believe this.

Calloway cautioned him and walked him out to the car, and the whole village stood in its doorways to watch, faces half-developed in the grey, glancing at each other now instead of the far bank.

That evening the wind turned, and the fog began to lift off the fen in long slow ribbons, the way a sheet is drawn off furniture in a closed-up house. The church spire came back first. Then the green, the causeway, the bright ordinary water.

Calloway stood outside the Anchor & Hope with Biscuit leaning against her leg, and thought that fog never hid anything, not really. It only gave people permission not to look. The village had known where Edwin Tarr was for four days. It had simply been waiting for someone to arrive who would say it out loud.
