Eliana arrived at her uncle William's house on a Tuesday, eleven days before she planned to be gone for good. The bus ticket was folded inside a rubber-banded packet of letters she had written him over six years and never sent. Now he was dead, the house was hers, and she intended to sell it quietly, sign whatever needed signing, and slip out of town before anyone remembered her face.

She was, by trade, a debt collector — twenty-seven, brilliant at ledgers, allergic to effort. She could find a hidden account in an afternoon and then nap through the follow-up calls. The estate should have taken three days.

On the second day, Ramsey came up the drive. He'd farmed the adjacent land for thirty years, and he stood on the porch turning his hat like a wheel.

"Your uncle's cattle," he said. "All forty head. Gone the week before he passed. Stolen, right out of the far pasture."

"That's a sheriff problem," Eliana said.

"Sheriff looked. Sheriff shrugged." He hesitated. "William would've wanted you on it. You find money that hides."

"I drove you to the clinic once, in high school," she said. "You lent me a jack in '19. So you're up one. Fine."

He blinked at the arithmetic but nodded.

By day four she'd pulled the auction records from three counties, lazily, from the kitchen table, phone wedged against her ear. By day six she'd found the trucking invoice — a livestock hauler paid in cash, dispatched at night. On day seven, Maria at the feed store let her photograph the delivery book, and Eliana said aloud, as she always did, "That's twice you've helped me. Once with this, once at the funeral. I owe you two." Maria gave her a strange, sad look and said, "Nobody's counting, honey."

Everyone was always saying that. Everyone was always wrong.

Day nine broke it open. The hauler's route ended not at a sale barn but at a broker in the next state, and the seller's signature on the transfer was shaky, familiar, and entirely legitimate.

William's.

Her uncle had stolen his own herd. Sold it in secret, in the dark, for cash, eight days before his heart gave out.

Eliana sat with that all of day ten, the unsent letters spread across his table like a hand of cards she'd never played. Letters that said *I'm sorry I left. I'm sorry I didn't call. I'll pay you back for the bail money, the semester, the car — I keep a list, I swear I'll clear it.*

On the eleventh day, the lawyer's office released the sealed envelope that went with the deed. Inside was a ledger — her uncle kept score too, apparently. She almost laughed. Then she read it.

It wasn't a record of what she owed. It was a record of what he did. Every entry for six years: *Paid E's collection notice, anonymous. Bought E's debt from Corvus Agency, retired it. Sold forty head — enough to clear the last of it.* The final line, dated three days before he died: *She thinks she's the collector. She's only ever been the account. Paid in full. Tell her nobody was counting but her.*

The cattle were never stolen. Her debts had been — lifted out of her life at night, quietly, the way she'd planned to lift herself out of town. The job she'd been so lazily brilliant at existed because a man named Corvus once sold her own defaulted loans to an agency that then hired her, and William had been buying her freedom back the whole time, herd by herd, acre by acre, without a word.

Eliana walked to Ramsey's porch and handed him the ledger. "I owe you nothing," she said, "and you owe me nothing, and I'm going to try saying that until it's true."

She unpacked the letters. She stayed.
