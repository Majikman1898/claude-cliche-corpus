The mass alarm went off while Ganzorig was two hands into losing his coffee ration at cards.

"Sit down," Odval told him, because he was already halfway out of his straps. "It's a rock. It's always a rock."

But the *Overburden*'s charts said this stretch of the Belt-to-Hygiea run was swept clean, and the nav computer was insisting on forty thousand tons of something eleven hundred kilometers off the bow. Sarnai pinged it with the rangefinder. The pulse went out and did not come back. She pinged it again, then ran a diagnostic on her own equipment, because in six years of hauling ore she had never once seen a return of zero.

"It's not absorbing everything," she said slowly. "It's absorbing everything we send at it. There's a difference."

They killed the burn and drifted closer with the floodlights on. At two hundred kilometers the thing finally resolved on optics: a long, faceted hull, black in a way that made the eye slide off it, the surface drinking their light the same way it drank their radar. No transponder. No running lamps. No registry marks. The mass reading said it was mostly full of something dense and cold.

At one hundred fifty kilometers it spoke to them. A clipped, machine-voiced squawk on a military band nobody had used since the Ceres armistice: *Identify. Transmit authorization. You have ninety seconds.* Then, less politely, four hatches opened along its spine and gimbaled point-defense mounts came out of sixty years of sleep and tracked toward them.

"Rock," said Ganzorig. "You said rock."

Odval had her hands flat on the console, doing arithmetic. Too close to burn away before the guns ranged them. Too far to do anything else. "Somebody tell me what that is."

Ganzorig was already pulling the optics feed onto his screen, and something in the hull geometry snagged an old memory. His mother had spent twenty years at the Pallas yards, and she used to sketch the classified contracts on napkins after two drinks: coated fuel depots, seeded through the deep lanes during the war so the fleet could run dark. Stealth skin, deuterium slush in the belly, a dumb little watchdog brain. Most were recovered after the armistice. The recovery records, his mother always said, did not add up.

"It's a caisson-class depot," he said. "It's not haunted, it's not alien, it's a gas station with a gun. And it takes maintenance handshakes on the wideband, because the tender crews were never trusted with fleet codes."

"You know the handshake?"

"I know it was printed on the napkin." He was digging through his personal drive, past family photos, into the folder of his mother's scanned notebooks he had kept out of sentiment and never opened. Forty seconds. He found the page, read the sequence twice, and Sarnai transmitted it raw: the tender-crew service ping, six decades stale.

The guns stopped tracking. The hatches closed. The depot, satisfied that a maintenance crew had finally, finally arrived, obligingly reported its status: fuel reserves at ninety-one percent, hull nominal, awaiting resupply schedule.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Ninety-one percent," Sarnai repeated. "Of forty thousand tons. Of deuterium."

Under armistice salvage law, an unrecovered military asset in a civilian lane belonged to whoever tagged it and filed first. Odval had the claim beacon out of its locker before Sarnai finished pulling up the forms. They spent an hour matching hull numbers against the public decommission registry to be sure no navy would come asking, planted the beacon, and logged the depot's watchdog into standby with Ganzorig's mother listed, at his insistence, as originating engineer on the claim.

Back in the galley, he gathered up the cards and shuffled.

"Deal me in," Odval said.

"Double stakes," said Ganzorig. "I can afford it now."
