The laundrette in Coble Quay had two machines, and the one nearest the door ate pound coins. Everybody local knew this. Itxaso did not, which is how Gordon first met her: hammering the coin slot with the heel of her hand at nine at night, swearing in a language he couldn't place, her wetsuit dripping from a hanger on the mop rail.

"It keeps the money," he said from the folding table. "You have to use the other one."

"Now it has four of my pounds."

"It's had about nine hundred of mine over the years." He went back to his towels. She went back to swearing, quieter.

After that the town kept dealing them to each other like a short deck. She was ahead of him in the queue at the bakery, buying the last cheese roll. He was on the harbour steps when she backed her trailer down the slip at an angle that made him suck his teeth loudly enough to be heard over the winch. She learned his name from the woman at the post office — Gordon, retired off the ferries, engineer, widower, on the harbour every day of his life. He learned hers off the side of her equipment cases: I. URIARTE — SEABED SURVEY, stencilled in yellow. Council had hired her from somewhere in the Basque country to map the scour under the old breakwater before winter. Nobody in Coble Quay could say her first name and most didn't try.

What Gordon did, the second week of October, he did in good faith, which made it worse.

Fourteen small pink buoys had appeared across the inner harbour, unmarked, cluttering the fairway. Lost pot markers, he decided — kids' junk, a hazard to props. He spent a Tuesday morning with his boat hook pulling every one of them, coiling the lines, stacking the lot neatly by the harbour office with a note: FOUND ADRIFT, CLAIM WITHIN.

They were her transect stations. Three weeks of positioning work, each buoy anchored over a numbered reference point she'd fixed by diving it, because the GPS along the breakwater wall bounced too badly to trust. Her scan data was useless without them. The council deadline was the first of November.

She found him at the laundrette. It was not a quiet conversation. The word she kept returning to was *professional*, as in, this was professional equipment, this was professional work, and he was a man who had appointed himself harbourmaster of a harbour that had a harbourmaster. Gordon, who had never once in seventy-one years been shouted at in front of his own washing, said the buoys weren't marked, said anything unmarked was fair salvage, said it louder than he meant to. She told him what he could do with his salvage. The machine nearest the door chose that moment to eat her pound.

He rang the council the next morning to say the fault was his, entirely, and would they extend her deadline. They would not.

So on Thursday, at low water, there were two figures out on the harbour in a crabber borrowed off the Renwick boys. Itxaso sat in the bow with her charts and a handheld sounder. Gordon stood at the tiller, because it turned out he did not need her reference numbers. He had pulled every buoy himself, and he knew that harbour bottom the way other men know a hymn — the boiler off the old dredger, the ridge of dumped ballast, the hole below the third bollard where the eels were. He put her over each spot in turn while she dived the shackle back down, and they argued about two of the positions, and he was right about one and wrong about the other, and she wrote both outcomes in her notebook in front of him.

Eleven stations relaid by dark. Three to go.

"Tomorrow, seven," she said on the slip, peeling her hood back. "You bring the boat. I'm buying the cheese rolls, and you're not having the last one twice."
