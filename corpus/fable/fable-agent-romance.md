The first time Marisol Vega saw the man with the yellow kayak, he was arguing with a parking meter on Delgado Street, feeding it coins it kept spitting back. She was late opening the bakery and didn't stop, but she noticed the kayak strapped to his rusted Subaru like a canary on a corpse, and she noticed he thanked the meter, sarcastically, when it finally accepted his quarter.

Puerto Almeja was the kind of town where you saw everyone eventually, but eventually usually took a week. Marisol saw him again that same afternoon, standing in the surf below the old cannery pier with his pants rolled to the knee, holding his phone above his head like an offering. Then Thursday, at the fish counter, buying one sad fillet of rockfish. Then Sunday, at the lighthouse trailhead, going up as she came down, both of them pretending not to recognize each other because acknowledging a fourth encounter would have required conversation.

By the fifth time — he was sitting in her bakery, which felt like cheating — she set down his coffee and said, "Either you're following me, or this town has exactly one of everything and we both need all of it."

"I'm Teo," he said. "And I think it's the town. I've been to the hardware store three times and the same woman was buying birdseed every time."

"That's Ines. She's at war with a gull."

"Is she winning?"

"No one wins against a gull."

Teo, it turned out, had come to Puerto Almeja to scatter his father's ashes, a job he'd budgeted a weekend for and had now stretched into three weeks because he couldn't decide on a spot. The kayak had been his father's. He confessed this on their eighth crossing, at the laundromat, both of them hypnotized by the same tumbling dryer, and Marisol understood then why he kept turning up everywhere: he was auditioning the whole coastline. The pier. The lighthouse. The cove past the cannery. Every place she went for ordinary reasons, he went to ask an unanswerable question.

"What was he like?" she asked.

"Stubborn. He liked places where the land gives up. His words. Where it stops pretending it's in charge and lets the water decide." Teo folded a shirt wrong and refolded it wrong again. "Everywhere I look is almost right. Almost feels like an insult, for ashes."

Marisol thought about it for two days. She thought about it while proofing dough at four in the morning, while watching Ines hurl birdseed at her enemy like a medieval siege, while cashing out the register beneath the water-stained photo of the harbor her own mother had hung and she had never taken down, though her mother was six years gone and the frame was warped.

The ninth time she found Teo — and she did find him, this one was on purpose, which changed something — he was at the meter on Delgado Street again, losing again.

"Punta Ciega," she said. "North of town. There's a sandbar that only shows at low tide. It comes up out of nowhere, this little road of land, and for about an hour you can walk out into the middle of the bay. Then the water takes it back." She shrugged, suddenly shy of her own certainty. "The land gives up twice a day out there. On schedule."

Teo looked at her the way you look at a stranger who has somehow been carrying your answer around in her apron pocket.

"Would you come?" he asked. "Not — I mean, it seems wrong to do it alone. He hated doing things alone. It's why I have a kayak and no idea how to use it."

Low tide was at 6:40 the next morning. She left the bakery dark for the first time in four years, and the two of them walked out on the temporary road while the gulls screamed their eternal grievances, and the water, patient, prepared to decide.
