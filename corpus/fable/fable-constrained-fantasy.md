Bittor's cheese cave owed Usoa four days' wages, and Bittor was paying in cheese, which was why she was on her knees in the dark with her thumb on his ward-knot instead of asking questions about why it had failed twice in one month.

Licensed hedge-magic was knots. You learned it from the Guild's correspondence course, forty lessons at a silver each: the churn-knot, the fence-knot, the knot against weevils. You tied them in the air and they held a season if you tied them tight. Usoa tied tighter than anyone in Lastur, and Lastur paid her in cheese, in split wood, in mended boots, and she was nineteen and already tired of it in a way she didn't have a word for.

The ward hadn't frayed. That was the thing. Fray she could fix. This knot had been drunk — emptied — and it sagged in her grip like a wineskin. She turned it over, and her thumbnail caught a loose loop, and without meaning anything by it she pulled.

The ward's last magic ran up her arm like cold water going the wrong way.

She sat back on her heels among the cheeses. Lesson thirty-one, the only lesson printed in red: magic is tied, never taken. Unbinding is theft from the world, and the penalty is the pillory first and the fire after. She had always assumed that meant it was difficult.

She practiced on her own work first, in the goat shed, at night: tie a fence-knot, drink it, tie it again. What she took didn't leave. It settled in her like a meal. Within a month her churn-knots held two seasons instead of one, and the widow Otxoa said it was because Usoa had finally stopped rushing.

Then the tax assessor's trunk sat overnight at the inn, sealed with a Guild lock-knot worth more than everything Usoa had ever tied put together, and she stood in the innyard with her hands in her apron and drank it dry through the keyhole from three feet away. She didn't open the trunk. She didn't want what was in the trunk. The assessor rode out the next morning none the wiser, his seal a dead thing, and Usoa spent the whole day feeling like a church bell just after the ringing.

It was Garazi who caught her — the peddler's girl, sixteen, sharp as vinegar, camped behind the inn with her father's mule. "You did something to that lock," she said, falling into step on the river road. "It went out. Like a candle. I watch things; that's my whole trade." Then, before Usoa could arrange her face: "The big one's under the bridge. The old one. Nobody remembers what it's for."

Everyone in Lastur knew the bridge-knot. Tied by somebody three hundred years dead, fat as a ship's hawser, humming faintly if you put your ear to the stone. For the river, people said. Or against the river. Or against something the river had brought once. Nobody paid to maintain it; it maintained itself, which meant it was not hedge-work, which meant it was the kind of magic Usoa had been told all her life belonged to people who were not her.

"Half," said Garazi. "I keep watch, you drink, we're gone by Michaelmas. There's a woman in Bayonne who buys mages with real weight in them. You'd never tie a weevil-knot again."

Usoa thought about the pillory. She thought about the fire. She thought about forty lessons at a silver each, and being paid in cheese until she was sixty, and about the bell-feeling, which had not entirely faded, and which she wanted back the way a drunk wants morning to be evening.

"We don't know what it's holding," she said.

"No," Garazi agreed, cheerfully, already turning toward the bridge.

The stone was cold under her knees. The hum came up through her palms and into her teeth. Usoa found the first loose loop by feel, the way she found everything now, and pulled.
