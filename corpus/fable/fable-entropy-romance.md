The first time Diana saw the stranger, he was standing on the seawall at Gullhaven with his coat snapping like a sail, watching her hives on the cliff above the harbor. Day one of eleven, though she didn't know yet that she was counting.

"Those yours?" he called.

"The bees are their own," she said, and went back to doing as little as possible, which was her particular genius. Diana kept forty hives on inherited land and had won the Gullhaven Honey Festival's gold ribbon nineteen years running. One more and she'd retire undefeated, which was the only ambition laziness had left her. Twenty was a clean number. Her twin would have said it was a coward's number, but Diana hadn't spoken to her twin in eleven years, so her twin could say nothing at all.

Day two, the stranger was at the bakery, buying the last rye loaf. Day three, at the chandlery. Day four, on the cliff path again, and this time he introduced himself: Dmitri. Renting the blue cottage. Just passing through.

"Nobody passes through Gullhaven," Diana said. "It's not on the way to anywhere."

Day five, Paul the harbormaster came puffing up her lane with catastrophe in his face. The storm surge had flooded the community hall. The festival, six days off, had no venue, no funds, and by town charter a festival cancelled once lapsed forever. "A hundred and forty years, Diana. It dies on my watch, I die with it."

She almost shrugged. Then she remembered that no festival meant no twentieth ribbon, and undefeated with an asterisk was just defeated with better lighting.

So Diana, who never lifted a finger, spent days six and seven lifting many. She browbeat Eric, who owned the fish cannery, into lending his drying shed. She charmed Ethan, the young pastor, into surrendering the church's folding chairs and half his choir for atmosphere. And when Paul despaired of feeding the crowds—the flooded hall had drowned the festival stores—Diana went home and stood a long time before her cellar door.

Behind it lay her shame and comfort: shelves of hoarded jars, honey and pickled pears and sea-plum jam, enough for three winters or ten, food she'd squirreled against a hunger that never came. Nobody had ever seen it. She brought up two hundred jars in a wheelbarrow, four trips, and told Paul a lie about a bumper year.

Day eight, Dmitri was at the drying shed, hanging bunting. Day nine, he was there again, and she found she'd saved him a jar of the sea-plum. Day ten, they sat on the seawall past dark, and he told her he'd come to Gullhaven because someone he loved had spoken of it all her life, and Diana talked about bees so she wouldn't have to ask who.

Day eleven, the festival opened under a scrubbed blue sky, saved, unmistakably, by her. Ethan's choir sang. Eric fried herring. Paul wept twice. And on the judging table, beside Diana's gold-bright wildflower honey, sat one other entry: dark as engine oil, heather and salt-thorn, entered that morning.

She knew before the judge's spoon came down. Brilliance recognizes itself, even estranged. She tasted the dark honey and tasted the far side of the headland, her sister's side, the side Diana had always been too idle to walk.

"Second place," the judge announced kindly, as if kindness helped.

Dmitri found her by the seawall. "She couldn't come," he said. "My wife. She wasn't sure you'd want her here. She sent me ahead, eleven days ago, to see the town. To see you. Diana—she taught the bees everything. She learned it from watching you."

Twenty years. One festival she'd rescued with her own hidden stores, like laying out the banquet for her own funeral. Diana looked at her silver ribbon, the color of herring, of storm water, of second.

"Tell Eleanor," she said, and stopped, because there was nothing to send but the truth. "Tell her she won."
