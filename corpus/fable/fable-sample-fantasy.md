# Fable 5 sample — fantasy prompt (young mage, remote village, forbidden power)

The frost came early to Harrowmere the year Wren turned sixteen, and everyone blamed her for it.

Not openly. Openly they blamed the mountain, the way they always had — the old glacier that hung above the village like a held breath. But Wren saw how the miller's wife pulled her daughter close when Wren passed, how the offerings at the shrine doubled after her testing day came back wrong. Not failed. Wrong. The examiner from the Collegium had pressed his brass instrument to her wrist, watched the needle spin backward, and packed his bags without a word.

Magic was supposed to flow outward. Hers pulled.

She learned what that meant on the night the fever took her brother. Tam was six, and the healer had already gone home, and their mother was praying to a shrine that had never once answered. Wren sat by his cot and held his hot little hand and felt — for the first time, clearly — the sickness in him. Not as an idea. As a current. Something she could take.

So she took it.

It came into her like river water through a broken lock, filthy and cold, and she clamped her teeth shut and kept pulling until Tam's breathing eased and her own arms had gone gray to the elbow. She spent three days in bed. She told her mother it was exhaustion. Her mother, who had watched the gray fade from her daughter's skin like a bruise healing in reverse, chose to believe her, because the alternative had a name, and the name had a pyre attached to it.

Drawing. The dead art. The reason the Collegium existed at all — founded not to teach magic, the old stories said, but to make sure no one ever again learned to take it.

Wren was not stupid. She understood why. Every winter she felt her range grow: first a sickroom, then a house, then — standing on the shrine hill on midwinter morning — the whole valley, every warm lit window full of life she could feel the way a tongue feels a loose tooth. The power did not want to be used for fevers. It wanted to be used entirely.

But the frost kept coming. The glacier crept. And in the spring, when the melt should have come and didn't, the village elders climbed the shrine hill and slaughtered a white goat, and the mountain ignored them, the way it had ignored them for three hundred years.

Wren went up the mountain alone on a Tuesday, telling no one, carrying bread, rope, and her grandmother's iron knife, which was not for the mountain. It was for her, if it came to that. She had thought it through with the grim practicality of a girl raised to butcher her own hens: if she opened herself to a glacier and the glacier won, someone needed a way to close the door.

At the ice wall she took off her mitten and laid her bare palm against three hundred winters.

And pulled.

They found her two days later, walking down through the pines with her hair gone white as the goat they'd wasted, half her ribs cracked from shivering, alive. Behind her the mountain streams ran loud and brown with melt, and under the old glacier's skin, for the first time in living memory, there was the sound of water moving.

They never blamed her for the frost again. They never quite looked at her either. Power taken is power owed, her grandmother used to say, and the whole valley understood, without a word being spoken, that the spring belonged to Wren now — and that they had better hope she never asked for it back.
