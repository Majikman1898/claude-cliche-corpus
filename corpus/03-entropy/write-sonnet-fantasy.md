Hans was twenty-seven and technically employed by the Kreuzberg listening post, which meant he sat in a shed on the edge of the village with a headset and a dog named Suppe, who had one eye and smelled of wet bread. He was lazy in the specific way of brilliant men who have never been made to prove it, and devoted to Suppe in the specific way of men who have given up on being loved by anything with opinions.

The forbidden thing arrived on a Tuesday, folded into a radio frequency nobody used anymore, an old military band from twenty years back. It wasn't a broadcast. It was a shape in the static, a pattern that resolved, if you were bored and gifted enough to notice, into a kind of pressure behind the eyes. Hans, being both, noticed. He found that if he hummed back into the mic at the right pitch, the pressure became a suggestion, and the suggestion became a small miracle: the shed's dead stove lit itself. Suppe barked once and went back to sleep.

He told no one. Not because he understood the danger — he understood almost nothing about it — but because telling people required effort, and also because the last time Hans had shared something remarkable, it had gone badly.

That was the old crime. Twenty years ago, when Hans was seven, a supply ledger for the village co-op had gone missing along with a great deal of grain money, and the signature authorizing the withdrawal had been young Matthias's father's — forged, everyone eventually agreed, though never quite proven, and Matthias's father had left town under the specific kind of cloud that never rains, just sits. Hans had learned to forge signatures around that age too, an idle talent, a party trick for excusing himself from chores, and he had never once connected the two facts because why would he. It was, after all, only ever paperwork.

The witness resurfaced when Hans was thirty-four. Her name was Lucia, and she'd been the co-op's clerk back then, and she came back to the village to die slowly of something the doctors in the city couldn't fix faster, and in the boredom of dying she started talking. She told anyone who'd listen that she'd seen the real ledger the week before it vanished, and the withdrawal signature on it hadn't looked like the father's hand at all — it had looked oddly rounded, oddly childish, like someone had practiced it.

Nobody but Matthias, now a broad and quiet man running the family's half-collapsed mill, seemed to hear this as an accusation. He came to the shed with the specific stillness of a man who has waited two decades to be angry at the right person. Hans, humming absently into the radio with the stove crackling and Suppe drooling on his boot, looked up and understood, all at once, that his idle talent had a body count of one reputation and possibly one exiled father.

"It was a game," Hans said. "I was seven."

"You forged the receipts for cigarettes at the Ostermann counter until you were twenty-two," Matthias said. "I have three of them."

There was a silence in which the radio hummed its impossible little hum, and Hans, whose whole personality was the avoidance of being looked at directly, felt looked at directly, and hated it, and also — for one disorienting second — wanted more of it. Being accused, it turned out, was its own kind of invitation.

He confessed. Not dramatically — there was no lightning, no tribunal, just Hans and Matthias and Lucia (wheeled in on a borrowed chair, delighted to finally matter) sitting in the shed while Suppe worked on a bone. Hans admitted he'd forged the old ledger signature at seven for no reason he could name except that his hand had wanted to, and that he had spent twenty years since becoming exactly powerful enough, in one useless radio shed, to set stoves on fire with his voice, and not powerful enough, in twenty years, to be invited to a single dinner in this village.

Matthias considered all this with the weight of a man who had built and rebuilt his rage several times.

"So the mill closes over a pack of cigarettes," he said finally.

"Looks that way," Hans agreed.

Lucia laughed so hard she had to be wheeled outside for air, and the forbidden power, offended by the anticlimax, sulked back into the static and never spoke to Hans again. He got invited to dinner that Friday anyway. He brought the dog.
