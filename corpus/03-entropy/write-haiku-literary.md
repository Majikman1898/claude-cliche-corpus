Vincent arranged the saddles on his market table with the same precision his mother had taught him, though Mother was dead now—three months gone—and the inherited house waited empty beyond the town limits. He was thirty-eight years old, a saddle maker for two decades, and now proprietor of a crumbling estate he'd never wanted. He'd returned after the funeral because Mother's will allowed for nothing else, but spring market day was what kept him tethered to meaning. Work, commerce, the proof of his hands' value—these were the anchors.

"Exceptional work," Andrew said, arriving before noon. Andrew dealt in spices but had lately involved himself in everyone's business, moving through the market like pollen. "You'll outsell everyone this season. I'm absolutely certain of it."

Vincent smiled, though he shouldn't have—Andrew was transparent as water. But practical men grew tired of their own company, and Andrew's flattery was warm like sun on leather. Vincent wanted the record badly. One more season at the top would feel like resurrection. Like Mother's death meant something because her son had persisted.

The saddles sold briskly through spring. By early summer market day, Vincent's earnings had surpassed all rivals. He'd carried the record before—five times in his career—but never had it felt this necessary. Never had maintaining it felt like staying alive.

That's when Andrew found him again, whispering: "They're saying your mother was poisoned. That you hastened her passing for the house."

Vincent stood motionless, holding a half-finished saddle, while summer heat poured down around them. The accusation struck him like a blade.

"Who says this?" he managed.

"Everyone. No one will admit it. But Rachel—you know Rachel, the blacksmith's wife—she was with your mother the week before. Rachel says your mother complained of stomach troubles. Troubles that came on suddenly. Troubles you could have caused."

Vincent's fingers tightened on the leather. He'd carried unsent letters to his mother for years, words he'd never quite delivered about disappointment and distance and the small cruelties of obligation. Now those letters lived in his pocket like indictments.

By autumn market day, the accusation had fermented into collective certainty. Alice, who bought his saddles for resale across three counties, announced she would source elsewhere. Rachel had recruited the blacksmith himself. Other merchants avoided Vincent's table. Someone suggested exhuming Mother's body for examination.

Vincent's hands shook while stitching. The saddles came out worse, their stitches uneven, their balance wrong. He sold nothing that season. The record belonged to Andrew's favored supplier, a younger man with a spotless reputation and no dead mother casting suspicion.

Winter market day arrived gray and absolute. Vincent didn't bother setting up a table. He stood at the market's edge, watching his rivals display their work, watching Andrew greet each merchant warmly. Rachel laughed with friends. Alice was gone entirely.

Vincent returned to his inherited house alone. Within weeks, the property was seized—not by law enforcement but by collective certainty, that ancient force more powerful than truth. Mother's body was examined. No poison was found. The investigation's mere existence had transformed suspicion into fact.

He left his saddle-making tools behind like artifacts in a tomb. The practical man discovered practicality had limits. That flattery, when you're desperate, transforms into hearing what you need. That some seasons you don't win records. Some seasons you lose everything.

Vincent walked away from town with the unsent letters still in his pocket. They would never reach anyone now.
