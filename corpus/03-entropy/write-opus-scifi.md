The debt was twenty years old, and Lily had never once believed it forgotten.

She had been twenty-nine when she signed it: a berth on the freighter *Marigold*, passage off a dying agricultural station, and enough credit to buy the culturing tanks that made her the only cheesemaker between the Reach and the Vellum Drift. The lender was a man named Anton, who smiled like a door opening. Everyone warned her. Lily, who wore a bolt of iron in her pocket against ill luck and would not let a knife lie crossed on a table, ignored every warning, because Anton had called her gifted, and no one had ever called her gifted before.

"Twenty years, no interest," he'd said, "unless I ever need it sooner."

She had spent two decades not thinking about that word: *unless*.

Now the *Marigold* hung motionless in deep space around a thing the instruments refused to name — a sphere of folded darkness, perfectly still, that swallowed light and gave back a faint sound like breath held too long. And Anton's message had arrived in the same hour, routed through six relays to find her: *The full sum. Now. Or the tanks are mine.*

"He timed it," said Kaori, the *Marigold*'s pilot, a blunt woman who had flown Lily's wheels of cheese to markets for eleven years and had never once flattered her. "He knows we're stranded. He knows you can't earn while we're stuck."

"He knows more than that," Lily said. She was watching the anomaly. Practical to the marrow, she did not believe in ghosts. She believed in luck, which was different — luck was a ledger, and hers had just come due on two fronts at once. That could not be chance. "Kaori. What reads off that thing?"

"Nothing. It's not there. Mass says empty, light says wall."

Rose, the ship's young engineer, spoke up from the deck plating where she'd been listening to the hull. "It's resonating. Whatever's inside is answering our transponder. Same frequency. Like it's — copying us."

Lily thought about Anton, who had built a life out of copying other people's hopes back to them at a price. She thought about how a man like that always knew where you were, because he'd sold you the map.

"Rose," she said slowly, "the message from Anton. It came through the relays. But we're dead in the water — how did it *reach* us, if we can't send out?"

Rose went white. "It didn't come from outside. It came from *there*." She pointed at the dark sphere. "The signal originated inside the anomaly."

For a long moment nobody breathed. Then Lily laughed — a short, hard, delighted sound, because superstition and practicality had finally shaken hands. The anomaly was not deep space at all. It was a lie made of her own fears, transmitted back at her: the debt, the timing, the flattery that had always found the soft place under her ribs. Something out here fed on people by reflecting their worst debts until they surrendered.

"It's got nothing of its own," Lily said. "It only has what we give it. Rose — cut the transponder. Kaori — full reverse the second she does. It can't call anything back if we stop talking to it."

"And the debt?" Kaori asked.

"There is no debt. Anton signed papers twenty years ago and died to a bad heart eleven years back — I read it and made myself forget, because I wanted to keep being someone worth calling in on." She straightened. "I'm done being flattered. Rose, cut it."

The transponder died. The *Marigold* lurched backward, and the sphere collapsed inward without a sound, folding shut on nothing, on the absence of anyone left to lie to.

They limped home three weeks later. Lily sold her wheels at the Vellum market herself, standing behind her own table, and the buyers came not because they tolerated the strange practical woman with iron in her pocket, but because they'd sought her out.

Invited, at last. Cleanly, and by no one's leave but her own.
