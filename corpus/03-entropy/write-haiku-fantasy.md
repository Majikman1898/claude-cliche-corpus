Paula's hands trembled as she gripped the crane's controls, something that had nothing to do with the weight of the steel beam suspended forty feet above the village of Thornhide. At forty-seven, she'd operated this machine for twenty years—ever since the night she'd lost everything.

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, its edges still singed, postage marks smudged beyond recognition. The censor's redactions were crude, heavy black lines obscuring most of the text, but three sentences remained visible: *Your accusation was false. Michael recanted before the Council. They kept it buried to protect their reputation.*

Paula's jaw clenched. She'd never been superstitious about much, but she'd worn the same iron charm around her neck since the day Michael testified against her—the day he claimed he'd seen her tampering with the sacred artifacts in the temple vault, the day they'd stripped her of the title of Keeper that she'd earned through decades of study and devotion.

That night, she climbed to the old shrine on Thornhide's highest peak, a place no one had visited in years. The stones hummed beneath her fingers, and she realized the humming wasn't outside her—it was inside, vibrating through her bones. All those years operating the crane, moving impossible weights with perfect precision, she'd been unconsciously channeling something. Magic. The village's forbidden magic, the kind the Council had outlawed after the Fracture, the reason they'd purged the archives and sealed the vault.

She understood then why Michael had lied. He'd seen her entering the vault, yes, but not to tamper—to *learn*. She'd discovered them first, the old methods, the binding sigils that let a person move things through pure will. It threatened the Council's monopoly on power, their insistence that magic was dead and dangerous.

Paula descended the mountain with her hands glowing faintly blue, a color she'd only read about in the redacted texts. She went to the Council chamber. Her quick temper, usually her enemy, served her well—she didn't hesitate, didn't soften her fury with politeness.

"I know what Michael did," she said flatly. "And I know why. But here's what you need to understand: the lie he told destroyed me, and you let it. You buried his recantation to keep your secrets. So I'm taking back what's mine."

She didn't wait for their response. She simply *reached*, with that new sense, and pulled. The great vault doors, sealed for a generation, groaned open. Ancient texts spilled onto the chamber floor.

The Council collapsed into chaos. By morning, Paula had her title back—officially, ceremonially, publicly restored. The village would have a new Keeper, they announced, one who would teach the old ways again.

But there was a cost, as there always was. The magic had marked her now, visibly. Her hair went white within a week. Her eyes carried that faint blue glow permanently. Children crossed streets to avoid her. Michael, when she encountered him at the market, looked at her with an expression between pity and fear.

She'd won. She'd proven her innocence and reclaimed her station. Yet standing in the vault, surrounded by the knowledge she'd died to possess, Paula felt the weight of it differently than she'd imagined. The title tasted like ashes, and the power—the real power she'd discovered—had cost her the life she'd actually built.

She was Keeper again. She was also utterly alone.
