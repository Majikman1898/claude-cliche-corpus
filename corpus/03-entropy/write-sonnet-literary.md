The bees came before the grief did, in the sense that Valerie had kept them for twenty years and the funeral was only six weeks ago, but standing now at the gate of her Uncle Frederick's house, she felt the two things fuse into a single humming weight in her chest. She unlatched the gate the way she'd unlatch a hive lid — slow, so nothing inside would rise up angry.

"I am here," she announced to the empty yard, "as the rightful custodian of this land, and of what lies beneath it." She had been saying things like that since Pastor Maxwell's congregation took her in that spring, since the day she'd walked forward at the altar call still smelling of smoke and propolis. Newly devout, newly certain that certainty itself was a kind of protection.

What she wanted was simple, or she told herself it was simple: to move her mother's grave from the scrubby corner plot behind the tool shed — where Frederick had let it sit unmarked for eleven years out of some old family spite — into the proper cemetery plot beside her father. Frederick was dead now. There was no one left to spite her. Only there was.

"You can't just dig her up, Valerie." That was Elena, Frederick's second wife, who had arrived within the week holding a folder of paperwork thick as a hymnal. "Frederick's will leaves the whole property, plot included, to me. That grave stays where it is or I call the county."

"I drove him to chemo eleven times," Valerie said. "Eleven. You weren't even answering his calls that spring."

"This isn't a ledger, Valerie."

"Everything is a ledger. God keeps one too."

The third claim came from a place Valerie hadn't expected: her cousin Jackson showed up in week two with a deed lawyer and a theory that the original transfer to Frederick had never been properly recorded, meaning the land — grave and all — reverted to the family trust, of which Jackson was sole surviving trustee. Two rival claims became three, then folded back into two, Elena and Jackson circling the same six acres like wasps over a dropped peach, and Valerie in the middle insisting the peach was never the point, the point was her mother, the point was doing right by the dead before it was too late to matter.

She threw herself into it with the same theatrical fervor she brought to swarm season — sermons at the property line, a hand-lettered sign reading LET THE DEAD REST WHERE THEY BELONG staked into Elena's tomato bed, a prayer circle of two beekeeping friends and a deacon who mostly wanted to go home. "I fed Frederick's cats for a decade," she told Jackson. "I painted this porch twice. Twice, Jackson. You remember that?" Jackson only sighed and said the lawyers would sort it, and she heard, underneath, the particular tiredness of someone who has stopped listening to a person keeping score.

Week four brought the hearing, a folding-chair affair in the county annex that smelled of coffee and toner. Valerie wore her good black dress and quoted Ecclesiastes with more confidence than accuracy. She catalogued her favors for the judge as if he were a ledger too — the rides, the porch, the cats, eleven chemo trips — as though enough tallied kindness might requisition a body's right to rest. The judge, unmoved by arithmetic, unmoved by scripture, ruled that Frederick's will controlled, that Elena held clear title, and that the unmarked grave — like the shed, like the tomatoes, like the whole scrubby corner — was hers to keep or clear as she pleased.

Valerie did not weep in the annex. She waited until the parking lot, until she was alone with the hives she'd left running in the truck bed, their low steady drone the only sound that had never once argued with her. She thought of all the things she might still say — to Elena, to Jackson, to God — and understood, finally, standing there totting up a lifetime of small debts nobody else had ever agreed to owe, that she had lost, plainly and completely, and that no accounting would change it.

She drove home past the cemetery gate she would never open for her mother, and did not stop.
