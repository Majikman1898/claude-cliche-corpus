The barge galley was six feet by four, and for thirty-one years Michaela had cooked in it without once raising her voice above the diesel hum. She was fifty-one now, and she wanted one more season on the water — one more, so she could hold the record nobody else cared about: most consecutive seasons cooking the Antwerp-to-Rotterdam grain run without a single missed meal. Thirty-one years. Number thirty-two was hers to lose.

Then the letter came, and the fog came with it.

The village was called Mistvenne, and it earned the name. Michaela stepped off the train into a grey that swallowed the church steeple whole. She had come because a solicitor, a soft-spoken man named Nathaniel, had written to say that her great-uncle Adolf — a man she had met exactly twice — had died and left her his houseboat, the finest vessel on the canal, worth more than every wage she had ever earned.

There was a condition.

"He wanted," Nathaniel said, not quite meeting her eyes, "for you to read the eulogy at the memorial. Aloud. In the village hall. To everyone." He paused. "He was very specific. He wrote that you hide your voice, and that this was his way of — his words — curing you of the shame of it."

Michaela felt her accent thicken in her throat like roux. To speak aloud, in that flat vowelled village dialect she had spent a lifetime swallowing, in front of strangers who would smirk — she would rather scrub the bilge with her tongue.

"There is a complication," Nathaniel added. "A man has gone missing. Markus, the sexton. He was to arrange the memorial. Without him, no one knows where the will's second copy is kept, and — well. The condition cannot be waived without it."

So Michaela, who had never solved anything harder than a broken thermostat, became a detective. She walked the fog-drowned lanes asking after Markus in a whisper, and people leaned close to hear her, and she died a little each time. She found his ledger. She found his boots by the water. She found, at ninety percent certain, that Markus had not vanished at all but had simply rowed to the next village to avoid his own gambling debts, and would be back by Michaelmas.

She was ten percent from proving it. The ledger's final page would have shown the withdrawal, the plan, everything. She had the ledger in her hands.

She put it down.

This was the thing about Michaela, the thing the barge crews knew and forgave: she abandoned everything at ninety percent. The sauce one whisk from perfect. The letter home three sentences from sealed. She got close enough to see the ending and then, terrified of it or bored by it or simply unable to bear the weight of finishing, she walked away.

She walked away from Mistvenne. She left the houseboat, the fortune, the eulogy, the ledger, the whole grey riddle of it, and she took the train back to the water and cooked season thirty-two.

Twenty years passed.

She held the record. Fifty-one seasons, then. No barge cook had come close. When they finally retired her at seventy-one, they gave her a certificate in a plastic frame, and a man from the shipping company read a short speech, and Michaela did not have to say a single word.

Markus, it turned out, had come back by Michaelmas that very first autumn, exactly as the unread page would have shown. The houseboat, unclaimed, had rotted at its mooring and been broken up for scrap. Adolf's fortune passed to the state and paid, in the end, for the resurfacing of one municipal car park in a town Michaela never visited.

She heard all this years later, secondhand, and only shrugged. The soup that evening needed salt. She added it, tasted it, found it nearly right — and, satisfied, set down the spoon.
