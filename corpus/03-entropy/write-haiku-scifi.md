Leo was twenty-two when the Aurora's navigation system catastrophically failed in the Kepler Void. As the ship's dowser—someone with an uncanny ability to sense gravitational anomalies and navigate impossible stellar terrain—he should have been invaluable. Instead, he was scowling at star charts in the bridge, refusing to ask anyone where anything was.

"Just find it," Captain Michelle said, her patience thinning. "Find whatever's out there causing the interference."

Stumble, Leo's ugly dog, wheezed encouragement from a cargo crate in the corridor. The creature was a genetic accident from some long-forgotten research lab, all wrinkled skin and eyes that pointed in dramatically different directions. Leo loved him with fierce devotion.

Three hours into the investigation, the ship's water supply began turning purple.

"Poisoned," Michelle announced grimly, gathering the crew. "Sabotage. Someone's trying to kill us."

Every eye locked onto Leo. He'd been wandering through the ship that morning, following his dowsing instincts, never consulting navigation logs or asking anyone where the water systems were located. He'd actually been near the filtration compartments several times—or at least, he thought he had been. He'd deliberately refused to ask directions to confirm his location.

"It wasn't me," he snapped, already furious at the accusation. "I was investigating the anomaly—"

"Alone? Without confirming your movements with anyone?" Michelle's voice was cold. "Very convenient."

She suspended him from all duties pending investigation. Stumble stayed pressed against Leo's leg in the brig, a constant, comforting weight. The anomaly loomed closer on the viewscreens, a swirling void of energy that violated every law of physics. Then something impossible happened: the phenomenon moved. The ship lurched violently.

And suddenly, everyone understood: the purple wasn't poison. It was bioluminescent particles seeping from the anomaly itself, microscopic organisms that had infiltrated a tiny hull fracture the Aurora's faulty sensors had completely missed. The water wasn't poisoned—it was colonized.

Leo was publicly vindicated, but the humiliation burned deep. He threw himself into his work with grim precision, becoming the ship's most essential crew member over the next twenty years. Michelle remained captain throughout, methodical and unforgiving. Stumble, improbably, lived far beyond any reasonable lifespan for a genetic accident, as if love alone could sustain him.

At forty-two, Leo finally accumulated enough capital for his second dowsing shop. The original Aurora was now a museum orbiting Kepler Station. Michelle, recently retired, occasionally visited the exhibits.

Leo was signing the lease on his new location when Michelle appeared. She looked smaller somehow, diminished by age and regret.

"I need to tell you something," she said quietly. "New research came out five years ago. Stumble's genetic line was specifically designed to produce bioluminescent compounds in saliva as a biological safety marker. The lab wanted them to turn water purple if it ever contaminated the water table."

Leo felt the world tilt.

"All those times Stumble followed you through the ship," Michelle continued, "nuzzling against you while you navigated by pure stubbornness and refused to ask for directions—he was licking the water intake. Stumble wasn't your loyal companion. He was the poison."

The absurdity was so complete that Leo erupted into laughter, genuine and helpless. Twenty years of vindication, twenty years of righteous anger, and his beloved ugly dog had actually been guilty all along.

"At least I finally opened my second shop," he said, wiping his eyes.

Michelle smiled sadly. "At least you did."
