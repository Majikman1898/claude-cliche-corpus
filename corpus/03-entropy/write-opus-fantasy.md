The old gravedigger of Hollowmere was named Samuel, and for fifty-seven years he had buried the village's dead without complaint, which was more than anyone could say for the village's living. He was seventy-three, recklessly generous with the little he had, and desperately ashamed of the way his vowels bent sideways whenever he spoke, the accent of a valley three ridges over that everyone here found comical.

The trouble began six weeks before the frost, when the miller's daughter Freya went through the ice on the tarn. Samuel, who happened to be digging nearby, did what no man his age should attempt: he went in after her. He hauled her out by the collar, and as he did, something opened in his chest like a second lung. He felt the cold stop. He felt her heart, which had faltered, take up its beat again at his silent command. The power had been in him all along, the old forbidden kind, the sort the church had scraped out of the valleys generations ago. He had only needed a life to spend it on.

Freya lived. And this was the disaster.

Because Freya, waking on the bank, decided she owed him. Not a thank-you. An obligation. An unpayable, bottomless obligation that she pursued with the zeal of the young. She brought him bread. She swept his hut. She told the whole village that Samuel had powers, and soon they came too, wanting boils cured and cows mended, and Samuel, being recklessly generous, could not say no to any of them, and so spent his strange new gift down to the dregs each day and woke exhausted.

The second person to notice was Victor, the visiting church clerk, a soft careful man with ink-stained cuffs. Forbidden power was Victor's whole reason for existing. He watched. He took notes. He waited for Samuel to condemn himself.

What Samuel actually wanted, through all of it, was none of this. He wanted one thing: an apology. Forty years ago Freya's late grandfather had stood in the square and mocked Samuel's accent until the whole market laughed, and had never taken it back, and had died unrepentant. Samuel wanted, absurdly, unreasonably, to hear those specific words: *I'm sorry I made them laugh at how you talk.* He wanted it from anyone. He had waited four decades.

So when Victor finally cornered him, ledger open, and said, "Sign here. Confess the source of your power, and the church will be lenient," Samuel did the thing he had always done too easily. He forged. He had signed dead men's names on burial writs for years to save the grieving the paperwork; his hand knew the trick cold. He took Victor's pen and wrote, in the looping hand of Freya's dead grandfather, a full confession — and beneath it, an apology. *I mocked the gravedigger's accent and I am sorry.* He signed the dead man's name with a flourish and handed it back, satisfied at last.

Victor read it. Victor frowned. "This is dated ninety years ago. And signed by a man who has been dead for six." He looked up. "Also the confession is for mocking someone's accent, which is not a crime, and says nothing about magic."

"Read the bottom part again," said Samuel.

"I read it."

"Read it aloud."

Victor, baffled, read the apology aloud. Samuel closed his eyes and let the words land in the wrong voice, from the wrong man, forty years too late, forged by his own hand. It was everything he had wanted, and it was completely worthless, and he found to his mild surprise that this was fine.

Freya peered over Victor's shoulder. "So are you a witch or not?"

"No," said Samuel, standing, his knees cracking. "I'm a gravedigger. I've a hole to finish before dark." And he went out and finished it, and the power, having no more lives to save that afternoon, dozed quietly in his chest like a dog by a fire, and nobody in Hollowmere ever got a straight answer about any of it.
