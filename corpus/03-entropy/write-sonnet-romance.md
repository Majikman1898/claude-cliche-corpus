Aiko arrived in Pentawick on a Tuesday, the same day the sign at the harbor chandlery finally gave up its last flake of paint, and she took the job before she'd unpacked her brushes. Fifty-four years old, retired twice already and unretired twice out of boredom, she'd sworn this would be her last stretch of ladders and lettering. Undefeated. That was the word she'd settled on. She wanted to go out clean, no half-finished signs, no unpaid accounts, no scores unsettled.

She met Felix on the second day, at the top of the steps that switchbacked down to the cove, where he was photographing the cliffs for reasons he didn't explain and she didn't ask about. He had the look of a man who'd been somewhere else recently and was still deciding whether he liked it here. They didn't speak more than good morning. But she saw him again that afternoon buying the same brand of instant coffee she bought, and again the next evening reading on the same bench where she ate her fish and chips, and by the end of the first week she'd started, without meaning to, keeping a tally of him.

It was Clara who told her about the road. Clara ran the guesthouse where Aiko was staying, a squat woman with flour permanently under her nails, and she said it the way locals say ordinary bad news, without much weight behind it: the coast road had gone, taken clean out by a slide above Marlow's Point, and no one was getting in or out by land until the county cleared it. Six weeks, they were saying. Maybe more.

Aiko took this as a kind of grace. A closed room to finish her work in.

She had come to God recently, in the unglamorous way — not a lightning strike, just a slow leaking-in over a bad winter, until one Sunday she found herself in the back pew of St. Brannoc's without having decided to go. She liked, now, the idea of finishing well. Of forgiveness, in theory. But she had also spent thirty years not speaking to a man named Dominic, over a debt he'd never repaid her — a matter of four hundred pounds and a broken promise back when they were both young enough for it to still sting — and when the ferry brought the mail out on the second week and among it was a letter addressed to a Dominic Vane, general delivery, she understood with the flat certainty of scripture that the town had trapped them together on purpose.

She found him on the fourth week, thinner than she remembered, running the harbor office now that the roads were cut and someone had to. He looked at her like a man watching a bill come due. She meant to forgive him. She had rehearsed it on the walk over, the loosening in her chest she imagined would come with saying it's fine, Dominic, it's done. But when she opened her mouth what came out instead was the four hundred pounds, itemized, with interest, thirty years of it, and he said he didn't have it, and she said she knew he didn't, that wasn't the point, and they stood there in the low tide smell saying old true ugly things to each other until Felix came around the corner and cleared his throat and neither of them had noticed how loud they'd gotten.

Felix walked her back. He didn't say much, which she'd come to understand was his whole method — he sat beside people until they emptied out. That night, for the first time, she told someone about the church, about wanting to leave this life without a single grudge still open, and he said, not unkindly, that it sounded like a hard way to keep score.

The road cleared in the sixth week. She heard the diggers before she saw them, a rumor of engines climbing back into the world.

She finished the chandlery sign the morning the road reopened — gold leaf, her best work in a decade, the kind of sign that outlives its shop. Dominic came to see it, unasked, and stood a long time. He said it was beautiful. She said thank you. Neither of them said the other thing.

She got her clean finish, in the end — the sign was flawless, the ledger between her and God as settled as she could make it, and the account with Dominic closed, in its way, forever: not with forgiveness but with the last word, hers, spoken and unspent now and impossible to take back. She had wanted to leave undefeated. She left having won, and feeling, on the ferry out, the particular hollowness of a woman who got exactly what she asked for.
