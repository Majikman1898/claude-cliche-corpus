Fourteen months ago, Benjamin would have told you a map was just a map.

He was sixty-six that first autumn in Harmon's Reach, a fur trapper who had spent forty winters up-country and come down to the coast to finish clean. One last season along the salt marshes, one last honest run of pelts, and then he would hang his traps undefeated—no bad year on the ledger, no season he'd limped away from. He had found God two springs before, quietly, the way a man finds a coin in an old coat, and he prayed now over the animals he took, which was new and which he did not explain to anyone.

He met Claire outside the harbormaster's shed, though he did not know her name yet. She was crouched over a chart spread on a lobster crate, frowning, and she said, without looking up, "This inlet's wrong."

He almost kept walking. Instead he looked. The official survey—the one pinned in the shed, the one the town trusted—showed the eastern channel running deep and clear behind Gull Point. Claire's map, hand-drawn on oilcloth and soft with age, showed a shoal there, and a true channel a quarter mile north.

"Where'd you get that," Benjamin said.

"My grandfather sounded this coast." She rolled it half-closed, protective. "The county's chart is a copy of a copy. Nobody's put a lead line down in sixty years."

They crossed paths again at the chandlery, and again on the breakwater at dawn, and again outside the little clapboard church where Benjamin now went on Sundays and stood in the back like a man not sure he was allowed. Each time the map came up. It sat between them like a third person. His trap lines ran the eastern flats—where the official chart said firm ground and Claire's said sink. If she was right, half his best sets were laid on ground that would drown a man at spring tide.

He wanted her to be wrong. He was quick to anger and quicker when he was afraid, and twice he said things on that breakwater he had to pray about after. But he went out and sounded the eastern channel himself, dropping a weighted line off his skiff in the grey morning, and the lead came up at four feet where the county promised eighteen.

She was right. That was the trouble. She was right, and being right, she wanted the town to act—reprint the chart, remark the buoys, tell the summer boats. Davis at the harbor office wanted signatures. Emily from the county wanted a certified re-survey nobody would fund. Old David, who had fished these waters longer than either of them, said the channel had always been fine and Claire's grandfather had been a drinker.

Benjamin found himself, to his own surprise, on Claire's side of it. He knew ground the way she knew water. Through the winter and into the next spring they worked the thing together—soundings, affidavits, a petition. It was the closest he had come in years to caring about something that wasn't his own record.

And that was where the old flaw found him. At ninety percent—the survey nearly funded, the county nearly persuaded, one more meeting between them and a corrected chart—Benjamin stopped. He told himself it was the season, the traps, the retirement he'd promised himself. In truth he could not finish. He never could. He walked away from the last ten feet of everything he had ever built.

So they did the only thing left. Claire and Benjamin sat at Davis's desk and drafted a single page: that the two of them disagreed with the county chart, that the county would not amend it, and that both records would stand, side by side, for whoever came after to judge. They each signed. Neither had won. Neither had yielded.

Benjamin hung his traps that June, undefeated, if that was still the word. He kept a copy of the signed page folded in his coat, next to where he'd once have kept a coin.
