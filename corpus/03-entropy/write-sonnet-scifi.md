The cabin lights of the Marrow Owl flickered amber, and Alan didn't like amber. Amber meant something upstairs was pretending things were fine.

"You felt that," Alan said. Not a question. He gripped the edge of the console like a trapline he refused to let a wolf drag off into the brush.

"Felt a thruster hiccup," said Chris, not looking up from the nav board. "Sensors say nothing's out there."

"Sensors lie same as men do." Alan had spent thirty years reading which way a rabbit's blood dried on snow, and he trusted that skill a hundred times more than he trusted a screen built by people he'd never met. Out the forward port, the anomaly hung like a bruise on the dark — a slow, violet churn that swallowed starlight and gave nothing back.

Hana floated up from engineering, wiping grease from her knuckles. "Whatever it is, it's not on any chart. Graham's trying to get a spectral read, but the instruments keep resetting."

"Told you," Alan said, to no one in particular, the way he always said it, the way he'd said it for four years since the Kestrel run, since the thing he saw out past Ganymede that nobody believed, the thing that got him laughed off two crews and left him checking traps alone in the black for a paycheck nobody respected. He wanted, more than air, more than gravity, for somebody to look at that violet smear and say: you were right, Alan. You saw it first.

Then Charles came up from the mail bay holding an envelope like it might bite him.

"This came through on the last relay ping. Fleet finally released it — leftover backlog, three years censored pending review." He held it out to Alan. "It's addressed to you. From the Kestrel inquiry board."

Alan's hands, steady enough to skin a fox blind, shook taking it. Three years. Three years of being the crazy old trapper who saw ghosts in the dark, and here was the paper that could undo all of it, government seal cracked open at last.

He read it in the amber light while Hana called out temperature spikes and Chris begged the computer to give him one honest number.

The letter confirmed it. Kestrel's anomaly — his anomaly — was real, catalogued, designated a Class-Theta gravitational tear. Recommendation: any crew encountering similar phenomena should reverse course immediately. No exceptions.

"We need to go around," Alan said, voice cracking not with fear but with vindication. "I was right. I was right the whole damn time."

But the ship was already sliding, caught in something none of Graham's instruments could name, the violet churn pulling like an undertow nobody had swum against and lived.

"Reverse thrust!" Alan bellowed, the letter still crushed in his fist, and for once nobody argued, because paper from Fleet Command outweighed a lifetime of nobody listening. Chris hauled the stick back. The engines screamed. The tear released them, spat them backward into open dark, hull groaning, air venting from a ruptured seam near engineering.

They drifted free. Alive. Chris slumped over the console, breathing hard. "You called it, Alan. Every bit of it."

Alan looked at the letter, at the seal, at three years of vindication finally in his hand — and then at Hana emerging from the shaft where the emergency bulkhead had sealed too late to save the aft crawlspace, where Graham had been running the last diagnostic.

Nobody said his name. They didn't have to.

Alan folded the letter along its old creases, the ones worn soft from years of imagining this moment, and found no comfort in a proof that had cost exactly what he'd never once been believed enough to prevent.
