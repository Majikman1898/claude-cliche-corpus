Clark had always said the house would come to him crooked, and it did.

He was fifty-two when the letter arrived, forwarded twice, its corner soft as a wilted leaf. His aunt was dead. The house on Ferrow Lane was his, along with what she'd called, in her spidery hand, its condition. He read it twice, tossed salt over his left shoulder out of pure habit, and did nothing about it for eleven days, because that was Clark: brilliant enough to catch a breech baby by the ankle in the dark and turn it, lazy enough to let three months of owed fees rot in a drawer rather than knock on a grieving door.

The condition, it turned out, was Thomas.

Thomas had lived beside the house his whole life, a widower with a caretaker's key and a caretaker's grievance. He met Clark at the gate the first evening. "You'll want to know about the inspection," he said, before hello.

"What inspection?"

"The one your aunt dreaded twenty years. County comes every score of years to certify the old lane houses. Foundations, wells, the lot. If it fails, they condemn. She spent her life keeping this one standing so it wouldn't be the year it fell." Thomas looked at the sagging porch. "It's this autumn. Nobody escapes it."

So Clark stayed. This surprised everyone, including Clark. He told himself it was the work — a house wants doing, and a wanted man wants a house — but the truth was smaller and warmer. Thomas set a second cup on the table without being asked, and Clark, who had spent a lifetime being tolerated at the edges of other people's kitchens, found he would repair any number of foundations to keep being handed that cup.

They worked the years like a slow tide. Clark delivered the lane's babies — Alexis's twins, then years on, Alexis's grandchild — and never once billed hard. "Pay when it's easy," he'd say, and it was never easy, and the debts drifted off like smoke, and he let them. Thomas scolded him for it. "You give the whole village away and keep nothing for the roof." Clark only shrugged and hung a horseshoe over the door, points up, to hold the luck in.

Twenty years the inspection loomed, and twenty years they shored the house against it. Sills replaced. The well relined. Thomas grew stooped; Clark grew grey and slower, though his hands stayed sure. The house grew, against all odds, sound. And Clark grew certain, in the superstitious marrow of him, that a house so loved could not be taken.

The inspector came in October. Not Thomas's county man of legend but a young official named Jose, with a tablet and a schedule. He walked the rooms. He was polite. He tapped the beautiful new sills and nodded.

Then he explained that the lane itself had been rezoned three years prior — a drainage corridor, a flood easement. The houses' condition was immaterial. Ferrow Lane was to be cleared regardless of any repair. It had been decided in an office Clark had never entered, by people who had never handed him a cup of anything.

"You could have told us," Clark said. "Twenty years."

"The notices went to the registered owner," Jose said, not unkindly. "There were letters."

Clark thought of his aunt's drawer, and of his own, both furred with unopened envelopes, unclaimed dues, everything he had always been too gentle to collect or too lazy to open. He had spent his life waiting to be invited in, and here at last was a summons, and it was an eviction.

Thomas stood in the doorway under the horseshoe, saying nothing.

Clark did not shout. He walked out to the gate where he'd first refused to leave, and he looked back at the good sound house they had saved for nothing, and he understood that being loved and being kept were not the same, and never had been, and that he had, as always, learned the sum too late to collect on it.
