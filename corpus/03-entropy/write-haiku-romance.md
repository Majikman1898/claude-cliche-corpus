The shipyard clock read 0600 when Akane's shift began. Every morning, they wheeled their cart past the harbormaster's office, past the diner, past the boutique—the same three blocks they'd walked since moving to the coastal town two years ago. Most people in town knew them by their work: the careful welder, always polite, careful with words, defensive about the slight Eastern accent that never quite faded.

Ivan first appeared in the third week of June, standing near the pier with a leather suitcase and an expression of profound boredom. Akane saw him again at the diner, at the supply shop, then lingering outside the metalworks facility where Akane was welding a custom commission. Their eyes met briefly each time. Akane nodded, said nothing.

By July, Ivan was everywhere. Akane started to recognize his routine: morning walks along the breakwater, afternoons at the harbor, evening drinks at the waterfront bar. On the thirteenth of July, Ivan walked into the tide during sunset. Whether it was carelessness or intention, Akane didn't pause to wonder. They dropped their tools and ran, pulling the man from the water before the current could take him further.

Ivan coughed saltwater and gratitude. "Thank you. Thank you so much."

"It's nothing," Akane said, careful with the words, knowing how their accent would bend them. "Anyone would do it."

But Ivan wasn't anyone. He was a venture capitalist, he explained over coffee, visiting town to assess development opportunities. And he wouldn't stop trying to repay his debt.

He invited Akane to dinner. They declined politely. He offered to sponsor Akane's attempt at breaking the regional welding record—the thing Akane wanted most in the world, one more season to prove themselves. Akane refused, polite as always, but the rejection cost them something they couldn't name.

So they began stealing.

Nothing valuable. A key chain from Ivan's hotel nightstand. A cufflink. A receipt from his jacket pocket. Small, useless things that Akane could hold at night and feel the weight of the debt actually being paid—one tiny theft for every moment of unwanted gratitude. It was wrong, and Akane knew it, but the alternative was carrying the obligation forever.

Violet worked maritime police. She was in the harbormaster's office on the twenty-seventh of July when Ivan filed a theft report. Petty items, he said, but concerning. He'd narrowed it down: the welder from the shipyard. He'd recognized them near his hotel, seen them leaving with suspicious timing.

On the eighteenth of August—week six—Violet came to the shipyard at the end of Akane's shift. She didn't make it a scene. She was professional, almost kind, as she explained the charges. Theft. Multiple counts. She'd already reviewed footage from the hotel. Ivan had already provided everything she needed.

Akane's face burned. They wanted to explain about the rescue, about the debt that felt like drowning in reverse, about needing to steal back autonomy in small, useless pieces. But they only stood there, politeness a broken thing, their accent thick with shame as they said, "I understand."

Violet placed the handcuffs on gently. Justice, when it came, was neither hot with anger nor cold with cruelty—it was merely indifferent, a mechanism that turned regardless. Ivan watched from the harbormaster's office window, and Akane understood finally: the debt had never been personal. It had always been business.
