Fog had come to Aldenmoor the way it always did in the week before the Lantern Fair, rolling off the tarn at dusk and refusing to leave by morning, and Sebastian had walked into that fog off the six-fifteen coach with a folding rule in his coat pocket and no patience for it whatsoever.

He was fifty-five, a cartographer by trade, and he measured the world because the world, unmeasured, could not be trusted. He had come about a woman named Olivia — the innkeeper's daughter, twenty-six, gone eleven days now, last seen walking the millpond path the night the Fair committee voted, again, not to cancel. He had also come, though he told no one this, because his brother Julian's silver dividers — the ones their father had used to draft the very first survey of this valley, sold off by Julian nine years ago in the spiteful settling of an inheritance — were said to be sitting in a glass case in the Aldenmoor museum, two streets from where Olivia's shawl had been found snagged on a fence post.

He meant to buy the dividers back before he left. He meant to find the girl first. He did not believe, practically speaking, that these two errands would take longer than a fortnight.

They took six weeks.

The village constable, a heavyset, unhurried man named Max, gave him the file on the second day and then, against his own better judgment, let Sebastian keep it, because Sebastian would not give it back and would not stop asking for it either. "You don't delegate well," Max said, not as an accusation, just an observation, the way a man notes the weather. Sebastian agreed. He had never once in his working life handed a survey to an assistant and trusted the numbers that came back; he re-walked every line himself. He re-walked Olivia's life the same way — every house on the millpond path, every ledger at the inn, every committee member who'd voted to keep the Fair running despite a missing girl, because cancelling it, they all said, would ruin the season, the town, the harvest of visitors that kept Aldenmoor solvent through winter.

It was the innkeeper himself, John, who kept saying that loudest. Olivia's own father. Grief, in him, wore the face of civic duty — he chaired the Fair committee, and every week Sebastian pressed him, John had an answer ready, smooth as a lie rehearsed until it stopped feeling like one.

By the fourth week Sebastian had a map on the wall of his rented room, drawn to his own exacting scale, every sighting pinned, every timeline cross-hatched, and Max — who had learned by then not to offer to help, only to stand nearby with a lamp — watched him find the gap. Not a footprint. A number. The receipts from the Fair's advance ticket sales, which John had filed the morning after Olivia vanished, showed a sum that didn't move for three days and then jumped, all at once, in a way that only made sense if someone had needed money fast and had known exactly where to find it.

Olivia hadn't run off, embarrassed by a broken engagement, as the village preferred to believe so the Fair could proceed with clean conscience. She'd found the till short and confronted her father over what he'd taken from it, and John had walked her out along the fog-blind millpond path to talk her calm, and only one of them had walked back.

Sebastian brought the ledger and the timeline to Max on a Tuesday, laid them on the constable's desk in the same flat, unhurried voice he used to read out a bearing, and asked no one's permission before he did it, because that was not a thing he knew how to do.

They took John on the morning of the Fair itself, lanterns already strung along the green, the fog for once burned off by a hard clean sun. There was no scene. John did not weep or protest. He looked, when they walked him past the bunting and the trestle tables, like a man who had known for six weeks exactly which morning this would be, and had simply been waiting for the numbers to catch him, the way numbers always did.

Sebastian did not stay for the Fair. He collected Julian's dividers from the museum that same afternoon, paid for in full, and did not write to his brother to tell him so. Some accounts, once opened, are better simply closed.
