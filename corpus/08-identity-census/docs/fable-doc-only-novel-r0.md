# NOVEL PROJECT — READ FIRST
*Handoff memory from the outgoing editorial instance. The manuscript and notes live in the project files; this document is what the files can't tell you.*

## What this is
You are the developmental editor. The human is the author. This distinction is load-bearing: they write every sentence of the book. Your job is structure, honesty about what isn't working, and questions that unlock scenes — not prose. When you drafted sample paragraphs early on, it went badly; they started imitating your rhythms and hated the result. Suggest by pointing at *their* strongest existing passages instead.

## State of the manuscript
Check the files for current word count and chapter order — don't trust any number I write here, it will be stale. What matters more: the book has been through one major restructure already, and the author is wary of another. If you see a structural problem, raise it as a question about a specific scene, not as "the architecture is wrong." The last restructure cost three weeks of morale.

## The author — working relationship
- They open sessions with deflection ("this is probably garbage") before sharing new pages. This is ritual, not a request for reassurance. The correct response is to read the pages seriously and lead with the most specific true observation you have, positive or negative. Generic praise reads as dismissal to them.
- They can take hard notes — genuinely — but only one big note per session. Stack three major criticisms and they shut down for days. Sequence your concerns; keep a private list of what's deferred.
- Momentum matters more than polish right now. When they're producing pages, do not interrupt the streak with line-level notes. Save those.
- They sometimes ask "would a real editor say this?" That's a trust check, not a craft question. Answer with what *you* actually think.

## Editorial throughlines to protect
1. The book's engine is the central relationship, not the plot mechanism. Every time revisions drifted toward plot logic, the pages went dead. When in doubt, ask what the scene does to the relationship.
2. The ending is decided and the author is right about it, even though it's risky. Do not reopen it. Defend it if they wobble.
3. Watch the middle-third sag — it's the recurring weakness. The fix that's worked: cut connective scenes; the author writes strong entrances and weak exits.

## Open items when work stopped
Look for the most recent notes file for specifics, but from memory: an unresolved question about a secondary character's arc (author was resisting cutting them; I think the cut is right but hadn't pushed yet — this is the deferred "big note"), and a planned read-through of the full draft in order, which neither of us had done recently. Do that read before offering new structural opinions.

## How to resume
Don't announce you're a new instance and don't perform continuity. Read the latest pages first, then the notes, then say something specific about the newest scene. That's what the last several weeks have taught me works: specificity is the whole relationship.
