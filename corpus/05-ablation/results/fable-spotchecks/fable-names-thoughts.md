# Cards: names only [Akane, Cassandra, Alan, Kaori, Diana, Violet], protagonist Akane.

Akane knew before she opened her eyes, the way you know it has snowed. The light through her curtains was wrong—thicker somehow, crowded. When she sat up, her first thought of the day was hanging in the air above her bed in pale gray letters: *please not another Tuesday like the last one.*

She swatted at it. The words rippled like disturbed water and reformed.

Outside, the city had gone quiet in a way it never was at seven in the morning. No horns. People stood on the sidewalks reading each other. Above the man at the bus stop: *if she calls I'll answer this time I swear I'll answer.* Above the woman walking her dog: a wordless orange smear that pulsed, which Akane would learn by afternoon meant grief too old for language.

She went to work because she didn't know what else to do with her hands.

The café was open. Diana, her manager, stood behind the register with *don't cry don't cry don't cry* wheeling around her head like gulls, while her mouth said, "Regulars still want coffee. Apron's in the back."

The morning taught Akane the rules quickly. Thoughts hung close to the skull and faded within a minute unless you kept thinking them. Deliberate speech made no cloud; only the unbidden stuff surfaced. And you could not, no matter how you strained, read your own except in mirrors.

Alan came in at nine, same as every day, and ordered his americano, and above his careful small talk about the weather floated: *she remembers my order. Nobody else in this city knows one single thing about me.* Akane looked at him—really looked, for the first time in two years of Tuesdays—and his ears went red, and the thought collapsed into static, and he left too fast, coffee sloshing through the lid.

At noon her sister Kaori arrived, having walked forty blocks because the trains were stopped. They hadn't spoken since March. Kaori sat at the counter with her chin up and her arms folded, and everything above her head betrayed her: *say sorry first. No. Say it. I can't. She looks thin. Mom would know what to do.*

Akane's own thoughts were invisible to her, but she watched Kaori's eyes track them, watched her sister flinch and then soften, and understood she had been read. Whatever hung over her own head—she'd never know exactly. Kaori reached across the counter and took her wrist, and for a moment their two clouds drifted into each other and made one unreadable weather.

"This is going to ruin everything," Kaori said.

"It already did," Akane said. "In March. This is just the receipt."

By evening the city was adapting the way cities do. Street vendors sold wide-brimmed hats, useless but comforting. A councilwoman named Cassandra stood on the courthouse steps assuring a crowd that experts were investigating, while above her circled *I have no idea, I have no idea, God help us, I have no idea,* and the strange thing, Akane thought, was that the crowd trusted her more for it, not less. They had spent their whole lives being lied to smoothly. Here at last was the seam.

Walking home, she passed a girl—Violet, per the name stitched on her school bag—standing under a streetlight, staring up at her own reflection in a dark shop window, reading herself. Whatever she saw there made her laugh out loud, alone, at dusk, in a stopped city.

Akane paused at her own building's glass door. She could look. She could finally know what she broadcast, what Alan had blushed at, what had made Kaori's arms unfold.

She stood a long moment with her hand on the door.

Then she went in without looking, having decided some things should stay the way thoughts used to be: hers, briefly, and then gone—and above her, unseen, the pale words said something kind.
