The eyepiece was still warm in Adrian's hands when the sky above Hawes began to fill with other people's thinking. He stood on the observatory roof at dawn, twenty-five years old and grinning like a man half that age, watching soft ribbons of light unspool from every chimney in the village below—words, mostly, and pictures, drifting up from sleeping heads like smoke. Paula the baker's thoughts were a golden ladder of bread and worry. Old Katherine's were a slow grey river with a boy's face in it. It was, Adrian decided, the most beautiful catastrophe he had ever seen.

It was also, for him specifically, a disaster.

Because Adrian had a secret, and secrets in Hawes now hung above their owners like weather.

Three nights earlier, tracking a comet for the survey station at Blockley, he had watched a patch of stars go out. Not dim—out. A blackness with an edge to it, crossing the field of his telescope, moving against the rotation of everything else in heaven. He had logged it, deleted the log, and told no one. Who would believe the cheerful boy-astronomer, the one the village council called *lad* even when he brought them charts? He wanted to arrive with proof, with answers, with something they'd have to respect. So he smiled, and said nothing, and kept the darkness to himself.

Now the darkness kept trying to leave him.

He learned to manage it, the way everyone learned that first strange week. Nora at the post office taught the village her trick—think loudly about weather, about soup, about anything, and the deeper thoughts stay down like stones in a pond. Adrian became a virtuoso of surface cheer. His ribbon of light was all constellations and birthday cake, and underneath it, pressed flat with tremendous effort, the shape with the edge, growing in his eyepiece night after night.

By the third week it had crossed half the northern sky's worth of catalogued stars. By the fourth, the animals felt it: dogs facing north at dusk, sheep refusing the high pasture. Isabella, who ran the pub, stood in her doorway and said to no one, her thoughts flickering red above her, *something's wrong with the hills*. Adrian bought a round for the room and laughed too brightly and went home and did not sleep.

It was Talia who caught him. She was nine, Paula's girl, and children read the thought-light better than anyone, the way they read faces. She followed him up the observatory stairs in week five and stood in the doorway while he pretended to calibrate.

"There's a black thing under your birthday cake," she said. "It's been there for ages. It's getting bigger."

Adrian's cheerful ribbon shredded like cloud. And to his surprise, what he felt was not fear but relief, enormous and buoyant. "Yes," he said. "There is. Want to see?"

She did. Then Paula did, summoned by her daughter, and then, because thoughts travel faster than gossip now, half of Hawes was on the hill by midnight of the fifth week, watching the missing stars through his telescope, their collective light rising off the crowd in one braided column of alarm and—he noticed, his throat tight—trust. They looked at him. They asked him what to do. Nobody called him *lad*.

They had one week. They spent it well: cellars stocked, the old fell-shelters cleared, Katherine's shortwave raised to Blockley and even, through some ham-radio miracle of Nora's, a sister station in Ichikawamisato, where operators reported the same devoured stars in their own northern sky. Whatever it was, it was coming for everyone, and Hawes would meet it warned.

On the forty-second night, the ridge above the village went silent—no dogs, no wind. Adrian stood at the telescope with Talia's hand in his, and the column of the village's thoughts went suddenly, perfectly white, and he looked up from the eyepiece as the edge came over the hills at last: a cold pressing down like a hand, and the smell of scorched iron rolling ahead of the dark.
