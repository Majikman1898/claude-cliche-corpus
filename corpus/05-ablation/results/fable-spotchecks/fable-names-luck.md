The first thing Lily noticed was that the vending machine on Fourth Street had stopped eating quarters.

It had eaten quarters for eleven years. It was famous for it. And then one Tuesday it began dispensing two candy bars for the price of one, every time, for everyone — and three blocks east, a crane cable snapped over a farmers' market and killed no one only because the load swung into the one stall whose owner had stepped away. Luck wasn't gone from the world. It was pooling. Somebody had dug a channel.

Lily worked at the Bureau of Actuarial Anomalies, fourth floor, a department that officially did not exist and unofficially consisted of her, a man named Jonathan who kept dice in his desk, and a coffee machine with a grudge. Her job was to notice when probability misbehaved. For six years, it hadn't. Now the misbehavior had a shape.

"Look at the map," Jonathan said, pinning another red thread. "Sixteen catastrophic near-misses, all in a ring. And inside the ring—"

"Nothing," Lily said. "No wins. No coincidences. No found pennies." She'd interviewed a woman inside the ring who had flipped a coin four hundred times for her insomnia. Four hundred tails. The world's background hum of chance had been drained out of an entire neighborhood, the way sound leaves a room before a storm.

At the center of the ring stood a house with a green door, and in the house lived a man named Tony, who had, according to records, survived two lightning strikes, a bridge collapse, and a lottery he hadn't entered.

Lily went alone. Jonathan wanted to bring the dice; she told him to stay and roll them every hour, as instrumentation.

Tony answered the door looking terrible — grey, thin, flinching at nothing. Behind him the house was crowded with luck. She could feel it the way you feel static: her shoelace, untied for hours, chose that moment to slip perfectly back through its knot.

"You're stealing it," she said.

"I'm *holding* it," Tony said, and stepped aside to let her in, and this was either courtesy or the luck arranging itself so she'd hear him out. "Do you know what luck is, actuarially? It's slack. It's the margin the world keeps for error. And do you know what I found out, working nights at the seismic office?" His hands were shaking. "There's a bad day coming. A real one. I've seen the numbers walking toward us for two years. The world is going to need every ounce of margin it has, all at once, in one place — and instead it's out there being *spent*. On parking spots. On coin flips. On candy bars."

"You don't get to decide that," Lily said. "People are nearly dying out there. The near-misses—"

"Are misses," Tony said. "Because I gave the ring's edge everything I couldn't hold. I'm not a thief, I'm a *dam*."

Lily thought of the crane. The stall owner who stepped away. She had assumed leakage; it had been overflow, placed.

"A dam that breaks," she said slowly, "is worse than no dam."

"So help me not break." He held out his hand, and she saw his palm was worn smooth, featureless, the lines of it — life, heart, fate, whatever palmists called them — erased, spent as collateral. "I can't aim it. I can only hoard it. But you people, you *measure*. You could tell me where it needs to go."

Her phone buzzed. Jonathan: *dice just rolled sixes 40 times. also the seismic office called. also DON'T touch anything.*

Lily looked at the outstretched hand of the luckiest, unluckiest man alive, and understood that whatever she chose, the choosing itself would be the last unweighted event of her life — the last thing that happened to her by chance instead of on purpose.

She took his hand.

Somewhere across town, a coin came up heads.
