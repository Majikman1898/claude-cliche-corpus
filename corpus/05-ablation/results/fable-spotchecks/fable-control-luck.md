The actuaries noticed it first, though they didn't call it luck. They called it "anomalous negative variance clustering," and they published a paper about it that nobody read.

What they'd found was this: starting in March, the world's coin flips had begun coming up wrong. Not all of them. Just more than they should. Lottery jackpots went unclaimed for record stretches. Organ transplant matches, which arrive at a certain modest rate, arrived at a rate slightly less modest. Lightning found more golfers. The margin was thin — a half-percent here, a tenth there — but it was everywhere, in every dataset, like a tide going out one millimeter at a time.

Ilsa Voss, who audited casinos for a living, was the one who thought to ask where the variance was going. Bad luck for everyone should mean good luck for no one; probability is bookkeeping, and the books have to balance. So she did what auditors do. She followed the ledger.

It took her eleven months. The trail ran through reinsurance filings and racetrack payouts and a strange bloom of survived plane crashes in one particular region — because that was the tell, in the end. Somewhere, the tide had to be coming *in*.

The somewhere was a village in the Carpathian foothills, and the someone was an old woman named Marta, who kept bees.

Ilsa found her in her garden, netting off her hives for winter. The garden was absurd. Tomatoes in October. A walnut tree that had clearly been struck by lightning and clearly not cared. Marta had survived, Ilsa knew from the records, two wars, three cancers, and a bus that went into a river killing everyone else aboard.

"You're taking it," Ilsa said. "I don't know how, but you are. Do you understand what you're doing? A child in Manila didn't get a bone marrow match last week. The odds were good. The odds are never good anymore, because the odds are *here*, in your tomatoes."

Marta didn't deny it. That was the unnerving thing. She poured tea — the kettle, Ilsa noticed, had boiled at exactly the right moment — and said, "Sit. I'll show you something."

She brought out a shoebox of photographs. A man in a miner's helmet. A girl on a bicycle. A young soldier, blurred, laughing. "My husband, cave-in, 1969. My daughter, a driver who ran a light. My son." She tapped the soldier. "A shell that fell where a hundred shells had missed. I was a lucky woman my whole life, and everyone I loved paid my balance. That is how it works, you know. It has always been finite. You've only just noticed because I stopped being polite about it."

"So this is revenge? Against — what, statistics?"

"This is a withdrawal," Marta said. "I am taking back what I am owed, with interest, and when I have enough, I am going to spend it."

"On what?"

The old woman looked at the photograph of her son for a long moment. "There was one second," she said, "when the shell was falling and could still have drifted. Luck lives in seconds like that. There are seconds like that everywhere, all the time — a truck, a clot, a river. I can't go backward. But I have learned to put it where I want it, and I want it *there*. In those seconds. All of it. Every falling thing in the world, drifting, just once, all on the same day."

Ilsa did the arithmetic she had come there to do. One day of miracles, purchased with a year of the whole world's thinned fortune. The books would balance. The books always balanced; that was the horror and the mercy of them.

She should have reported it. Her flight home landed softly in a crosswind that should have been trouble.

In her report, she wrote: *anomalous variance, cause unknown, expected to self-correct.* Then she checked the date Marta had circled on the beekeeping calendar, and she waited, like everyone, to see what the world would survive that day.
