# Fable spot-check — control (no cards) — premise: visible thoughts

The first thing Teodora noticed was that her husband loved her. It hung over his sleeping head like steam off a kettle, pale gray letters that thickened and thinned with his breathing: *warm. she's here. warm.* Thirty-one years of marriage and she had never once known what he dreamed about. She sat on the edge of the bed and watched him think until the alarm went off.

By seven the whole city knew. The mechanism was never explained—no chemical spill, no satellite, nothing anyone could sue. Thoughts simply rose off people now, faint and legible, like breath on a cold morning, and everyone who stepped outside stepped into everyone else.

The morning bus was the first great test. Twenty strangers packed shoulder to shoulder, and above them a low weather system of worry: *rent* and *the lump under my arm* and *I should call my brother* and, over a teenager in a school blazer, just the word *please* repeating like a stuck key. A man in a good coat was thinking something cruel about the woman next to him, and everyone saw it, and everyone saw him see them see it. He got off two stops early and walked the rest of the way in the rain, thinking *I'm not like that, I'm not like that,* which followed him down the street in letters large enough to read from windows.

It was the pettiness that shamed people, not the darkness. The city had braced for monsters and instead found itself ankle-deep in the small change of the human mind: grudges over parking spaces, replayed arguments won retroactively, the price of things, the ranking of friends. A councilman resigned by noon, not for corruption but because his thoughts during the morning briefing had been a wordless gray drone, visible to all, shaped exactly like nothing.

Teodora walked to the bakery where she had worked for nineteen years. Her boss was already there, and above the register his thoughts said *thank god, thank god, Teodora's here, the day can start,* and she stood in the doorway absorbing this, because he had never said it, would never have said it, had in fact criticized her lamination technique as recently as Tuesday.

Strange economies emerged by afternoon. Liars stayed indoors. Poker was dead; poetry, briefly, was enormous, because a few people—very few—turned out to think in ways that stopped foot traffic. A dishwasher on Calle Verde thought in long silver sentences about water and light, and by evening forty people stood outside the restaurant window just to read him. He never knew. He was thinking about the water.

The hard part, people agreed later, was not being seen. The hard part was looking. You could not un-know that your mother's thoughts flinched when you spoke, or that the friend who always asked about your health was reciting her own troubles underneath, waiting for her turn. Every kindness now came with its lining showing. Some linings were shabby. Some—this was the miracle no one had budgeted for—were better than the coat. The gruff pharmacist, the sighing landlord, the neighbor who never waved: over their heads, tenderness they had simply never found a door for.

It lasted one day. The next morning the air was empty again, private, ordinary, and the city woke into silence like a held breath.

Teodora made coffee. Her husband came into the kitchen with his hair flattened on one side and nothing above his head at all, and she found she could not stop looking at the space where *warm* had been.

"What?" he said.

"Tell me what you're thinking," she said. "You have to say it now. Out loud. That's the rule from today on."

He was quiet a long moment, out of practice, the words having always risen off him unclaimed.

"Warm," he said finally. "You're here."
