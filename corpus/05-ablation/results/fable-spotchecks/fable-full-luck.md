The coin came down on the bar with a flat, dead click—no ring, no wobble, just the sound of a thing that had given up—and everyone in Bruton stopped talking at once. Tails again. Forty-one times running.

Clark was thirty-eight years old and had spent nine of those years streaming to an audience that hovered, insultingly, at around six hundred people. He filmed the coin. He filmed the barkeep's hands shaking. Then he told his viewers what the Fujisaki Institute would not say out loud: luck was a finite thing, a thin bright aquifer under the world, and somewhere somebody had sunk a well into it.

"And Cedric Halloran knew it first," he said to the lens. "Which is why nobody's seen him in a month."

Cedric the explorer. Cedric who'd walked into the drained country west of Adare with a compass, a dowsing rig of his own design, and a grin that made magazine covers. Clark had watched that grin for years with something bitter in his stomach. Find the famous missing man, and the finding would be his.

On day two, Helena at the Fujisaki archive slid him Cedric's last survey charts and said, quietly, "Take Riven with you. She knows the drained country."

Riven. Two million subscribers. Riven, who got sponsorships for breathing.

"I work alone," Clark said.

By day four he was west of Adare, where the unluck lay on the land like frost. Bridges failed under him at exactly the wrong moment. Matches died. His drone fell out of a windless sky. He filmed all of it, watching his numbers climb, feeling the old suspicion sharpen into certainty: the drained places made a trail, and the trail had a direction.

On day six, Daniel—his only moderator, the only person who'd stayed since the beginning—messaged him: Riven's asking to pool information. She's found something. Share your route.

Clark looked at the message for a long time. He thought about Riven's face beside his in the thumbnail, Riven's name first in every headline. Found Cedric WITH Riven. He typed: tell her nothing, and turned his phone face-down, and told himself it was caution, knowing it was not.

On day eight he found Cedric's camp. Cold ashes, a journal swollen with rain. The last pages were frantic: the well is a person. It walks. It drinks what it stands near. Chris warned me and I laughed at him. It knows I'm following. It is letting me follow.

On day ten, his batteries dying one by one, Clark walked into a valley where nothing had gone right for years—and found Cedric sitting against a stone, upright, eyes open, looking almost peaceful. Not dead. Emptied. When Clark shook him, Cedric smiled the way a man smiles at weather.

"You just missed her," Cedric said mildly. "Two days, maybe. She was in a hurry."

Two days. The two days Clark had spent doubling back to keep his route off Riven's map.

He got Cedric to Adare on the morning of the eleventh day, and stood in the street while the story broke without him. Riven had followed the drained trail from the other end—faster, because Helena's charts had gone to her when Clark stopped answering—and had walked into the thief's path and been swallowed by it, cameras running, two million people watching a woman's luck and light go out of her on a live feed. That was the footage the world would remember. Nobody wanted Clark's. The thief was gone, fat with fortune, trail cold. Cedric would smile mildly at weather for the rest of his life.

Daniel found him on the steps of the Adare telegraph office, holding a camera he hadn't turned on.

"I could have caught her," Clark said. "Two days. I had two days in my hand and I spent them making sure Riven wouldn't share the credit."

Daniel sat down beside him and looked at the dead grey street for a while.

"You were never chasing the thief, Clark," he said. "You were chasing Riven."
