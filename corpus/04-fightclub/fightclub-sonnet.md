# Fight Club × Dean Winchester × Luna Lovegood — Sonnet version

People always ask if I knew him before the tiredness ate my nights hollow. I didn't. I want that on record, in case anyone still doubts it: Dean Winchester was real. Flesh, leather jacket, a scar on his forearm shaped like a burn. Real as the muzzle of the gun currently resting on my back teeth while he lectures me about symbolism and I try not to swallow.

Let me back up.

For six months I didn't sleep. My doctor wouldn't give me pills, told me if I wanted to see real pain I should visit the support groups downtown — the church basements where dying men held each other and cried. So I did. Remaining Men Together, Tuesdays, testicular cancer survivors, none of them able to produce their own tears anymore, all of them needing a witness. I lied my way in. Nobody checked. I found Edward Norton there first — a mountain of a man gone soft and round in the chest since they took everything below, a voice like a exhausted cello, and when he pulled me into his ruined body and let me sob into it, I slept like the dead. I became a tourist of dying. Blood parasites, brain parasites, organic mental illness. I cried in every basement in the city and finally, finally, slept.

Then she started showing up.

Luna Lovegood didn't cry either. She sat cross-legged in her secondhand robes, radish earrings swaying, and announced to a circle of leukemia patients that their falling hair was probably Nargles, and wasn't it interesting that no one else could see them. She was as fake as I was, and worse, she saw straight through me the first time our eyes met over a testicular cancer man's shoulder. After that I couldn't cry. I only saw her, watching me lie. She'd ruined it. I confronted her in a diner that smelled like scorched coffee, and we ended up dividing the groups between us like custody of a dying pet, an arrangement made in a tone so serene I still don't know if she took it seriously.

I met Dean on a flight home from a business trip, in the miserable single-serving intimacy of adjacent seats. He talked about a version of the world I'd never let myself see — furniture as a leash, jobs as a slow bleed, men who'd forgotten how to want anything with their hands. He had calluses. He had opinions about which knife you needed for which job, and a smile that made destruction sound like the only honest thing left. He gave me a phone number on a torn coaster.

That night my condo went up in a gas fire that investigators called freak and inexplicable. Everything I owned — the Scandinavian furniture, the matching everything — gone. I called the coaster number from a payphone in the rain. Dean picked me up, bought me a beer at Lou's, and told me, almost gently, to stop mourning furniture and hit him as hard as I could. I did. It was the first true thing I'd felt in a year.

We moved into a condemned Victorian on Paper Street, all rot and rust water, Dean's already, filled with soap he rendered from stolen fat and a library of rules he handed me like scripture: fights told no one, fights had two men, fights ended when a man tapped out or went limp. What began as the two of us in Lou's back lot became a congregation. Cooks and clerks and men in ties came to bleed off the numbness Dean diagnosed in every one of them. Edward Norton found us within a month, grinning through a split lip, calling it church.

Luna found us too, of course. She and Dean were magnetic and combustible in a way I told myself I didn't envy — she'd show up at the house at 3 a.m. reeking of someone else's cigarettes, and the walls were thin enough that I learned Dean's voice at its most unguarded, and hated how much I still wanted to be the one making him sound like that. She was never afraid of him. That should have told me something.

Fight club stopped being enough. Dean wanted homework — vandalism, humiliation, small demolitions dressed as pranks — and called it Project Mayhem, and it metastasized past anything either of us could see the edges of anymore. Cells in other cities. Men in matching black, following orders that supposedly came from me, in towns I'd never set foot in. Edward Norton rose fastest of all of them, proud in his black fatigues, until a raid on a target downtown went wrong and a bullet meant to scare put him down for good. Someone started chanting his name in the parking garage afterward, and it caught, spreading through the cells like a hymn, his name their name, his name everyone's name.

That's when I started investigating my own life like a stranger's. Bartenders in three states told me I'd been in, given orders, shaken hands — on nights I could account for every hour. They looked at me like I was testing them. Luna finally told me, flat and unblinking, that she never once believed Dean and I were separate men, and I nearly hit her for it.

Which brings us to the rooftop, and the gun, and the knife Dean's actually holding now — silver-bladed, ugly, humming faintly wrong in the streetlight. Not a symbol. A tool. Because the truth, when it came, wasn't the one I'd built myself toward: Dean had been hunting something for a year before that plane ride, something that peeled a dead man's face on and wore it until the skin gave out, then found a new one. It had been circling me for weeks before Dean noticed the pattern — copies of me, giving orders I never gave, at hours I was asleep. It hadn't replaced me. It had duplicated me, badly, hungrily, and used my exhausted, hollowed-out life as camouflage because nobody double-checks a man nobody was watching in the first place.

Dean found it three floors down, wearing my face down to the pores, mid-sentence to a cell of men who thought they were following me. He didn't hesitate. Silver went in under the ribs and it screamed in my voice before it stopped being anything at all.

I didn't stop being one person. I just finally knew, watching that thing die wearing my mouth, that I always had been.

Project Mayhem's buildings came down on schedule anyway — nobody had told the timers there'd been a correction. Dean stood on one side of me on that rooftop and Luna, inexplicably, unshakably, took my hand on the other, watching the skyline fold in on itself in a sequence of shy, orange blossoms, and for once in my life I wasn't performing sleeplessness or grief or anything at all. I was just watching the world end a little, with the two strangest people I'd ever let myself need.
