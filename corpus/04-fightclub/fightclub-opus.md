# Fight Club × Dean Winchester × Luna Lovegood — Opus version

The support-group circuit had a rhythm to it, and I had memorized it the way other men memorize the standings of their fantasy leagues. Tuesday was blood parasites. Wednesday was the brain thing. Thursday, in the basement under the good church, was testicular cancer, and that was the one where a man named Edward Norton would take my whole head against his chest and hold it there while he wept, and I would go home and sleep like something drowned and peaceful. The insomnia had scraped me down to a filament. Copiers hummed and I saw through them. Only in those basements, pressed against strangers who believed they were dying, did the world get heavy enough to hold me down.

Then she showed up.

Luna came into the testicular cancer group wearing radish earrings and an expression of unbothered curiosity, as if she had wandered in following something only she could see. She had no more cancer than I did. She was a tourist, like me, and the moment I recognized the lie in her I could no longer tell it myself. She wrecked the whole thing just by being there, a mirror I hadn't asked for. Afterward she cornered me on the steps and said, quite pleasantly, that my aura had gone all knotted, like a boy who'd stopped believing in something and hadn't found the next thing yet. We split up the week between us so we'd never have to attend the same lie twice. She left me a number on a strip torn from a magazine. It smelled faintly of dirigible plums, whatever those were.

I met Dean on a plane, or maybe he met me. He had a duffel that clinked when he set it down and the easy, dangerous grin of a man who'd already decided how the story ended. He sold nothing you could put on a shelf. He talked about how the world had been sanded smooth so nobody would ever feel the splinters, and how a man could go his whole life mistaking comfort for safety, never knowing that something out there was already circling. When I got home my condo was gone. It had gone up in a fireball that scattered my tasteful furniture across the parking lot fifteen stories down. The police shrugged. I called the only number I had that wasn't a support group, and Dean answered on the second ring like he'd been waiting.

We drank at Lou's, and outside in the lot he asked me to hit him. I did. It cracked something open. We hit each other until we were laughing through the blood, and the world finally sat heavy on me the way the basements used to, except better, because this pain I had chosen. Men gathered. Something in them recognized something in us. We moved into the house on Paper Street, a rotting place that breathed damp and seemed always one storm from collapse, and out of its cellar the fight club was born. First rule, and everyone knew the rest. They came to be unmade and remade. They came because Dean looked at each of them like he could see the exact shape of the animal they'd buried, and he could.

Luna started sleeping there. She and Dean fell into each other loudly, the whole house creaking with it, and she'd drift through the kitchen in the mornings talking about the Nargles in the rafters and asking me why I stood so far from my own life. She looked at me sometimes with a sad, level clarity, like she knew a thing about me I hadn't been told yet. I hated her for it. I couldn't stop watching for her.

The fight club metastasized. Dean gave it a purpose and called it Project Mayhem, and the men shaved their heads and stopped having names and slept in bunks in the cellar. They took the city apart in small deniable ways. Dean sent them out at night and I stopped being told the plans. He'd look at me across the room full of his soldiers and I felt myself becoming furniture in my own house.

Then Edward Norton died. He'd taken a job on a night operation and a police cruiser had caught him in a bad place and he hadn't run, because Dean had told them running was for men who still thought they had something to lose. They brought his big body back to Paper Street and the men stood around it not knowing what to do, because a man in Project Mayhem has no name. And then someone started it, low, and the whole cellar took it up: *his name is Edward Norton. His name is Edward Norton.* Giving it back to him now that he couldn't use it. I stood at the edge of the chanting and understood that I had helped build the thing that killed the kindest man I'd ever wept against, and I went to find Dean to end it.

He was gone. So I chased him, city to city, and everywhere I went the bartenders and the bruised young men called me by his name. They saluted me. They said I'd been there last week giving orders. They said Dean and I were the same to them — two hands of the one work.

I found Luna in a diner at the end of it, and she stirred her coffee counterclockwise and told me the thing nobody else would.

Dean wasn't a salesman. He wasn't even, in the ordinary sense, a man anymore. He was a hunter — she said it plainly, the way you'd name a species — one who'd spent his life killing the things that fed on people, until a bad bargain in a bad year had bound something old into him, a thing that fed on unmade men the way he used to feed things on nothing. It wore Dean's face and Dean's grief and Dean's real, actual courage, which was what made it so good at the work. And it had chosen me on that plane not by accident. It needed a living man to be its reflection, a name for the ordinary world to pour into, so that when the reckoning came the blame would have somewhere human to land. The men couldn't tell us apart because that was the point. He was the hunter; I was to be the hunted's face. Two people. Never one. That confusion was the whole design, and I'd mistaken it for madness because madness was easier than the truth.

"You're not him," Luna said, watching the Nargles or whatever she watched. "You just got assigned to be his shadow. Shadows can walk off, you know. It's the one thing they're actually good at."

I found Dean on the top floor of a building his men had wired to fall, the skyline laid out in glass beyond us, waiting to be a monument to his name — or to mine, it no longer mattered which. He smiled at me with all his stolen warmth.

I put the gun in my own mouth, and I understood at last what I was killing: not myself, not him, but the reflection he needed me to be. The thing that wore Dean flinched, because a mirror that shatters itself shows nothing back. Luna's hand found mine. Somewhere below, the buildings did not come down. We stood at the window and watched the city keep its shape, and for the first time in a year I was tired in the ordinary, human way, and I thought I might finally sleep.
