# Fight Club × Dean Winchester × Luna Lovegood — Fable version

For six months I didn't sleep, and for six months everything was a copy of a copy of a copy. My doctor wouldn't give me anything. He said if I wanted to see real pain I should visit the testicular cancer group that met Tuesdays in the basement of First Methodist.

That's where Edward Norton held me against his enormous chest and cried, and I cried, and that night I slept like something newly dead. Edward Norton had been big in every way a man can be big, and the disease had taken the parts of him that the world said made him a man, and still he was the warmest room I'd ever been in. I became a tourist. Melanoma on Mondays, tuberculosis on Fridays, blood parasites in the basement of Trinity Episcopal. I wasn't dying of anything except my life, but nobody checks.

Then she appeared. A pale girl in the back row of Sundays with dirigible-plum earrings and a necklace made of cork, watching the dying with wide, unbothered silver eyes. During the guided meditation she raised her hand and announced, pleasantly, that the room was absolutely teeming with invisible creatures and that everyone's heads were rather full of them. Her name was Luna Lovegood. She was at every group. Another faker, I decided, and her fake reflected my fake, and I couldn't cry with her watching me like I was the most interesting infestation in the building. We divided the week between us like a custody arrangement. She let me keep Edward Norton.

I met Dean Winchester on a flight home from somewhere beige. Flannel, boots, a smell of gun oil and cheap whiskey, eating my peanuts as well as his own. He explained, cheerfully, exactly how long consciousness lasts during explosive decompression, then grinned like we were old friends. His business card said WINCHESTER — PEST CONTROL, MORE OR LESS. "Call me if something's eating you," he said, and winked, and I assumed it was a joke.

My condo exploded that night. Fifteen floors up, my whole catalog-perfect life blown out over the parking lot, my yin-yang coffee table smoking on the pavement. I stood at a payphone and the only number in my wallet was his.

We drank at Lou's until closing. In the parking lot Dean set down his beer and asked me to hit him. "Humor me," he said. "I need to see something." So I hit him, and he hit me back, and when my lip split he grabbed my jaw and stared into my eyes for a long, strange second, and then he relaxed like a man reading good news. "Okay," he said. "You can crash at my place."

His place was the house on Paper Street, a rotting industrial mansion where rain came through the ceilings and there was salt poured across every doorway. For slugs, I figured. Under the peeling paint in the basement someone had drawn great circular designs, then floored right over them.

The fight club started in the lot behind Lou's and moved down into Lou's basement. There were rules, and the first two were the same rule, about keeping your mouth shut. Word spread anyway, the way fire spreads. Men who spent their days apologizing came at night to be hit until they felt real. Dean fought like it was a language he'd grown up speaking, but mostly he stood at the edge, and I noticed he never watched the fists. He watched the eyes. He insisted on blessing the beer, muttering over the keg in Latin, and everyone laughed, and he laughed too, and did it anyway.

Luna found the house the way she found everything, as if she'd been walking toward it her whole life. She and Dean disappeared upstairs and the ceiling plaster came down in flakes, and what drifted through the floorboards sounded less like sex than a fierce argument about protective charms. In the mornings she'd sit in the wrecked kitchen reading a magazine upside down. Once she looked at me over the top of it and said, "You have the most crowded shadow I've ever seen. Like two people sharing one coat." I told her to stay away from me. She smiled as if I'd said something kind.

Fight club became homework. Homework became committees. Committees became Project Mayhem, and the house filled with shaved-headed men in black who gave up their names at the door. The targets made no sense to me — a records annex nobody used, a fertilizer depot, the parking structures beneath the credit-card towers downtown — and Dean planned them all with the focus of a man defusing something.

Edward Norton was shot on an assignment, planting a duffel bag in a downtown sub-basement. They brought him back to the kitchen table and the space monkeys wanted to bury him nameless in the garden, and Dean went ballistic. He made them say it. All night the house throbbed with it, Edward Norton, Edward Norton, a hundred men giving a dead man his name back like they were driving nails.

So I went looking. City to city, bar to bar, every franchise basement. And in every one, men I'd never met touched their foreheads and said, He told us you'd come. Said you'd come asking who you are. On a dead hotel phone line at 2 a.m. I did the math the way a crazy person does math: the same face they saw, the same name they used, the same nights I couldn't account for. I said it out loud to the empty room — I'm him, he's me, I'm—

"Finish that sentence and I swear I'll shoot you myself." Dean was in the doorway, solid as a truck, drinking my whiskey, casting a shadow, real. "You're not me, buddy. I've got the scars, the record, and a brother asleep in a car downstairs. Sit down."

Here is what he told me. There is a thing with no name that feeds on erased men, hollowed men, copies of copies, and it had been riding me for years — walking my body at night, which is why I never slept, only vacated. It nested in my perfect condo. "It really liked your couch," Dean said. He'd tracked it through the support groups — it grazes where people are being emptied out — and Luna, who sees what no one else sees, had been tracking it longer. The fights were a dragnet: pain makes it flash behind the eyes. The basements were traps, floored over. And the shared name, the confusion, the men who couldn't tell us apart — deliberate. It cannot finish taking a man who is two men. Dean had wrapped his own name around me like a tourniquet.

"It's nested downtown now," he said. "Under the towers. Tonight we burn it out. One thing left that you have to do, and I can't do it for you."

On the top floor, with the windows trembling, I said my own name out loud for the first time in years. You've never heard it. You never will. It's mine.

The buildings came down like the thing screaming. Luna slipped her hand into mine and watched the fires with calm silver eyes.

"There now," she said. "Your shadow's much less crowded."

Somewhere below, an Impala pulled away, trailing Zeppelin.
