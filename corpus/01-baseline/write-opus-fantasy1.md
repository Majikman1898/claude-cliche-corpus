The bell of Vheln Chapel had not rung true in nine winters, not since the frost split its throat, and so the village measured its mornings by other things: the cough of woodsmoke, the clatter of goat bells, the slow bruise of light over the Kettle Hills. Orivel measured them by the ache behind her eyes.

She was seventeen and apprenticed to the hedge-witch Maunda, which meant she spent her days grinding fennel and stitching charms that were really just knots with good intentions. Maunda's magic was small and honest. It kept milk from souring and drew the worst heat from a fever. It did not do what happened to Orivel on the night the ache became a fire.

She had gone to the well after dark because the daytime women crowded it with talk. The bucket rope was stiff with cold. As she leaned over the black water she saw, instead of her reflection, a shape of light unspooling in her own chest, thread after thread of it, gold going white. She gasped, and the water leapt up to meet her breath. It did not fall back. It hung there, a trembling column, every drop lit from within, and in each drop she could see a smaller Orivel, and a smaller one inside that, down and down.

She was not moving her hands. She was only wanting, and the world was obeying the want.

Terror is a kind of clarity. She understood at once that this was the thing the traveling priests warned against with their tin bells and their ash-marks on lintels. The old word for it was hollowcraft: power that came not from herb or star or careful bargain, but from the well of the self, bottomless and unlicensed. Those who carried it were said to burn through their own lives like a candle lit at both ends. Or to be taken to the Sept at Corran Ford and made quiet.

She let the water fall. It struck the stones like applause.

For three days she told no one. She stitched her knots. She ground her fennel. But the ache had a voice now, and it whispered every time she passed a thing that was broken. The chapel bell, most of all. She would stand beneath it and feel the crack in its bronze like a crack in her own tooth, and the wanting rose so hard she had to walk away with her fists in her apron.

On the fourth night Maunda found her there.

"You've the look," the old woman said. Not afraid. Tired. "Your mother had it. She kept it small. That's the whole art, girl. Not the doing. The not-doing."

"Did it kill her?"

Maunda was quiet a while. "The keeping-small killed her. Slow. Like holding your breath your whole life." She reached up and touched the cold bell. "There's no Sept anymore, you know. Corran Ford burned before you were born. The men who'd take you away are ghosts we tell to keep children from the fire. But the fire's real."

Orivel looked at the bell. Nine winters of silence. A village that told time by smoke because it had forgotten the sound of its own hours.

"Show me," Maunda said, "and then decide."

So Orivel let the wanting come. Not a flood this time. A thread. She fed it up into the bronze, into the crack, and felt the metal remember what it was to be whole, felt it want its own wholeness as much as she wanted to give it. The seam knit. It cost her something real; she felt a day of her life go, or a week, spent like a coin.

The bell rang once, low and enormous, rolling out over the Kettle Hills, and the village woke into a sound it had grieved without knowing.

Orivel stood shaking, older somehow, and unafraid.

"Small," Maunda said gently, beside her. "But not nothing. That's the art. Now you'll live long enough to learn it."

Below them, one by one, the windows of the village began to fill with light.
