The morning ferry to Callanish always ran ten minutes late, and Isla Rennick had learned to time her arrival on the harbour accordingly. She was leaning against the seawall, coffee cooling in her hands, when the man appeared beside her for the third time that week.

"You again," he said, not unkindly.

He was tall, sandy-haired, with the sunburn of someone new to the coast. She'd first seen him at the bakery on Tuesday, arguing gently with Morag about whether a bridie counted as breakfast. Then Wednesday, at the little museum above the chandlery, both of them the only visitors, circling the same glass case of whalebone tools without speaking. Now here, at the water.

"Small town," Isla said. "You start to memorize faces."

"I'm beginning to think the town is memorizing me." He shifted his rucksack. "Tom. I've been trying to find a cottage that apparently doesn't have an address. Just 'the blue door past the Rennick place.'"

Isla laughed before she could stop herself. "You're looking for my grandmother's old croft. I'm a Rennick. The blue door's mine now, actually. Or it will be, once I decide what to do with it."

Tom's face did something complicated, relief and embarrassment folding together. "Then I've been walking past your house for four days."

"You have. I watched you go by yesterday with a map upside down."

"It was not upside down."

"It was. The sea was at the top."

They fell into step without deciding to, walking the curve of the harbour past the upturned boats and the nets drying like enormous cobwebs. Tom explained he was a joiner from Perth, hired sight-unseen to assess the croft's roof for a buyer who'd purchased it at auction. He didn't yet know, he admitted, that the buyer and the seller were now standing on the same stretch of shingle.

"So you work for the man who bought my grandmother's house," Isla said slowly.

"I work for whoever pays the invoice." He glanced at her. "But I'd rather not fix a roof for someone who doesn't understand what they've bought. That place has a whole wall of names scratched into the doorframe. Heights. Dates going back to 1911."

Something tightened in Isla's throat. She knew that doorframe. Her own name was on it, aged six, aged nine, a crooked line at eleven where her grandmother's hand had slipped laughing.

"I sold it because I live four hundred miles away," she said. "Because keeping it felt like keeping a room I'd never sit in."

"And now?"

"Now a stranger keeps materializing to remind me it's still standing."

They reached the blue door. Up close the paint was flaking, the sea salt had chewed the lower planks, and the whole thing leaned with the particular tiredness of buildings that have outlived their people. Tom crouched and pressed his palm flat against the wood the way a doctor might, listening.

"The roof's sound," he said. "The frame wants love, not money. Whoever buys this could keep the doorway. Should keep it." He stood, brushing his hands. "I'll write that in the report. That it's worth saving as it is."

Isla looked at him, this man who kept crossing her path in a town too small to lose anyone in, who had spent four days walking past her history without knowing it was hers.

"There's a complication," she said. "The buyer pulled out this morning. Cold feet. So there's no roof for you to assess, and no invoice."

"Then I've come a long way for nothing."

"Or," Isla said, surprising herself, "you've come a long way to help me figure out whether I can keep it after all. I could use a joiner who reads doorframes."

The ferry sounded its horn across the water, ten minutes late, exactly on time. Tom smiled and set down his rucksack on the step, as though he had always meant to stay a while.

"Show me the wall of names," he said.
