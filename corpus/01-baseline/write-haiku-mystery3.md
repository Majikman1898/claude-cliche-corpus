The fog in Ashmore didn't lift. Detective Sarah Chen had been in the village for three days, and visibility never extended beyond fifty meters. The locals said it was normal for October, but their eyes suggested otherwise.

Margaret Tallow had vanished from her cottage on the northeast edge of town. Sixty-eight years old, widowed, lived alone. Last seen by her daughter on Tuesday morning. By Wednesday evening, when no one could reach her by phone, the search had begun. By Thursday, it had become official.

Sarah stood in Margaret's kitchen, cold now, the tea gone stale in its cup on the counter. No signs of struggle. No forced entry. The back door was unlocked—residents of Ashmore didn't lock doors, the local constable had explained with a shrug that irritated her. The front door was open when Margaret's daughter arrived. Just open, like Margaret had stepped out into the fog and simply forgotten how to return.

"People don't just disappear," Sarah said aloud.

"In Ashmore, they do." 

She turned. Thomas Ashmore stood in the doorway—no relation to the village, he'd explained when they met. He was the vicar, seventy-two, articulate, with the careful eyes of a man who'd heard too many confessions. He'd known Margaret for thirty years.

"What do you mean by that?" Sarah asked.

"Eighteen years ago, a boy named David Webb walked into the fog during the autumn mist. He was twelve. They found his shoe a mile away, but never David. Before that, in 1987, a woman went out for her evening walk. Just the walk she took every night. They never found her either." He paused. "There are others."

Sarah felt the weight of something in the air—not quite dread, but a thick presentiment that refused to dissipate like the fog. "You're suggesting something supernatural."

"I'm suggesting you ask yourself why a woman who has lived here for forty years, who knows every street blindfolded, would get lost in weather she's weathered a thousand times."

That night, Sarah sat in the Ashmore Inn, a modest establishment run by a woman named Phyllis who served excellent beef stew without comment. Sarah's notebook lay open before her, filled with statements and facts that didn't cohere. Margaret had no debts, no enemies, no reason to run. Her daughter confirmed her as unremarkable in temperament—steady, predictable, devoted to her garden and her church.

Unremarkable women didn't vanish.

Sarah returned to Margaret's cottage and let herself inside. The fog pressed against the windows like something alive. She moved through the rooms methodically, opening drawers, examining photographs. Margaret at a garden show. Margaret with her daughter's children. Margaret decades younger, a blonde woman in a sundress, smiling at someone just out of frame.

In the bedroom, Sarah found the journal in the nightstand.

Margaret's handwriting grew increasingly erratic across the pages, moving backward in time as Sarah read. October—I hear it calling. September—the fog speaks to me. August—I remember now. I was supposed to go. Thirty years ago. I said no, and it's never forgotten.

Sarah sat on the bed. Her phone showed no signal—the fog seemed to swallow such things. She thought of Thomas's careful eyes, his catalogue of the vanished. 

In the fog beyond the window, something shifted. A shape moving parallel to the glass, too large to be human, too deliberate to be imagination. Sarah's hand went to her service weapon and found she couldn't draw it. The fog had weight now, pressing against the cottage walls.

She understood then. The fog hadn't taken Margaret. Margaret had answered something that had called to her for decades, patient and persistent, waiting for the moment she would finally say yes.

Sarah pulled out her phone to call for backup, but already she could hear it too—a sound like singing, like waiting, like inevitability.
