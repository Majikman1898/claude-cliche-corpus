The key stuck in the lock the same way it had forty years ago. Maya jiggled it, felt the familiar resistance, then the click. The door swung open onto the familiar smell of old wood and dust, and something else—lavender sachets, probably decades old, hanging somewhere in the dark hallway.

She set down her suitcase and stood on the threshold of Whitewood House, unable to fully cross into the foyer. The afternoon light stretched across the hardwood floors in long stripes, illuminating everything she'd been avoiding.

Her mother's house. Her mother's inheritance, now hers.

The funeral had been three weeks ago in Portland. Maya had returned to Boston the same day, to her apartment, to her job, to the blessed anonymity of her own life. She'd let the lawyers handle the paperwork. Let her brother David make the decisions about selling. She couldn't bear to think of this place, let alone visit it.

But David had called yesterday. "You need to come back. There are things to decide. Besides, I think you need to be here."

She'd wanted to tell him he was wrong. She wasn't the sentimental one. That had been their mother—the one who kept every birthday card, every photograph, who couldn't throw away her children's old school papers. Maya had spent twenty years building a life that required nothing from the past.

Yet here she was.

The living room was exactly as she remembered. The wingback chairs positioned toward the fireplace. The heavy oak side table. The bookshelf with its particular architecture of leather spines and faded dust jackets. On the mantle, the framed photographs: Maya at twelve with a missing front tooth, David in his high school soccer uniform, their parents on their twentieth anniversary, all three of them at Christmas 1998.

She found herself in the kitchen, drawn by an old instinct. The refrigerator—the ancient yellow one their mother had always meant to replace—still hummed in the corner. Maya opened it. Empty, of course. David must have cleared it out. But the magnet collection on the door remained: a small red barn, a lobster, postcards from Acadia, from Cape Cod, from every place their mother had loved.

On the counter sat a single envelope with her name written in their mother's careful script. Maya's hands trembled as she opened it.

*Darling, if you're reading this, it means you've finally come home. I know how hard that is for you. You've always been so determined to look forward, never back. I never understood that desire to shed the past like a coat you've outgrown. But I'm leaving you this house anyway, because I think sometimes—maybe just once—you might want to put that coat back on and remember who you were before you became so busy being who you thought you needed to be. The roses still bloom in June. The floorboards in my room still creak at night. The view from the attic window still shows you the mountains. Come home when you're ready. I'll be waiting.*

Maya sank into her mother's chair at the kitchen table and let herself cry. Not the controlled, appropriate tears from the funeral, but deep, gasping sobs that seemed to come from somewhere in her chest she'd locked away years ago.

After a long time, she rose and walked upstairs. The hallway was lined with photographs—her life in sequence. In her mother's room, the quilted bedspread was folded neatly across the foot of the bed. On the nightstand, a book lay open, page-down, waiting.

Maya climbed the narrow stairs to the attic. The afternoon sun slanted through the small window, and there, just as her mother had promised, were the mountains—the same mountains she'd stared at as a girl, dreaming of escape.

She sat on the dusty floor and began to remember.
