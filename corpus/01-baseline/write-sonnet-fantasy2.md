The wind came down from the Kestrel Peaks three days before the frost fair, and it brought with it the smell of burnt copper — which was how Senna Vail knew something in the old cellar had woken up.

She was seventeen, the youngest apprentice ever taken on by Mistress Odalys of Thornmere, a village so far from the capital that the royal roads gave up twenty miles short of it and became goat paths. Thornmere had no grand towers, no academies, only a scattering of stone cottages, a chapel bell that rang flat, and Odalys's crooked house at the village's northern edge, where Senna had spent four years learning to coax fire from flint-light and to mend torn cloaks with a whispered stitch-charm.

She was not supposed to go into the cellar. Odalys had said so plainly, the way she said most things — once, and without patience for a second asking. But the copper smell had seeped up through the floorboards of the workroom for two nights running, and curiosity in Senna had always been a stronger current than obedience.

The door was warded, but the wards were old, stitched by a hand less careful than her own, and they parted for her like wet paper. Stairs of packed earth went down further than a cellar had any right to go. At the bottom, in a room lined with jars of sealed dark, she found a stone the size of a loaf of bread, veined with light that moved like something breathing.

She should have left it there.

Instead she touched it, and the light did not simply glow — it *answered*, pouring up through her fingers and into the marrow of her, and for one unbearable moment Senna understood why the wards existed. This was not village magic, not flint-light or stitch-charms. This was old power, the kind the Concord of Ashenmoor had outlawed three centuries ago after it hollowed out a kingdom from the inside, the kind no living mage in Thornmere had ever been meant to touch. It knew her. It had been waiting, patient as stone, for someone careless enough to open the door.

She wrenched her hand back, and the cellar went dark again, but something stayed lit inside her, a coal she could feel banked behind her ribs.

Odalys found her an hour later, sitting on the workroom floor with both hands pressed flat to the boards, as if she could hold the whole house down by will alone.

"You went below," the old woman said. It was not a question.

"I didn't mean—"

"No one ever means to." Odalys sat across from her, slow with the particular carefulness of someone whose joints had opinions. "That stone was sealed by my teacher's teacher. It is called a hearth-core. It does not grant power. It *is* power — raw, unchosen, the kind that eats the hand that holds it if the hand is not ready." She looked at Senna for a long moment. "You are not ready. But you are not nothing, either, or it would not have answered."

Senna's voice came out smaller than she wanted. "Am I in trouble?"

"You are in danger," Odalys said. "Which is worse, and more interesting." She rose, joints creaking like door hinges, and held out her hand. "We could reseal it and pretend this never happened. Thornmere has survived a long time on pretending. Or you can spend the next years learning what almost swallowed you today, slowly, properly, so that one day it answers to *you* instead of the other way around."

Outside, the frost-fair bell rang its familiar flat note, and villagers were stringing lanterns between the eaves as if the world had not just tilted on its axis beneath their feet. Senna thought of the coal banked behind her ribs, warm and patient and utterly unafraid of waiting longer than she could imagine.

She took the old woman's hand and stood.

"Properly, then," she said.
