The dust cloud around Vesper-9 wasn't supposed to have edges.

Captain Anaya Reyes had flown the *Marlowe's Gambit* through worse — solar flares off Kepler's Rim, a pirate ambush near the Chalice Belt — but she had never seen a nebula that stopped like it had been cut with a knife. One moment the viewport showed the usual amber smear of ionized gas, and the next, nothing. A perfect black hemisphere, three kilometers across, hanging in the void like someone had erased a piece of the universe and forgotten to fill it back in.

"Tell me that's a sensor glitch," she said.

Oskar Lindqvist didn't look up from his console. Six years as her navigator had taught him that panic was a luxury item, one he couldn't afford. "Wish I could. I've run diagnostics four times. Whatever that is, it's real, and it's not emitting anything. No heat, no light, no radiation. It's not absorbing our scans either — they just stop existing when they hit the boundary."

"Define 'stop existing.'"

"I mean the pulse goes in and doesn't come back. Not reflected, not absorbed. Gone."

In the back of the cockpit, Priya Chen unclipped herself from the engineering station and drifted forward, her tablet clutched to her chest like a shield. "Captain, I want to log an objection before anyone gets any ideas about flying closer."

"Noted," Anaya said. "Objection logged. Oskar, distance?"

"Eight hundred kilometers and closing, unless you want to reverse thrust."

She didn't. That was the thing about the *Gambit* — it was a survey ship, built for exactly this, for finding the things nobody else had found first. Reyes had signed on three years ago because she wanted to see something that didn't have a name yet. Well. Here it was.

"Hold position at five hundred," she said. "Priya, get me a spectral breakdown of the boundary. Oskar, I want passive scans only — nothing that pokes it."

The ship drifted closer, engines whispering. On the main display, the hemisphere grew, swallowing the starfield behind it in a way that made Anaya's stomach twist — not because it was dark, but because the dark had a texture to it, a faint rippling like heat haze over asphalt, except there was no heat, no air, nothing to distort.

"Got something," Priya said, voice tight. "The boundary isn't static. It's breathing. Contracting and expanding, about four centimeters a second. Regular as a heartbeat."

"Could be gravitational lensing," Oskar offered, though he didn't sound like he believed it.

"Lensing doesn't pulse in four-four time," Priya said. She turned her tablet around. A waveform, subtle but unmistakable, rhythmic. "I've never seen a natural phenomenon do this."

Anaya stared at the black shape on the screen, and for a moment she let herself imagine what artifact might make a hole that pulsed like a living thing. Then she caught herself — that was the story she wanted, not the one in front of her.

"Log it, don't chase it," she said. "Oskar, bring us about. We're not equipped to investigate a — whatever that is. We radio it in, we mark the coordinates, and we let a research vessel with actual containment protocols take the risk."

"You're passing this up?" Priya sounded almost disappointed, for all her earlier objections.

"I'm passing it on," Anaya said. "There's a difference. Something that doesn't reflect light and breathes on a four-count isn't a discovery. It's a message. And messages deserve to be read carefully, not grabbed at."

She watched the black hemisphere recede on the aft display as Oskar brought the *Gambit* around, the rhythm of its unseen pulse staying with her long after it slipped back into starlight — patient, steady, and utterly indifferent to whether anyone had been watching.
