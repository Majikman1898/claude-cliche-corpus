The fog came off the moor like something with intent, and by the time Inspector Dorne reached Wren's Hollow it had swallowed the church spire whole. He drove the last mile at a crawl, headlights bouncing back at him, and parked where a woman in a green mackintosh stood waiting at the roadside as if she had grown there.

"You're the one they sent about Ellis Mabry," she said. Not a question.

"And you are?"

"Constance Fell. I keep the shop. I'm the one who telephoned." She turned without waiting and he followed her into the murk, past cottages that appeared and dissolved, until they reached a low house with its door standing open. "He's been gone four days. His breakfast still on the table."

Dorne stepped inside. The kitchen smelled of cold ash and old milk. A chair lay overturned. On the table a plate held a rind of toast gone hard as slate, and beside it a teacup, full, skinned over.

"Nobody heard anything?"

"In this?" Constance gestured at the window, at the white nothing pressed against the glass. "You could scream in the Hollow and the fog would eat it before it crossed the lane. That's the trouble with us. We hear only what stands beside us."

He walked the room slowly. A man does not leave a full cup and a fallen chair by choice. Yet the door had no forced marks, and Mabry's coat still hung on its peg, his boots still by the hearth. Whatever took him, he had gone out into the moor in his stockinged feet.

"Did he have enemies?"

"He had debts," Constance said. "And a brother, Sorrel, who wanted the land. They quarreled the night before. The whole lane knew it, though nobody would say so to your face."

Dorne knelt by the hearth. In the grey drift of ash something pale caught the light. A button, bone, with a scrap of dark thread still through the eye. He turned it in his fingers. Not from Mabry's coat, whose buttons were horn, every one accounted for.

"Where does the brother live?"

"Up the top lane. But you'll not find it alone. The fog turns you." She hesitated. "I'll take you. But there's a thing you should know first, Inspector. Sorrel came to my shop yesterday morning, white as the weather, and bought rope and lime. Said it was for the fences." She met his eyes. "We've no fences left up there worth mending."

They went out again into the smother. Dorne kept close to the green of her coat, the only colour in the world. The lane climbed. Somewhere a sheep coughed like an old man. And then, through a thinning in the vapour, he saw the shape of a barn, and outside it a mound of fresh-turned earth, the lime scattered pale across it like frost that would not melt.

He stopped. Constance stopped with him.

"You knew," he said quietly. "You knew what was here."

"I suspected." Her voice did not shake. "That's why I called the city, and not Sergeant Pryce, who is Sorrel's cousin and would have found nothing. I could not dig it up myself. I needed a witness the Hollow couldn't buy."

Dorne looked at the mound, at the barn, at the woman who had led him true through a fog designed to lose men. Behind the barn door something moved, and a bolt slid.

"Whatever's in that ground," he said, "we uncover it together, in daylight, with the both of us to swear to it."

"There'll be no daylight today," said Constance Fell. "But I'll stand as your second, and the fog can witness the rest."

He took out his torch and started toward the barn, and the white closed over them both, and held its breath.
