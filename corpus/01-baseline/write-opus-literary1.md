The wind off the estuary still carried salt, the way it had when Marisol was small enough to ride the gate as it swung on its hinge. She stood at the end of the lane now with her suitcase handle sweating in her palm and looked at the house her grandmother had left her, and she understood for the first time that a house could look like a held breath.

Nan had been gone eleven weeks. The funeral in March had been a blur of umbrellas and casseroles and cousins she barely knew, and Marisol had flown back to the city the next morning because there were meetings, because there was always something. The key had arrived by post in an envelope with her name written in the careful, slanted hand she would now never see form her name again. She had kept the envelope in a kitchen drawer for two months, unable to open the door it belonged to.

The lock gave with a familiar shudder. Inside, the air was cool and close and smelled of woodsmoke and lavender and something underneath both that was simply Nan, that no cleaning would ever wholly lift. Marisol stood in the hallway and let the quiet press against her ears.

Everything was where it had always been. The barometer on the wall, forever stuck on Change. The coats on their hooks, one of them Nan's green wax jacket, the sleeves still holding the shape of her arms. On the hall table sat a bowl of buttons, thousands of them, sorted by no system Marisol had ever cracked, and beside it a pair of reading glasses folded and waiting, as if their owner had only stepped into the garden to check the beans.

She had come to sort and to sell. That was the plan she had told everyone, told herself, a clean and sensible plan. A young family would love this place. She would take a few keepsakes and let the rest go. She walked through the rooms rehearsing it, opening drawers, and in the kitchen she found the tins she remembered, and in the third tin down, under the flour, an envelope with her name again.

Inside was a single card. *Sweetheart,* it read, *if you're reading this you know I've gone on ahead. Don't you dare be sad in my kitchen. Put the kettle on. The tea is where it always was. Sit a while before you decide anything.*

Marisol laughed, or something like it, a sound that broke halfway and became the crying she had not managed at the funeral, had not managed on the plane or in the eleven weeks of drawers left unopened. She sank into the chair at the table, Nan's chair, the one with the cushion worn to the shape of a body, and she wept until the light through the window went from white to gold.

When she was done she did as she was told. She found the tea. She filled the kettle and stood listening to it climb toward its whistle, and while she waited she looked out at the garden gone wild, the beans bolted, the roses reaching over the path, and she felt something in her chest loosen a single notch.

She did not decide anything that evening. She drank her tea and washed the cup and set it on the rack, and she slept in the narrow back bedroom under a quilt Nan had made from thirty years of worn-out dresses, and in the morning the estuary light came in silver and the birds were loud and ridiculous.

Marisol went out and stood in the overgrown garden in her socks. The dew was cold. She pulled one weed, then another, and by the time the sun was properly up she had cleared a whole bed and her hands were black and her knees were soaked and she was, to her own great surprise, humming.

She stayed. Not forever, perhaps. But the sign never went up, and the buttons stayed in their bowl, and the kettle learned her hand as it had learned Nan's, and the house, at last, let out its breath.
