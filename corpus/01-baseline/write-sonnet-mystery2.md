The fog reached Alderbeck three days before Mira Voss did, and by the time she stepped off the last bus at the crossroads, the two had settled into the village like an old married couple, comfortable and unwilling to leave.

She'd been sent because the local constable, a heavyset man named Purcell, had put in a call to the regional office that read more like a confession than a report: Edwin Talle, sixty-one, dairy farmer, gone eleven days, and nobody in Alderbeck seemed especially eager to look for him.

"He kept to himself," Purcell said, walking her along the lane toward Talle's cottage. The hedgerows dripped. Somewhere off to the left, a cow lowed once and went silent, as though it had thought better of it. "Wife passed two winters back. No children. He fed his herd, he drank at the Anchor on Fridays, and that was the whole of him."

"And nobody noticed he'd stopped doing either."

"His herd noticed." Purcell didn't smile. "That's rather the trouble. Tom Wickery found the cows unmilked and half-starved. Talle wouldn't have let that happen if he were breathing."

The cottage smelled of cold ash and spoiled milk. Mira worked the rooms methodically — a kettle furred with rust-colored scale, a calendar with the ninth of the month circled twice, hard enough to tear the paper. Under the circled date, in a hand gone shaky at the end: *ask her about the boundary again.*

"Her who?" Mira asked.

Purcell's jaw tightened. "Agnes Fenn. Keeps the sheep on the rise above his lower field. There's been a dispute over the fence line going back to his father's time."

The fog thickened as they climbed toward the Fenn croft, swallowing the lane behind them so thoroughly that Mira found herself watching her own footprints to be sure the ground was still there. Agnes Fenn was somewhere near seventy, spare and weathered, and she watched them come up the path with the flat patience of someone who had rehearsed this conversation already.

"He came up here the night of the ninth," Agnes said, before Mira had asked a single question. "Shouting about the survey markers, same as always. I told him what I always tell him — go to the county office if he wants it settled — and I shut the door on him. That's the last I saw of Edwin Talle."

"Convenient, that door."

"Convenient would be lying about it." Agnes's mouth was a hard line. "I've nothing to hide, girl. Search the croft if you like."

Mira did, and found nothing — no blood, no struggle, no sign that violence had passed this way. But behind the sheep pen, half-buried where new-turned earth hadn't quite settled to match its surroundings, her boot caught the edge of something that wasn't stone. A boundary marker, iron and old, pulled from wherever it had stood for eighty years and dropped here, out of place, like a tooth knocked from its socket.

She didn't dig further that afternoon. Instead she asked Agnes a different question, one that had nothing to do with fences: "When did your husband die, Mrs. Fenn?"

The old woman's composure didn't crack so much as settle, the way a held breath finally goes out. "Fourteen years ago March. Why?"

"Because that marker's been moved twice. Once a long time ago, and once about eleven days back — by someone who wanted it to look like it had always sat where Talle said it shouldn't." Mira crouched by the disturbed earth. "You didn't shut the door on him that night, did you. You went down to that field together to argue it out once and for all, and something happened that wasn't an argument anymore."

Agnes Fenn didn't answer, but she didn't have to; she was already looking past Mira, down the slope, to where the fog thinned for one unhelpful moment over a field that had never been searched, only assumed empty. By evening, with lanterns and shovels and Purcell's reluctant men, Alderbeck's fog gave up what it had been keeping so carefully to itself.
