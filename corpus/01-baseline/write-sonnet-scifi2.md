The signal came in at 0347 hours, ship time, three days out from anywhere.

Vex Amara had the watch when it happened, which meant she was the one who saw the reading before it made sense to anyone. The *Halcyon Drift* was a survey vessel, six crew, running a slow loop through the Corvid Reach mapping gravitational anomalies nobody expected to find anything in. That was the job. Nothing ever happened out here. That was, in fact, the entire appeal.

"Captain, you should look at this," Vex said into the comm, and tried to keep her voice level.

Captain Osei Mbeki arrived in his sleep clothes, hair pressed flat on one side, and looked at the screen the way a man looks at a bill he doesn't remember incurring. The object — if it was an object — sat four hundred thousand kilometers off their bow. It read, on every instrument they had, as nothing. No mass. No albedo. No thermal signature. And yet the long-range camera showed a shape, a rough ovoid about the size of a small moon, matte black against a field of stars that seemed, around its edges, very slightly wrong — smeared, like a thumb dragged through wet paint.

"Could be sensor fault," said Teodor, the engineer, arriving with his shirt inside out. He said this the way people say a thing they don't believe, to see if saying it makes it true.

It was not a sensor fault. They ran diagnostics for two hours and the smear stayed. Priya Nandagopal, their physicist by training and their conscience by temperament, sat very still in front of the readout for a long time before she spoke.

"It's not emitting light," she said. "It's bending it. Whatever's out there, it's got the mass of something you'd need a sun to make, compressed into a shape you could park a shuttle inside. And it's not gravitationally pinned to anything. It's just sitting there."

"Drifting," Osei said.

"No," Priya said. "Sitting. There's a difference. Drift has a vector. This doesn't have one that I can find."

They voted, as ships like theirs were required to, on whether to approach. It was four to two, with Teodor and the quiet communications officer, Bram Solheim, dissenting, and dissenting hard. Osei broke no ties himself, on principle, but he backed the majority, because that was also the job.

They closed to twenty thousand kilometers before anything changed. Then all six of them, in different parts of the ship, doing different things, stopped at the same moment, because a single low tone had begun sounding from every speaker aboard, no source they could isolate, a note like a hand drawn once across a wine glass and left ringing.

Vex, who was nearest a porthole, saw the anomaly bloom — not brighten, exactly, but *unfold*, the way a photograph left in developer too long starts to reveal a second image underneath the first. For four seconds, she would later swear, she could see straight through it to a sky that was not their sky: wrong constellations, a banded gas giant too close and too orange, and something small and swift crossing that unfamiliar dark that might have been a ship, or a bird, or nothing at all.

Then it closed up, like a fist, and was only black again.

Bram's board lit with three hundred years of compressed telemetry arriving and departing in under a second, gone before he could do more than confirm it had existed. Nobody's instruments agreed with anybody else's afterward. The mass reading was gone entirely. The object drifted now, with a vector, a perfectly ordinary asteroid-sized rock tumbling end over end, radiating a very ordinary, very cold nothing.

They logged everything. They argued about the tone for a week, about the sky through the crack for longer than that, about whether to file the report as anomaly or malfunction, knowing that either word was a kind of lie.

Osei stood alone at the port window on the last night before they turned for home, watching the black where the black thing had been, and thought — not for the first time, but more clearly than before — that the universe did not owe them an explanation, and had, in its way, been perfectly polite about not providing one.
