The fog came in off the marshes the night Edda Vance went missing, and it hadn't lifted since. Detective Inspector Rourke Calloway had been in Thistlewick three days, and in that time he'd seen the sun exactly twice, both times as a pale coin behind grey wool.

The village sat in a fold of the hills like something dropped and forgotten, sixty-some souls clustered around a green with a dry fountain at its center. Edda Vance, nineteen, had walked out of her mother's cottage on a Tuesday evening to fetch water from the well at the crossroads and had not come back. No footprints past the well. No sound. The lamplighter, a stooped man named Pruitt who'd have finished his round about that time, swore he'd seen nothing, though he admitted the fog had been thick enough that night to swallow a cart and horse whole.

Calloway had worked disappearances in the city, where people vanished for reasons that were sad but ordinary — debts, lovers, despair. Villages were different. Villages had memories that went back generations, and secrets that got passed down like inherited furniture, dusted but never opened.

He found the well easy enough to reach and impossible to see from more than ten feet in any direction. The fog didn't roll here so much as sit, thick as a held breath. He crouched at the stones and found a single hairpin wedged in the moss, tarnished brass, shaped like a swallow.

"That were her mother's," said a voice behind him. He turned to find a woman of perhaps seventy, wrapped in a shawl the color of wet slate, appearing out of the grey as though she'd stepped through a curtain. She introduced herself as Mrs. Aldous Fenn, though everyone called her Nan Fenn, and she'd lived by the crossroads longer than the crossroads had been paved.

"The Vance girl was engaged," Nan Fenn said, "to a boy who isn't from here. City boy. Came out selling insurance two summers back and never quite left."

That detail hadn't been in the file. Calloway found the city boy — a mild, sweating man named Julian Pratt, lodging above the tavern — by suppertime, and found him lying almost immediately, in the small, panicked way of a man who has never had to lie to a policeman before. Pratt said he'd been in his room all evening. But the innkeeper's daughter, a sharp girl named Ivy, mentioned offhand that she'd seen him come in past ten, boots soaked through past the ankle, though it hadn't rained in a week.

The marsh, then. Calloway went out with a lantern and Ivy as a guide, since she knew the safe ground and he didn't. Two hundred yards past the well the ground went soft and treacherous, and there, half-sunk near a stand of dead reeds, he found a woman's shoe, and twenty feet beyond it, caught on a root, a strip of blue cloth that matched the description of Edda's dress.

They did not find Edda that night. But they found her the next morning, alive, shivering in the loft of an abandoned drying shed a half-mile further on, where she'd fled after a violent quarrel with Pratt — who had, it turned out, another fiancée in the city, a fact Edda had learned three days prior and confronted him with at the well, before running blind into the fog rather than marry a liar.

Pratt was arrested for what he'd done after, trying to silence her with more than words, a bruise blooming green and yellow along her jaw that he could not explain away. Edda would recover. The village, Calloway suspected, would talk about it for thirty years.

He left Thistlewick on the fourth morning, and for the first time since he'd arrived, the fog had thinned enough that he could see the hills rising green and ordinary in the distance, as if the whole strange business had simply been waiting for daylight to end.
