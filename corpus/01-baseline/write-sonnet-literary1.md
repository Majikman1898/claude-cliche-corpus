The gravel of the driveway hadn't changed. That was the first thing Maren noticed, stepping out of the rental car with her knees stiff from the drive—the way the stones still shifted and clicked under her shoes exactly like they had when she was eleven, running barefoot to beat her grandmother to the mailbox. Everything else about Birchcombe Road felt smaller, but the sound of the gravel was correct, was faithful, and it undid her a little before she'd even reached the porch.

The lawyer had mailed the keys three weeks ago, in a padded envelope with a sticky note that said *Sorry for your loss—call with questions.* She hadn't called. She'd just driven, eventually, when the silence of her own apartment in the city got to be worse than the thought of her aunt's empty house in Colby Falls.

Her aunt Odetta had lived alone at 14 Birchcombe for forty-one years, and had died alone too, of a heart that gave out on a Tuesday in April while she was watering the ferns on the back porch. A neighbor had found her. Maren hadn't been able to get away in time for anything but the funeral, and even that she'd attended in a fog, shaking hands with people who called her "little Mare" and asking her own reflection why she felt so little, so numb, when she'd loved the woman.

The grief had come later. It always did, with Maren. It waited until the timing was inconvenient and then arrived all at once, like weather.

The key stuck in the lock the way it always had—you had to lift the door slightly by the knob while you turned—and the smell that met her in the front hall was so exactly her aunt's house, cedar and old coffee and the ghost of pipe tobacco from an uncle three decades gone, that Maren stood in the doorway for a full minute before she could make herself step over the threshold.

Nothing had been touched. The lawyer had said as much, but she hadn't believed it until she saw it: the reading glasses folded on the arm of the good chair, the crossword half-finished on the side table, a coffee mug in the sink with a dried brown ring at the bottom. Odetta had left for the porch that Tuesday morning expecting to come back in.

Maren went room to room the way you'd check a wound, testing how much it would hurt to look. The guest room where she'd slept every summer of her childhood, twin bed still made up with the quilt made of her grandmother's old dresses. The kitchen with its yellow curtains, sun-bleached at the edges to the color of weak tea. The back porch, where a rusted watering can still sat on the railing, tipped slightly, as if Odetta had just set it down.

She didn't cry until she found the recipe box.

It was tucked in the pantry, tin and dented, and inside were index cards in three different handwritings—her aunt's looping cursive, her grandmother's cramped print, and, near the back, a few cards in Maren's own careful ten-year-old hand, copied out one long-ago summer when Odetta had decided she ought to learn to make her mother's biscuits properly. *Maren's first try, needs more butter,* someone had written in the margin of one, and Maren realized with a lurch that it was her own note, made at eleven, criticizing her own cooking, kept for twenty years in a box in a pantry by a woman who apparently threw nothing away that mattered.

She sat down on the pantry floor and let it happen—the loud, ugly, hiccuping kind of crying she hadn't done since she was a child, surrounded by cans of tomatoes and her aunt's handwriting and the specific quality of afternoon light that only existed in this kitchen, in this town, at this hour.

When it passed, she made tea, because Odetta would have made tea. She sat at the kitchen table with the recipe box open in front of her and began, slowly, to read.

Outside, the gravel driveway sat empty and correct in the last of the sun, waiting, the way houses do, to see who would come next.
