The gravel turned to washboard about a mile before the turnoff, and Mara felt every ridge of it in her teeth. She hadn't been out this road since March, since the funeral, since her mother's voice had gone quiet in a hospital room that smelled like lemon disinfectant and something underneath it that no amount of lemon could fix.

The house looked smaller than she remembered. It always did, coming back. Two stories of gray clapboard leaning very slightly west, like it had spent forty years bracing against a wind that came from that direction and only that direction. The porch light was on, which startled her until she remembered she'd left it on herself, the last time, because her mother had always said a dark house looks like it's given up.

She sat in the car for longer than she meant to.

The key still hung on the nail inside the mudroom door, which meant she had to use the spare hidden under the cracked flowerpot, the one shaped like a frog that her father had bought at a yard sale in some other decade. The lock stuck the way it always stuck. She had to lift the door by the handle and shoulder it at the same time, a motion she found her body remembered before her mind caught up.

Inside, the air was thick and still, holding the smell of woodsmoke and her mother's hand lotion, lavender and something medicinal. Dust had begun to settle on the surfaces in a way that felt less like neglect than like the house exhaling slowly after being held too long.

She walked through it room by room, not because she needed to see everything again but because her legs seemed to want the confirmation. The kitchen with its yellow curtains, sun-faded on one side. The clock on the stove blinking 12:00 over and over, patient and wrong. The living room where the afghan her mother had knitted still lay folded over the arm of the recliner, as if she'd just stepped out to answer the phone.

In the hallway, photographs marched up the stairwell in their mismatched frames — Mara at six missing her front teeth, her parents on a dock somewhere in Michigan, a black and white one of a grandmother she'd never met. She touched none of them. She had learned, in the months since, that touching things sometimes made them more gone rather than less.

Her mother's bedroom she saved for last, and even then she only opened the door partway before deciding that today was not the day. Tomorrow, maybe. Or the day after. The lawyer said there was no rush, that grief had its own math and the house would keep.

She ended up back in the kitchen as the light went orange and then gray outside the yellow curtains. She found the same chipped mug her mother had always used, the one with a chicken on it that had lost most of its glaze, and made tea she didn't particularly want just to have something warm to hold.

Sitting at the table, she noticed for the first time a small stack of mail her mother had left there, undisturbed since whenever the illness had made stairs and small errands too much to manage. A seed catalog. A bill. A birthday card, unopened, addressed to Mara in her mother's looping hand, postmarked three days before she died.

Mara did not open it right away. She sat with it a while, turning it over, feeling its ordinary weight, while the refrigerator hummed and the stove clock kept blinking its patient lie that no time had passed at all.

Outside, the first cicadas of the evening started up, and somewhere down the road a dog barked twice and quit. The house settled around her the way old houses do, its joints ticking as they cooled, and for a moment it did not feel like a house she had inherited so much as one that was simply, finally, letting her back in.
