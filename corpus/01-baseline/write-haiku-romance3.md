The first time Maya saw him, he was sketching in a notebook at Harbor Point Café, a cup of coffee gone cold beside his hand. She only noticed because the light caught his concentration—the way his pencil moved in quick, assured strokes across the page—and when he glanced up, their eyes met for exactly one second before she looked away.

The second time was at Brennan's Books three days later. She was shelving returns when he approached the counter with a biography of Anselm Adams. The clerk rang it up. He paid. He left. She didn't even turn around.

By the third encounter—at the pier, both of them watching the sunset from different sections of railing—Maya had started to notice. The odds seemed impossible in a town of five thousand. She studied his profile: angular jaw, dark hair that needed cutting, the same worn canvas jacket he'd worn at the café.

"Beautiful evening," she said quietly.

He turned, surprised. "It is. I've been here six weeks and I keep forgetting to come out here at this hour."

"Where are you from?"

"Portland. You?"

"Here, actually. I've never left." She wasn't sure why she volunteered that last part.

His name was David. He'd come to Whitmore Bay to recover from a life he'd disassembled carefully, piece by piece. Photography was supposed to help. The town was supposed to be quiet. He was supposed to figure things out.

They didn't exchange numbers.

But then he was at the fish market where she bought coffee. At the library where she worked part-time, checking out books on local history. On the beach at Crescent Cove, where she ran most mornings and where he apparently had started running too.

"You're following me," she said one morning in June, jogging alongside him as the sun climbed.

"I was thinking the same thing about you," he replied, breathless.

They stopped running and walked instead, letting the ocean wind shake the salt spray onto their clothes. He told her about a marriage that had calcified into silence, about photography that had once felt alive and now felt hollow. She told him about a career in accounting that paid bills while teaching her nothing about herself, about a hometown she'd never quite left even though she wanted to.

"Why are we keeping crossing paths?" Maya asked.

"Maybe we're not supposed to keep walking past each other," David said.

They got coffee—intentionally this time, sitting at the small table where she'd first seen him at Harbor Point Café. She looked at his sketches: the lighthouse, her storefront pharmacy, the bent figure of an old man mending nets. They were good. More than good.

"You should take photographs," he said, pulling out his camera. "I've seen you noticing things. The light on the water, people's faces. You see like an artist."

She'd never considered it. She'd never considered that noticing things could be a skill worth developing, a language worth speaking.

By July, they'd moved in sync. Morning runs at Crescent Cove. Afternoons at the café or the beach. Evenings watching the sky change from blue to purple to black. He'd started selling prints at the gallery on Main Street. She'd borrowed his extra camera.

One evening, walking back from the pier, David took her hand.

"This town was supposed to be temporary," he said.

"Mine was supposed to be permanent," Maya replied.

"I think I'm staying. Are you?"

She thought about Whitmore Bay, about the way the light bent here, about the impossible mathematics of collision and connection. About how sometimes a small town is exactly large enough for two people to become strangers, and small enough that they couldn't help but become something more.

"Yeah," she said. "I think I am too."
