The gravel crunched differently than she remembered, softer, sunken in places where roots had pushed up beneath it over the years. Marianne parked the rental at the top of the drive and sat there for a moment with the engine ticking as it cooled, looking at the house through the windshield like it might change its mind about existing if she waited long enough.

It didn't. It just stood there, white paint gone the color of old teeth, waiting for her the way it had waited for her mother, and before that, for her grandmother.

The key was where the lawyer said it would be, under the ceramic frog by the porch steps, the one with the chipped eye that her mother always meant to replace and never did. Marianne turned it in the lock and the door swung open on a smell that hit her like a hand to the chest — lemon oil and old paper and the particular mustiness of rooms that had been closed up through a whole summer.

She stood in the front hall for a long time without moving.

The house hadn't been touched since the funeral. Her mother's cardigan was still slung over the back of the kitchen chair, as if she'd just stepped out to the garden and would be back any minute to finish her tea, which sat on the table gone cloudy and cold, a skin formed on its surface like a held breath. Marianne picked up the cup, then didn't know what to do with it, so she set it back down exactly where it had been, like a woman defusing something.

She'd expected to cry. She'd budgeted for it, actually — three days off work, a box of tissues in the car, a plan to let herself fall apart in the privacy of a house no one else would see. But what she felt instead, moving from room to room, was a kind of stunned recognition, the way you feel meeting someone you knew as a child grown suddenly old. The wallpaper in the hallway, roses gone the color of weak tea. The stairs that still complained on the fourth step. Her own height marked in pencil on the pantry doorframe, dated in her mother's cramped hand, stopping abruptly at fourteen, the year she'd decided she was too old for that particular ritual and had felt, at the time, very grown up about refusing it.

Upstairs, her mother's bedroom was the hardest. The bed was made. Of course it was made — her mother made her bed every morning of her life like it was a moral position. Marianne sat on the edge of it and put her hand flat on the quilt, and that was when it came, finally, not as a storm but as something quieter and more total, a kind of dissolving.

She stayed like that until the light through the window went orange and then grey.

Later, she went down to the kitchen and found, in the drawer where the good tablecloths lived, a shoebox of photographs she'd never seen: her mother young, laughing, on a beach somewhere, with a man who wasn't Marianne's father. Her mother at twenty, holding a baby who could only have been Marianne, looking down at her with an expression so unguarded it felt like trespassing to see it.

She made tea in her mother's kettle, in her mother's kitchen, and sat at the table across from the cold cup that was still there, and she let herself, for the first time in three weeks, simply be in the house rather than dreading it.

It would take months to sort through everything — the furniture, the letters, the decision of whether to sell or to stay. But that first night, she didn't think about any of that. She climbed the stairs to her childhood room, made up the narrow bed with sheets that smelled of cedar and dust, and slept there like something coming home, in a house that had been waiting, patiently, to become hers.
