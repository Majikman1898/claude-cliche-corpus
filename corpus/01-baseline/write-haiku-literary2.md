The key still turned the same way. Grace half-expected it not to, as though the house itself might have changed enough to reject her. But the lock gave its familiar click, and she stepped inside to the smell of dust and something floral—the lavender sachets her mother had tucked into every drawer.

Three months had passed since the funeral. Three months of the lawyer's letters, the executor's forms, the well-meaning calls from people who'd known her mother longer than they'd known Grace. She'd been working in Portland, and it had been easy to stay there, to let the silence of the inheritance sit in a folder on her desk.

But the house was selling. That was the plan. The realtor would come Monday, and before then, someone had to walk these rooms and decide what mattered.

She moved through the downstairs slowly, running her fingers along the piano they'd never learned to play, across the back of the wingchair where her mother read every Sunday morning. The kitchen was cleaner than she'd expected—someone had been in to take care of things. The refrigerator held only a glass pitcher and a bottle of mineral water.

The stairs creaked on the third and seventh steps. She remembered jumping over the third step as a child, certain it was booby-trapped. Her mother would watch from the bottom, amused, never saying it was perfectly safe.

Upstairs, the master bedroom held the bed they'd all avoided thinking about. Grace closed that door.

Her own room remained exactly as she'd left it nine years ago—twin bed with the white iron frame, posters of bands she'd forgotten about, a shelf of books with their spines cracked from actual reading. On the dresser, a framed photo from her college graduation: Grace in her gown, her mother beaming, her father already two years gone.

She sat on the edge of the bed and noticed, for the first time, that the light coming through the window was the same gold-honey color she remembered from childhood afternoons. Nothing had changed but everything had.

In the hallway closet, she found the boxes. Labeled in her mother's careful handwriting: "Grace—when you're ready." There were three of them. Grace carried them to her room and opened the first.

Her childhood drawings. Report cards. A envelope of loose photographs. A letter addressed to "Grace, my love," in her mother's hand, dated two months before she died.

Grace didn't open it yet. Instead, she looked at the photographs—pictures of herself at every age, some she'd never seen. Her mother at thirty, holding baby Grace in front of this very house. The two of them at the beach, squinting into the sun. Her parents' wedding photo, both of them impossibly young and impossibly happy.

She moved to the window and looked out at the yard where they'd planted tomatoes one summer, where they'd sat reading on lawn chairs, where so many small, perfect moments had accumulated like light.

The realtor would come Monday. The house would sell. Someone else would live here, make different memories between these walls.

But tonight, Grace lay on her childhood bed in the house her mother had loved, and she read the letter. It was short, and in it, her mother told her that houses were just walls, that what mattered was carried inside, that she was proud, that she was loved.

Grace folded the letter carefully and held it to her chest. The gold light slipped away as the sun set, and she didn't turn on the lamp. She just sat there, letting the darkness come slowly, letting the past and present exist together in the small room under the eaves.
