The station-keeping thrusters had been ticking for six hours when Renna finally admitted the numbers were wrong.

She'd been watching them since her shift began aboard the *Little Osprey*, a survey hopper barely larger than the cargo tug it had been built from. The ship was fourteen light-years past the last charted beacon, drifting through a stretch of nothing the atlases labeled simply as the Vaugh Deep. Nothing was supposed to be out here. That was the point of coming.

"Toma," she said into the intercom. "Come look at this."

Toma arrived chewing the end of a protein stick, hair still creased from the sleep webbing. He was the *Osprey's* other half — pilot, mechanic, and the only other living thing aboard besides the algae tanks. He leaned over her shoulder and squinted at the readout.

"Thrusters are correcting," he said. "So?"

"They're correcting *toward* something. We're being pulled. Slow, but steady. Bearing's fixed."

"A rock?"

"No mass. That's the thing. The gravitometer reads flat. Whatever's tugging us doesn't weigh anything."

Toma stopped chewing. He tapped through the sensor stack himself, the way he always did, as if the instruments might tell a different man a different truth. They didn't. Something ahead of them, roughly nine thousand kilometers out, was drawing the ship gently inward, and the object registered on nothing — not mass, not heat, not light, not radio. It existed only as an absence the ship kept falling toward.

They killed the thrusters and let the *Osprey* drift, to see.

For two hours the anomaly resolved on the forward optics as a smudge, then a disc, then a perfect circle of pure black — not the black of empty space, which is faintly grained with distant stars, but a black that swallowed the starfield behind it and gave nothing back. It was the size of a small moon by the time they were close enough to feel afraid.

"Stars," Toma murmured. He wasn't cursing. He was pointing. Around the edge of the circle, the stars behind it weren't hidden. They were *bent*, smeared into thin bright arcs that curved along the rim like water going down a drain. "It's lensing. But there's no mass to lens with."

Renna had stopped trying to explain it. She was recording everything — every band, every instrument, dumping it all to the armored log-core that would survive even if they didn't. That was the job. You went to the empty places and you brought back what you found, and if you didn't come back, the log did.

"We should burn out," Toma said quietly. "Now, while we still have the fuel to fight it."

"I know."

Neither of them moved for a moment. The circle filled the whole forward window now, and Renna had the sudden, childish certainty that it was looking back — that the absence had a texture of attention to it, patient and enormous and old.

Then, at the very center of the black, a single point of light appeared. It was warm, amber, steady. It was, unmistakably, the color and flicker of the porch lamp outside the house where Renna had grown up on Deshan, a lamp that had been cold and dead for thirty years.

She heard Toma's breath catch and knew, without asking, that he was seeing something else. Something of his own.

"Burn," she whispered. "Burn now."

Toma's hands were already moving. The engines lit, and the *Osprey* groaned as it clawed away from the thing that weighed nothing, the little amber light shrinking behind them until the starfield closed back over it like a healed wound.

They flew for a long time in silence. Later, checking the log, Renna found the anomaly had recorded on every instrument perfectly — a clean, complete dataset of an object that could not exist.

Except the visual band. Every frame of it came back black. Whatever each of them had seen at the center, the cameras had seen nothing at all.

She filed the report anyway. She just left that part out.
