The frost had come three days early that year, and Mira Voss noticed it the way she noticed most things nobody else did — quietly, and alone.

She was seventeen, the only mage Kelsbrook had produced in two generations, which meant she was also the only person who could have felt the wrongness pressing up through the floorboards of her grandmother's cellar. The village elders called her gift small. A candle-lighter's talent, they said, good for drying laundry and warming kettles. Mira had never argued, mostly because she had never found anything to argue with — until the night the cellar floor cracked open like an eggshell and breathed cold air up into her face.

She went down with a lamp she didn't need, because the crack itself was glowing, a thin seam of blue-white light threading through old stone that predated the cellar, predated the house, predated Kelsbrook itself. When she knelt and put her palm against it, the light didn't recoil from her fingers. It reached back.

What poured into her wasn't warmth. It was memory that didn't belong to her — a sense of vast cold rooms, of something old counting itself asleep, of wards laid down by hands more careful than kind. And under all of it, a name rose up unbidden in her mind: Aurent's Seal. She had heard the word once, in a nursery rhyme her grandmother sang to frighten children away from wells. *Don't wake what Aurent bound, don't call the deep by name.*

She should have sealed the crack back up. She told herself this later, many times. Instead she spent that night, and the four after it, sneaking down with her lamp, letting the seam of light unspool its power into her like thread onto a spindle. It made her stronger in ways she could measure — she could light a hearth from across a room now, could feel the shape of water moving under the earth, could, if she concentrated, hear the heartbeat of the old stone itself. It felt like waking up after a lifetime of sleepwalking.

It was her grandmother, Ilse, who caught her on the fifth night, standing at the cellar stairs with a face gone the color of ash.

"You've been drinking from the Hollow," Ilse said, not a question.

"It isn't hurting anyone," Mira said. "It's just power, sitting there unused—"

"It is not unused. It is *held down.*" Ilse gripped the stair rail so hard her knuckles whitened. "Your great-great-grandmother sealed that place because the thing beneath doesn't give power, girl. It lends it. And it always, always collects."

Mira wanted to laugh it off, the way she'd laughed off every warning about her strangeness her whole life. But she thought of the counting she'd felt beneath the cold — not sleep, she realized now, but patience. Something keeping time.

"How do we close it?"

Ilse's answer was not comforting: nobody living remembered how. The seal had been forged before written record, in a language that had died with the woman who cast it. All they had was the rhyme, and rhymes were not spells.

So Mira did the only thing she could think to do. She went back down one final time, not to draw power out, but to press her own back in — every ember of borrowed strength she'd taken, offered back like a debt repaid before it came due. It cost her. When she climbed the cellar stairs at dawn, her hands shook and her breath came thin, and something in her chest that had felt vast for five bright nights had gone ordinary again.

The crack sealed, slow as a wound closing, the blue light dimming to nothing.

Mira never told the village elders what she'd found beneath Kelsbrook. She let them go on thinking her gift was small, good for kettles and laundry lines. But some nights, lying awake, she still felt it — that patient counting, waiting under the stone, and she understood that she had not defeated anything. She had only bought the village more time, and hoped that whoever inherited the debt after her would be wiser than she had been.
