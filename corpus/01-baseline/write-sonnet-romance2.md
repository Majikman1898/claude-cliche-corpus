The lighthouse hadn't worked in eleven years, but Marisol Vance still called it that when she gave directions, because everyone in Poldwick did. Turn left at the lighthouse, past the bakery, mind the gulls. She'd been saying it to herself all morning, rehearsing the town for a stranger who hadn't asked.

She saw him first outside Ferris's, the little grocer with the crooked awning, squinting at a hand-drawn map that was clearly wrong about something, because the man kept turning it upside down like that might fix the coastline. He had a canvas bag over one shoulder and the sunburned nose of someone who'd been walking the cliffs since dawn without sunscreen.

"You're looking for the harbor," she said, not really a question.

He looked up. "Is it that obvious?"

"You're holding the map for Bellhaven. Wrong town. We get their tourists sometimes." She pointed south. "Harbor's that way, but there isn't much to see. Just boats."

"I like boats," he said, and something about the plainness of it made her laugh before she caught herself.

His name was Theo Ambrose, he told her, here for two weeks to paint, though he didn't say what kind of painter, and she didn't ask. She told him her name and where the good coffee was, which was not Ferris's, whatever Ferris's sign claimed, and then she went on to the pharmacy and forgot about him, mostly.

She saw him again that evening, sitting on the seawall with a sketchbook, and this time she didn't stop, just lifted a hand as she passed with her groceries, and he lifted his sketchbook back like it was a wave in kind.

The third time was three days later, in the rain, both of them ducking under the awning of the shuttered ice cream stand at the same moment from opposite directions, so that they nearly collided and then stood there dripping and laughing at the absurdity of it, this town so small it kept folding them into the same six feet of dry pavement.

"Are you following me," he said, "or am I following you?"

"Neither. Poldwick is following both of us. It only has four dry places and you keep finding them by accident."

"What's the fourth?"

"The library. Wednesdays it's closed."

He laughed at that, too easily, like he'd decided already that she was someone worth laughing with, and she felt the small alarm that came with liking a stranger too quickly, the one she usually silenced by leaving first. She didn't leave first this time.

They talked until the rain thinned to a mist, about nothing that mattered — the ferry schedule, a dog that always sat outside the post office as if waiting for a letter that never came, the particular gray of the water in October, which he said he'd come all this way to paint and hadn't managed to get right yet. She told him it wasn't a color, it was a mood, and you couldn't mix moods from a tube, and he wrote that down in the corner of his sketchbook like it was something worth keeping.

By the second week she'd stopped being surprised to see him. He appeared at the bakery counter, at the far end of the pier at dusk, once absurdly at the top of the church tower where she went to be alone and found him already there, not alone, being exactly the kind of company she hadn't known she wanted.

"This town isn't big enough for both of us," she told him, the day before he was meant to leave, only half joking.

"That's the trouble," he said. "I keep thinking I'll run out of it before I run out of reasons to stay."

She didn't answer that directly. But when the ferry left two mornings later than planned, and then two mornings after that, nobody in Poldwick was terribly surprised, least of all the dog outside the post office, who had, by then, gotten used to seeing him too.
