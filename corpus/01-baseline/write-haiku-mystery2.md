Detective Sarah Venn had been in Greyhollow for three days, and the fog hadn't lifted once. It pressed against the stone cottages like something alive, muffling sound, erasing landmarks. She stood in the train station—a converted barn, really—reading Thomas Ashford's missing person file for the dozenth time.

The man had vanished from his home on Moorside Lane. Forty-two years old. Retired schoolteacher. Nothing remarkable except that his daughter had called the police after he missed their weekly telephone call. His car remained parked outside. His wallet sat on the kitchen table. It was the kind of disappearance that felt impossible in the modern world.

"You'll want to talk to Margaret Thorne again," said Constable Weir, a young man with worried eyes. "She's the closest neighbor. She might remember something."

They walked through the village in near-total blindness. Sarah counted her steps—forty-seven to the turning, another sixty to Moorside Lane. The fog swallowed their torch beams. Somewhere nearby, a church bell tolled.

Margaret Thorne was waiting with tea. She was perhaps seventy, with sharp features and sharper eyes that didn't miss much.

"He was being followed," Margaret said without preamble.

Sarah set down her cup. "By whom?"

"Don't know. But I'd see him, late evening, looking over his shoulder at the mist. First time I really noticed was about two weeks before. He was standing in his garden in the rain, watching the treeline like something might come out of it."

"Did you ask him about it?"

"I tried. He said I was imagining things." Margaret's hands twisted in her lap. "But I wasn't. Something changed him."

Back at the station, Sarah pulled Ashford's bank records. Small withdrawals. Five hundred pounds in cash, taken out three days before his disappearance. She called his daughter in London.

"Did your father mention anything unusual? Financial troubles? Threats?"

"No. But there was... he started asking me questions about our family. About grandfather. I assumed he was just getting sentimental in his old age."

"What kind of questions?"

"About what happened to him. During the war. I told Dad I didn't know the details—Mother never liked to discuss it."

Sarah hung up and drove to the local archive, a converted library in the village center. It took an hour of searching, but she found it: a newspaper article from 1945. Greyhollow Constabulary had discovered wartime documents hidden in the rectory. The vicar at the time had been working with a smuggling ring, moving German operatives across the channel. Among them had been a man named Ernst Müller.

In the photograph, Müller looked unremarkable. Ordinary. Forgettable.

Except Sarah's breath caught. She'd seen that face before.

She pulled Ashford's birth certificate. His mother's maiden name was Müller.

At dawn, the fog finally broke. Sarah walked through Greyhollow as the mist retreated, revealing a village suspended in time—the same cottages, the same church, the same people going through their routines. But now she understood what Margaret had seen in Ashford's face. Recognition. The burden of inheritance.

She found him at the cliffs beyond the village, sitting on a bench overlooking water that was still swallowed by fog below.

"Your grandfather contacted you," she said quietly.

Thomas nodded. "He had a granddaughter. My half-sister. I have a family I never knew. But the people who had him—who helped him hide, all these years—they wanted it kept secret. They gave me a choice."

"And you chose to disappear."

"I chose to stop being a part of a lie," he said. "Margaret saw me making peace with it. That's all."

Sarah sat beside him as the sun burned higher, gradually reclaiming the world.
