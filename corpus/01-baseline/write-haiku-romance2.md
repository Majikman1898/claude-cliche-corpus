The first time Maya saw him, he was sitting on the weathered bench outside Pelican's Rest coffee shop in Meridian Bay, sketch pad balanced on his knee. She nearly collided with him rounding the corner, apologizing twice as if the near-miss were somehow her fault.

Three days later, they were both reaching for the last copy of a used paperback at Tidewater Books. His fingers touched the spine a millisecond before hers.

"Please," he said, stepping back. "I've already read it twice."

She bought it anyway, wondering if he was the type who lied politely or if he'd simply been testing her.

The third encounter happened at the pier at sunset. Maya came here most evenings to watch the light change over the water, a habit born from her recent divorce and the desperate need for quiet. She found him leaning against the railing, sketching again—this time the sky itself, oranges bleeding into purples.

"You're good," she said, before she could stop herself.

He turned, surprised to see her, and she recognized something in his expression that she felt in her own chest every morning. Loss, maybe. Or just the weight of being solitary in a place designed for lovers.

"Architecture, mostly," he said, showing her his pad. The sketches were technical, precise—buildings that didn't exist yet. "But I'm trying to remember how to draw what I feel instead of what I measure."

They talked until the sky went dark, about nothing and everything. His name was James. He'd left his job in Boston and rented a cottage behind the hardware store, hoping to figure out who he was when he wasn't performing for anyone.

She understood that language perfectly.

After that, they began coinciding. Not by plan—they both insisted it was chance—but at the bookstore café where she worked three afternoons a week, at the farmer's market, at the library where she'd started taking out books on divorce and self-discovery, books she was reading more for company than clarity. Once, they were the only two people at an outdoor screening of an old film at the town pavilion, sitting three rows apart, pretending not to notice each other.

Six weeks into their accidental friendship, Maya found him sitting on the bench outside Pelican's Rest again. Spring had turned to early summer. The tourists were beginning to arrive.

"I'm leaving next week," he said without preamble. "Got a job back in New York. Contract work on a restoration project."

She'd known this was coming. People didn't stay in Meridian Bay. It was a waypoint, a place to recover between chapters. But she'd stopped being sure about which chapter she was in.

"That's good," she said. "That sounds like you."

He closed his sketch pad—the one he'd been filling with her profile over the past few weeks, though she didn't know it yet. "Do you want to know something ridiculous?"

"Probably yes."

"I came here because I didn't believe in coincidence. Thought I needed to find somewhere quiet to convince myself life was just statistics and probability. But then I kept running into you, and I couldn't explain it away." He smiled sadly. "So I started drawing instead of calculating."

Maya sat down beside him on the bench. The afternoon was warm. Somewhere, a sailboat was leaving harbor.

"I'm staying," she said. "I think I finally know that now. I'm staying."

He didn't ask her to leave with him. They both understood that some people cross your path to remind you who you are, then move on. It wasn't romantic, the way stories said it should be. It was something quieter—a benediction, a nod from the universe that you were exactly where you needed to be.

He left on a Tuesday. She didn't go to the bus station. Instead, she sat on their bench and drew for the first time in years, trying to capture how it felt to be found by a stranger, then found by yourself.
