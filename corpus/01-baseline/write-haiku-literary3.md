Marcus pulled into the gravel drive of Rosewood as the afternoon light slanted gold through the oak trees. Dust from the road still hung in the air, and he sat in the car for several minutes, hands on the steering wheel, not quite ready to leave the engine running—that small sign of forward momentum.

The house hadn't changed. The white colonial still had its black shutters, the front porch still sagged slightly where his grandfather had never quite gotten around to fixing it. But the flower beds were overgrown now, tangled with weeds his mother used to keep meticulously pruned.

He'd scattered her ashes here three weeks ago. That had been his sister's idea—their mother had always loved this place, had spent summers here as a child, had dragged the three of them back every Easter and Thanksgiving for as long as he could remember. But Marcus had never felt the pull of Rosewood the way she had. To him, it was always his mother's house, his grandmother's house. Not his.

The realtor's letter had been formal and final: You are the sole inheritor of the Rosewood estate, valued at approximately—he'd stopped reading after the numbers. He'd called his sister instead. "I can't sell it yet," she'd said quietly. "It's too soon."

He understood. Selling it meant she was really gone.

Inside, everything smelled the same—old wood and lavender sachets and something else, something like absence. His footsteps echoed across the hardwood floors. The living room held the furniture from his childhood: the cream sofa where his mother had taught him to play cards, the bookshelf she'd refilled every trip, the painting of the garden that had hung above the fireplace for forty years.

In the kitchen, her last grocery list sat on the counter, written in her precise handwriting. Milk. Honey. The kind of ginger tea she'd started drinking more of in the last year, as if small comforts could hold back what was coming.

Marcus opened the refrigerator. Everything in it was ancient—mustard from last summer, a jar of something unidentifiable. He threw it away, ran the disposal. Made himself tea. Sat at the kitchen table watching the backyard through the window, where the lawn had started to go wild.

His phone buzzed. A text from his sister: How is it?

He didn't know how to answer. It was exactly as his mother had left it—which meant it was a museum of her. Everywhere he looked, he saw her last choices, her last purchases, the small decisions she'd made in the ordinary moments before the diagnosis had suddenly accelerated everything.

He spent the night in the guest room, not quite able to sleep in the master bedroom. At three in the morning, he got up and went to the study. His mother had kept journals—she'd written in them for decades. He found them arranged by year on the top shelf, leather-bound and dust-free. He pulled one from five years ago at random, opened it.

*Took Marcus and his family to Rosewood for the holidays. He seems so busy now, so far away, but at dinner he laughed at something James said just the way he did when he was small. I watched him looking at the house, and I wondered if he knows that one day, it might be his to love the way I love it. I think places absorb us if we let them. They hold us.*

Marcus closed the journal carefully. Outside, the night was perfectly quiet. He could hear the old house settling around him, speaking in its familiar creaks and sighs. He sat very still and listened, as if it might tell him who he was supposed to be in this place, in this inheritance of both property and memory.

Slowly, he began to understand that his mother had left him more than a house. She'd left him a choice.
