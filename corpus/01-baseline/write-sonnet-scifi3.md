The distress signal reached the *Marlow Wren* eleven minutes before anyone believed it was real.

Ensign Okonkwo-Reyes heard it first, a thin repeating pulse threading through the comm static like a held breath. She almost logged it as instrument drift. Then Captain Adaeze Solano leaned over her shoulder, and the pulse resolved into something almost, but not quite, like a voice.

"That's not a language," said Teo Marsh, the ship's linguist-slash-navigator, hunched at his console with his coffee going cold. "That's not even sound, structurally. It's math pretending to be sound."

They were four months out from the Kestrel Reach relay, hauling ice-core samples back to the Coriolis Station labs, when the anomaly bloomed across their forward sensors — not an object, not quite a field, but a wrongness in the dataset. Space where the *Marlow Wren*'s instruments insisted there was space, and also, according to the same instruments, wasn't.

"Diameter's holding at four kilometers," said Priya Anand, running gravimetrics from the aft station. "But the mass reading keeps flipping between zero and something that would tear us apart if we got within a thousand klicks of it."

Solano ordered a full stop. The ship's old bones groaned as thrusters bled off their momentum, and the anomaly hung there against the black like a smear on a window nobody could quite clean.

"Options," she said.

"We log it, we route around it, we go home," Marsh said immediately. "That's not a decision, that's the only sane sentence in the room."

"The pulse is intensifying," Okonkwo-Reyes said. "Whatever's making that sound — it's noticed us noticing it."

Solano felt the old itch, the one that had gotten her this command in the first place and nearly cost her the last one. Curiosity dressed up as duty. "Priya. Can we get a probe close enough to sample without risking the ship?"

"Define risk."

"Point taken. Do it anyway."

The probe took nine minutes to reach launch range and forty seconds to die. Not explode — die, the telemetry simply thinning out mid-transmission like a sentence trailing off, the numbers going soft and strange before they stopped meaning anything at all. The last frame of visual data showed no debris. No flash. Just the probe's outline unstitching at the edges, the way a word looks wrong after you've stared at it too long.

Nobody spoke for a moment.

"Bring us about," Solano said quietly. "Best speed away from it, Priya, no arguments."

"None coming from me," Priya said, and her hands were already moving.

But as the *Marlow Wren* began its slow, groaning turn, the pulse changed. It sharpened, focused, and for one heartbeat every screen on the bridge — nav, life support, the mess hall's ancient entertainment console two decks down, all of it — flickered the same three-second burst of visual noise. When it cleared, Okonkwo-Reyes was staring at her console like it had bitten her.

"Captain. It sent something back. Not a signal. A — I don't know what to call it. A shape. It's sitting in our storage buffer like it's always been there."

"Don't open it," Marsh said.

"I'm not stupid, Teo." She looked up at Solano, and there was something behind her eyes that hadn't been there an hour ago — not fear exactly, but its more patient cousin. "It's not asking permission. I think it already looked."

The anomaly was shrinking behind them now, folding into itself with the eerie unhurriedness of something that had never been in danger of anything. Solano watched it go on the aft display, and did not feel, as she'd expected to, relief.

"Get us to the relay," she said. "And somebody figure out what that shape is before we get there. Quietly. I don't want a panic on my ship over something we don't understand yet."

"And if we never understand it?" Priya asked.

Solano didn't answer right away. Behind them, the smear on the black had already closed like a wound with no scar, as if the universe itself had simply decided to forget the *Marlow Wren* had ever been there at all.
