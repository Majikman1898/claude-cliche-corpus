The gulls arrived at the Anchor Café before the tourists did, and so did Maren.

She had the same table every morning — second from the window, where the salt-fogged glass gave the harbor a look of being underwater — and the same order, a black coffee she never finished before it went cold, because she liked the smell more than the drink. It was a Tuesday in June, the kind of gray that Port Ilsa specialized in, when the man in the wet raincoat sat down at the table beside hers without asking, because every other table was taken by a bus tour from Portland.

"Sorry," he said, not sounding it. "They cleared out the room across from mine. Some kind of confab about lighthouse preservation."

"There's a confab about everything here in June," Maren said, not looking up from her book.

He ordered eggs. He left before she did. She didn't think of him again until Thursday, when she found him crouched in the aisle of the hardware store, muttering at a wall of drawer pulls like they'd personally wronged him.

"You're the confab man," she said.

"Elias," he said, standing too fast and knocking a box of hinges to the floor. "I'm renovating the old Pruitt house. Or trying to. It's renovating me, mostly."

"That's the yellow one on Widow's Walk Road?"

"You know it?"

"Everyone knows it. It's been empty since I was a kid. My mother used to say it was haunted by a sea captain's disappointment."

"That tracks," Elias said. "The disappointment's structural. The floors slope four inches toward the ocean."

She laughed before she meant to, and it surprised her enough that she left without buying the twine she'd come for.

The third time was not an accident, though neither of them would admit that until much later. She was walking the tide line before dawn, the way she did most mornings when sleep wouldn't hold her, collecting the green glass the town was known for, and there he was, sitting on the seawall with a thermos and a spiral notebook, looking like a man doing penance.

"Do you live on this beach," she said.

"I couldn't sleep. There's a sound the house makes at night. Like it's deciding something." He held up the notebook. "I've started keeping a list of the noises so I don't lie there convincing myself they're footsteps."

"Read me one."

"Three fourteen a.m., something like a held breath, then a sigh. I'm calling that one the Captain."

"My mother would like you."

"Is she around?"

"She died four years ago. But she'd have opinions about a stranger who buys her old ghost story a thermos."

He didn't apologize, which she appreciated. He just nodded, like grief was a fact of tide and weather, something a person from a place like this would understand without being told twice. They sat while the sky went from slate to pale rose, not talking much, passing the coffee back and forth, and when a heron picked its way along the shallows he pointed it out without looking away, as if he already knew she'd want to see it too.

"I have to go," she said eventually. "I open the bookshop at eight."

"The one on Harbor Street? With the cat in the window?"

"You've seen it."

"I've been meaning to come in. I need something to read at three fourteen in the morning that isn't a list of noises."

"Come by," she said, and was mildly astonished to find she meant it as more than a courtesy.

"I will," he said, and for once, standing to go, he didn't knock anything over.

She watched him climb the path toward Widow's Walk Road, a dark shape against the brightening gray, and thought that some towns were small enough that you kept meeting the same people until you ran out of reasons to call it coincidence.
