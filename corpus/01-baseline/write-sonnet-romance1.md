The gulls had already claimed the pier by the time Marisol Vance arrived that morning, and so had the man with the broken umbrella.

He was crouched near the bait shop, trying to coax the thing shut against a wind that clearly had other plans, and losing. Marisol watched for a moment longer than was polite, then kept walking toward the ferry office, where she'd left a manuscript she wasn't ready to sell and a landlord who was.

Cormorant Bay was small enough that you could walk its entire waterfront in twenty minutes, which meant that by early afternoon she saw him again, this time sitting on the seawall outside the Anchor & Thread, eating fried clams out of a paper boat with the grim focus of a man finishing a task rather than enjoying one.

"You're the umbrella," she said, because it was true and because the wine from lunch had made her bold.

He looked up. Somewhere in his thirties, sunburned in the specific way of someone unused to being outdoors, a paperback splayed face-down beside him like a shot bird. "I'm Theo," he said. "The umbrella didn't survive."

"Understandable. It never had a chance." She nodded at the paperback. "Bad book?"

"Good book. Bad reader. I keep looking up." He said it like a confession, and something in his face suggested he meant more than the clams and the view.

She didn't sit. She had the manuscript to think about, and thinking about it in the presence of strangers who noticed things felt unwise. She said something about the tide schedule, he said something back that was funnier than it needed to be, and then she left, telling herself it didn't matter either way.

But Cormorant Bay kept doing what small towns do, which is nothing at all except arrange people's paths without asking permission. She found him three days later crouched over the same broken umbrella, now abandoned in a trash bin outside the hardware store, buying a replacement he was testing, with visible skepticism, in the parking lot. She found him again at the Thursday market, arguing gently with a woman selling raspberries about whether raspberries could, in principle, be too sweet. She found him at low tide, alone on the flats with his shoes in his hand, looking at something in the mud that turned out to be nothing, just a shape the water had left behind.

"Are you following me?" she finally asked, the fourth time, or maybe the fifth, when she came around the lighthouse path and found him already there, watching the horizon like it owed him money.

"I was going to ask you the same thing." He didn't turn around right away. When he did, his sunburn had faded into something closer to a normal color, like the town had started fitting him better. "I'm here for six more days. I keep thinking I should stop noticing where you are, and then I don't stop."

"That's a strange thing to say to someone you've spoken to twice."

"Four times. This is four." He said it like a fact he'd been keeping in a drawer.

The wind came up off the water the way it always did around that hour, salt-heavy, indifferent. Somewhere behind them a screen door banged twice and settled. She thought about the manuscript sitting unsold in her bag, about the landlord's patience running thinner than the tide, about how none of that had anything to do with this, and how she'd let it anyway.

"There's a bench at the end of this path," she said. "It's ugly and the paint is peeling and from it you can see the whole harbor."

"Is that an invitation?"

"It's a fact. What you do with it is yours."

He fell into step beside her without needing to be asked twice, and the gulls, unbothered, went on arguing over something only they could see.
