The frost came early to Hallowmere that year, and it was in the cold hush before the first snowfall that Wren Ashcombe found the door beneath the mill.

She was seventeen, apprenticed to old Master Corrigan, who taught her the safe magics—candle-flame charms, mending spells, the small weather-calls that kept the barley dry through harvest. Hallowmere was three days' ride from the nearest city, tucked into a fold of the Greywater valley where nothing much happened and nobody much minded. That suited Wren fine, mostly. The village had a comfortable smallness to it, like a coat worn soft.

The mill had been abandoned since before her grandmother's time, its wheel long rotted from the millrace, and she went there sometimes to practice away from Corrigan's watchful correcting. It was during one such evening, chasing a firefly-light spell across the dusty floor, that the light dipped through a crack between floorboards and did not come back up.

She pried the boards loose with a rusted hinge-pin. Below was a stair, and below the stair was a door marked with a sigil she almost recognized—a spiral inside a broken circle, half-worn from the wood. She knew enough of her letters in the old tongue to make out a single carved word beneath it: BENEATH.

Master Corrigan had told her once, in the offhand way he mentioned things he didn't want asked about twice, that there existed a kind of magic called undermagic—power drawn not from the sun-warmed threads mages usually wove, but from what lay beneath things: beneath sleep, beneath silence, beneath the visible world. It had been forbidden for three generations, since a mage in the capital had used it to peel a man's shadow from his body and set it walking on its own errands.

Wren knew she should climb back up, nail the boards shut, tell Corrigan everything.

She opened the door instead.

The room beyond was small and dry, lined with shelves of jarred things she didn't examine too closely, and at its center stood a stone basin filled with what looked like still black water, though no water in Hallowmere had ever been so quiet. When she leaned over it, her own reflection did not lean with her. It stayed upright, watching, a beat behind her every movement, like it was deciding whether to bother pretending.

"You're not supposed to be down here," it said. Her own voice, but underneath it, something older.

She should have run. Wren had grown up on the stories: undermagic asks a question, and whatever you answer becomes the price. But her hand was already in the water before she'd decided to put it there, and where her fingers touched, she felt the whole shape of the valley for a moment—every root, every buried stone, every sleeping badger and old bone, as if the ground itself had opened an eye and let her look through it.

It was the most extraordinary thing she had ever felt, and it terrified her more than anything Corrigan had ever warned her against.

She pulled her hand back. The water stilled. Her reflection caught up to her at last, and smiled with her own mouth in a way she hadn't asked it to.

Wren did not tell Corrigan that night, nor the next. She told herself she was deciding what to do, and told herself that for three more evenings, each of which ended with her climbing back down the mill stairs to sit before the basin and not quite touch it. On the fourth evening she found she had already lowered her hand before she'd finished deciding not to.

She did not think of it as a fall. It felt, if anything, like finally standing up straight after years of stooping to fit through a doorway sized for someone else. But she noticed, too, that she'd begun rehearsing what she'd say if anyone asked where she'd been—and that the lie came easier each time, smooth as water finding the lowest ground, and just as patient about where it was going.
