The first time Marcus saw Eleanor, she was buying fish at Morrow's Market on a Tuesday morning. He was there for coffee beans, and they reached for the same jar on the top shelf. Their hands touched. She laughed and pulled back first.

"All yours," she said, which seemed to apply to more than just the coffee.

He saw her again three days later at the Beacon Point lighthouse, climbing the spiral stairs as he descended. They pressed themselves against opposite walls to pass, and the stairwell felt suddenly small. She smelled like salt water and something floral.

"Third time's the charm?" she said when he literally bumped into her at the pier on Saturday, dropping her bag of vegetables.

Marcus crouched to help her gather scattered tomatoes rolling across the weathered boards. "I'm Marcus. I feel like I should introduce myself officially, since we keep colliding."

"Eleanor." She was smiling now, genuinely, and it changed her entire face. "Are you stalking me, or am I stalking you?"

He laughed. "I genuinely don't know."

It turned out they both rented small houses on Inlet Road. Eleanor had moved to Harborview three months ago—a fresh start, she said vaguely. Marcus had been there nearly a year, working remotely for an architecture firm, drawn to the town's quiet rhythms. They walked together after that encounter, along the cliff path where wild grass bent in the wind and the ocean churned against black rocks below.

She told him she'd left a marriage that felt like slowly forgetting who she was. He told her he'd burned out so thoroughly that even ambition felt foreign. They walked mostly in comfortable silence, speaking in fragments about nothing important and everything that mattered.

Over the following weeks, the coincidences seemed less coincidental. Eleanor would mention she was going to the library, and Marcus would show up. He'd text that he was trying the new restaurant on Crescent Street, and she'd appear with an excuse about being hungry. Eventually, the pretense dissolved.

They started taking coffee together at the small table outside Morrow's Market, watching the fishing boats and tourists pass. Eleanor sketched in a worn notebook—idle drawings of doors, windows, architectural details. Marcus realized she'd illustrated the entire town without meaning to. The patterns of their lives in Harborview appeared in her pages: the corner store's awning, the lighthouse beam, the weathered pier.

"Why architecture details?" he asked one morning.

"Because I'm interested in doorways," she said. "Entry points. Thresholds between what was and what comes next."

He understood she wasn't really talking about buildings.

One evening, standing at the point where the rocky beach met the cliff path, Eleanor turned to him. "I used to believe in signs," she said. "Destiny, fate, all that. I thought if I was meant to meet someone, the universe would handle it." She smiled at him. "But I don't think that's how it works. I think you have to keep showing up. In the same place. Over and over. Until eventually, someone shows up too."

Marcus took her hand. It was easier than speaking.

They stayed there until the sun dissolved into the ocean, painting the sky in shades of amber and violet. The lighthouse beam cut through the gathering dark. Tomorrow, Marcus would probably see Eleanor somewhere unexpected—at the market, the pier, the library. But tonight, he knew exactly where she was, and that was enough. That was everything.
