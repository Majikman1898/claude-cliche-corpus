The Meridian's sensors had been quiet for three weeks when the anomaly appeared.

Captain Elena Vasquez was halfway through her morning coffee when Kace, their navigator, let out a low whistle from the observation deck. She made her way forward, already knowing that whistle meant something unusual.

"There," Kace said, pointing at the viewscreen.

At first, Vasquez saw nothing but the familiar ribbon of the Cutter Nebula, that sprawl of gas and dust they'd been mapping for the better part of a month. Then she saw it—a section of space where the stars simply weren't. Not blocked by dust or obscured by gravitational lensing, but absent. A perfect circle of nothing, maybe half a kilometer in diameter.

"That wasn't there yesterday," Kace said. He was the type who noticed things. "I ran the same star charts three times this morning."

Vasquez pulled up the sensor logs. Nothing. No electromagnetic signature, no radiation spike, no gravitational anomaly. The Meridian's instruments had detected absolutely nothing unusual in the past eighteen hours, yet something was actively erasing the starfield behind it.

"Get Dr. Okafor," Vasquez ordered.

Their physicist arrived within minutes, his silver beard disheveled from sleep. He stared at the screen in silence for a long moment.

"Have you tried hailing it?" he finally asked.

"With what?" Kace replied. "We don't know what we're looking at."

"Exactly," Okafor said. "Which means we should try everything."

They broadcast on all frequencies—standard greeting protocols, mathematical sequences, music from Old Earth. Nothing came back. The void remained perfectly, impossibly silent.

Vasquez ordered the ship to a halt at twenty thousand kilometers. Close enough to study the phenomenon, far enough to run if it turned hostile. Not that she had much faith in running. Whatever could erase stars from the universe without warning could probably catch them.

Over the next six hours, they learned almost nothing. The anomaly maintained its perfect circular shape and didn't move. It didn't seem to be rotating or generating any energy signature they could detect. It simply existed, a hole in reality that their instruments couldn't quite process.

"It's like looking at something you can't see," Okafor muttered, running his hands through his hair. "The absence itself is what we're detecting."

That was when the Meridian's lights flickered.

Vasquez felt the familiar knot of dread tighten in her stomach. She was across the bridge to the engineering station in three strides. The power readings were stable, all systems nominal. Another flicker. Then another.

"It's coming closer," Kace breathed.

He was right. The void had drifted—or been pulled, or had fallen through some logic Vasquez couldn't grasp—to within five thousand kilometers. Her crew looked at her with the same question in their eyes. Jump? Flee? Fight something they couldn't even name?

Vasquez made a decision that would spend the rest of her career being debated in academy classrooms. She stood up and walked to the viewscreen, looking at that terrible absence of stars, and did something their protocols said never to do.

She reached out her hand and pressed her palm against the cool metal of the screen, and she whispered a simple greeting into the dark: "We mean you no harm."

The lights stopped flickering.

The void hung motionless for another heartbeat, then began to withdraw. The stars reappeared slowly, like a photograph developing in reverse. Within moments, the anomaly was gone, and Vasquez's hand was still pressed against the screen.

Kace released the breath he'd been holding. "Did we just communicate with something?"

Vasquez didn't answer. She was still staring at the space where the nothing had been, wondering if perhaps something very old had simply been saying hello the only way it knew how.
