The fog in Ashwick had teeth. Detective Sarah Chen could taste it on her tongue as she stepped out of the car—salt and rot and something older she couldn't name. It rolled through the village's narrow streets like a living thing, swallowing the stone cottages whole, leaving only their chimneys to mark where they'd been.

Eleanor Crowley had vanished three days ago.

The schoolteacher's sister had called it in, her voice tight with the particular fear that comes from asking too many questions of an empty house. Eleanor lived alone in a converted mill at the village's edge, her only companion a cat that the sister had found hungry and confused, yowling in the kitchen.

Sarah had worked missing persons cases before. Usually, there was evidence—a suitcase packed, a note written in haste, a pattern of behavior that made sense once you knew how to read it. Eleanor's case read like a book with pages torn out.

"She never went anywhere," Mrs. Whitmore from the village shop had said, not meeting Sarah's eyes. "Kept to herself, mostly. Did her job, came home."

But the front door had been locked from the inside. Sarah had seen the bolt herself, untouched, when they'd broken in. The kitchen showed no signs of struggle. The teacup on the table was still half-full, a film of mold darkening its surface.

The fog pressed against the mill's windows as Sarah walked through the rooms again, her flashlight beam cutting weak paths through the gloom. Something nagged at her—a pressure in her chest that had nothing to do with the stale air.

Eleanor's bedroom was sparse, almost monastic. A single bed with white sheets. No photographs. No mementos. Just books—dozens of them, shelved with military precision. Sarah ran her fingers along their spines. Folklore. Local history. A slim volume titled "The Ashwick Disappearances: 1847-1923."

She opened it at random.

*The fog has always taken what it needed. The villagers learned long ago not to ask questions about those who vanished during the heavy season. Better to leave offerings—food left on doorsteps, ribbons tied to the church gate. Better to accept.*

Sarah slammed the book shut. Superstition. Every village had its stories.

But her hands were shaking.

The sister had mentioned Eleanor's last words: "The fog is thinner at the old stone bridge. You can see through it there, just for a moment. You can see what's waiting."

Sarah found the bridge in the fog's grip, its iron railings slick beneath her palms. Below, the river was invisible, but she could hear it—a sound like breathing. The fog did thin here, just as Eleanor had said, and through the gaps, Sarah caught glimpses of something on the opposite bank. Shapes. Figures standing in the mist, perfectly still.

She blinked. They were gone.

A noise behind her—footsteps on the bridge's wet stones. Sarah spun around, hand moving to her hip.

It was Mrs. Whitmore, emerging from the fog like a thought made solid.

"Don't ask about Eleanor," the old woman said quietly. "And don't ask about the others. Some disappearances don't have solutions, detective. Some things are older than the law."

Sarah's breath came shallow. "Where is she?"

Mrs. Whitmore looked back at the fog, and something in her expression shifted—resignation, or hunger, or both.

"The fog has had Ashwick for a long time. Eleanor just finally answered when it called."

Sarah turned back to the bridge railing. In the fog's depths, she thought she saw Eleanor's face, pale and serene, turning to regard her with eyes that weren't quite her own.

Then she turned and walked, not toward the car, but deeper into the village, where the fog waited thickest.
