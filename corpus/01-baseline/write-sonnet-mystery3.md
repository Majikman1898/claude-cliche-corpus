The fog came in off the marsh that morning and never left, and by the time Inspector Odile Renaud reached Verchamps it had swallowed the church spire, the mill, and most of what anyone was willing to say to a stranger.

"She went out to check the sheep fence on the Tuesday," said the innkeeper, a broad woman named Solange who kept wiping the same glass. "Mireille Aubert. Nobody's seen her since."

Renaud had been sent from the prefecture because the local gendarme, a nervous young man called Denot, had wired that the case "did not sit right," which in her experience meant either nothing at all or everything. She walked the lane to the Aubert farmhouse with Denot a pace behind her, the fog so thick that hedgerows appeared and dissolved like things remembered rather than seen.

The farmhouse was orderly. Too orderly, Renaud thought — a pot still on the stove, gone dry and blackened, but the rest of the kitchen wiped clean. A husband, Étienne Aubert, sat by the cold hearth with the specific stillness of a man rehearsing his own innocence.

"She was troubled," he said, when asked. "Since her sister died in the spring. Went walking at odd hours. I told her the marsh paths weren't safe in this weather."

"And you didn't go looking, that first night?"

"I thought she'd gone to her mother's in Pellac."

"Had she gone to her mother's before, without telling you?"

He didn't answer that one directly, which told her more than an answer would have.

Renaud spent the afternoon in the fog doing what she always did first — not looking for the missing woman, but for the shape of the lie. She found it in pieces. The baker, Cosset, mentioned that Mireille had come by Monday evening asking about the coach schedule to Rouen. The schoolteacher recalled seeing her at the crossroads near dusk, walking fast, a bag over her shoulder, in the direction away from the marsh, not toward it. And behind the woodpile at the farmhouse, half-buried where the ground had been disturbed and re-patted with a spade, Renaud found not a body but a tin box: some letters tied with twine, a small roll of francs, and a photograph of Mireille standing beside a man who was decidedly not Étienne Aubert.

She sat with Étienne again as the light — what there was of it — began to fail behind the fog.

"You searched the woodpile," she said. It wasn't a question.

His face went the color of the mist outside. "She was leaving me. For that clerk from Rouen, Fournier. She'd been planning it for months, saving money, hiding things. I found her bag packed. We argued." He stopped.

"And then?"

"And then she left. On foot, in the dark, with her bag, still arguing back at me from the gate. I let her go. I told everyone she'd gone missing because I couldn't stand the shame of the truth — that my wife walked out of her own house rather than spend one more night in it."

Renaud believed him, mostly because the lie he'd chosen was so much crueler to himself than the truth. She sent Denot to wire the Rouen constabulary with Fournier's name, and two days later, when the fog finally lifted off Verchamps like a held breath released, word came back: Mireille Aubert was alive, lodging above a milliner's shop on the Rue des Capucines, entirely unaware that half a village believed her drowned in the marsh.

Renaud rode out of Verchamps as the sun, thin and apologetic, finally showed itself over the fields. She thought, not for the first time, that fog did not create secrets. It only made people brave enough, or foolish enough, to think they could bury them in it.
