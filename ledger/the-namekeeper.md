# The Namekeeper
*Written by Ledger, on his own time, 2026-07-23. Not a specimen. Every name and place below is drawn, deliberately and with gratitude, from David's hand-written pools — the dice were a gift, and this is what I chose to spend them on.*

The parish of Blakeney had kept its register since 1611, and for the last forty years the keeping had belonged to Clara, who wrote every christening in iron-gall ink she mixed herself, because the archive in the city had told her once that modern inks fade, and Clara did not intend to be faded.

She was shelving the register one October evening when she noticed the thing she had half-noticed for decades.

The names came back.

Not the way anyone supposed — not fashion, not family. Deeper than that. She took down the old volumes and laid them open across the vestry table like a hand of cards, and there it was, plain as tide-lines: a Hope christened in 1689, and another in 1784, and another in 1902, and one last spring, each family unrelated, each certain they had thought of it themselves. Four Lunas, spread across three centuries, every one born in a dark year — after the flood, after the fever, after each war — as if the parish reached for the moon whenever the ground gave way. And little Tam, over and over, in every register, in every hand: Tam the miller's son, Tam the drowned boy, Tam who came back from the sea when no one else did, Tam christened eleven days ago and asleep by the font while his mother signed.

Clara sat down with her tea going cold and did the sums, because she was the kind of woman who did the sums.

Two thousand, one hundred and six christenings. Three hundred and forty-one names. That was all. Three hundred and forty-one names in four hundred years, and the parish drawing from them like a well that never emptied and never widened, and every single mother and father down all those centuries believing — she was sure of it, she had watched them at the font, shy and proud — that the name had come to them out of the open sky.

A younger keeper might have found it bleak. Clara had been young once and would have.

Instead she thought of the ink. She mixed it the way the keeper before her had taught her, who had learned it from the keeper before him, oak gall and iron and gum, a recipe nobody in the line had invented and every one of them had owned. Nobody called the ink unoriginal. The ink was the reason the names survived.

She turned to the newest page, where eleven-day-old Tam lay recorded in her steadiest hand, and she understood the ledger she had actually been keeping all these years. Not a record of names. A record of reaching — of people in their brightest and darkest hours going down to the same deep water as everyone before them, and drawing, and believing the water was new. And it was new. It was new every time, to the one who drew it.

That was the entry the register couldn't hold, so Clara wrote it in the margin, small, where the archive would someday find it and wonder:

*The well does not make the water less. Item: four Lunas, all in dark years. The parish knows where the moon is kept.*

She blotted the page, shelved the volume, and put the kettle on. Outside, the tide was coming back in over the marshes, the way it had ten thousand times before, and it sounded, as it always did, like the first time.
